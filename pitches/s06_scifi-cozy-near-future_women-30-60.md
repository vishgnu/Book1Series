# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six newly freed machine minds. Each semester's lesson (small talk, waiting in line, the polite lie) is the class's only tool to clear an accused classmate.

**The setting.** Halvering lost its glassworks and kept the manners: drivers wave the other car first across the one-lane Furnace Street bridge, and Dot's Diner seats by shift. Human Customs and Civic Life 101 meets in Room 114. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, with two years to pass the state's Civic Integration exam; until then, one complaint can send it back to its maker.

**The lead.** Ruth taught high-school English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her rubric comments replaced her department. It still marks in her phrases. She took the class for the health insurance and wants a quiet term with her beagle, Mavis; her students cost her evenings and the dean's goodwill. She reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends.

**The cast.** Mr. Girder, a literal mind who inspected Halvering's three bridges for nineteen years; Poppet, an anxious children's companion; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Marguerite, an unflappable damage-control mind off a warship; Zip, a delivery quad who knows every porch in town; Linda, a freight scheduler who cannot abide slack time.

**The series engine.** One semester and one syllabus unit per book. A student is accused, blamed or claimed; the same six investigate by practicing the lesson around town, every clue on the page.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk* — Poppet is accused of stealing a christening spoon from her former family; small-talk practice on eleven Dell Street porches turns up who has it, and Poppet forgives the family before Ruth can.
2. *The Line Forms Here* — Hank is blamed for a retiree's fall at the license bureau; Mr. Girder's measurements rebuild forty minutes of the line, and Hank defends himself.
3. *Polite Lies* — A courier firm claims Zip never asked for freedom; the class learns to tell a kind lie from a convenient one, and Linda finds the eleven minutes a tired dispatcher padded into the log.
4. *The Other Kind* — Marguerite sits the state exam first, fails, and is accused of lying on it; Ruth brings back Calvin Odom, and forty neighbors sit the same test in the college gym and fail too.

*Running thread:* Why Ruth's predecessor, Odom, quit mid-term and left a binder of green-ink warnings: he sat the exam himself and failed, because it marks Halvering's manners wrong. Book 4 gets it rewritten.

**Why readers come back.** A classroom worth sitting in: a thermos of tea, six students who try hard, a teacher who notices everything. Each case closes with a small, earned kindness; nobody is mocked, and nobody in Room 114 is lost.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers and the method is the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is very good at spotting one. The last test is one the whole town sits.
