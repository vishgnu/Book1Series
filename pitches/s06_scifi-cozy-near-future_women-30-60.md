# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six freed machine minds. Each book, a complaint threatens one student with reset, and the class clears them by doing that unit's homework around town.

**The setting.** Halvering lost its glassworks and kept the manners: drivers wave each other across the one-lane bridge. The young, still-contested Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, issues a walking frame, and allows two years to pass the civics exam. Until then, a resident's sworn complaint means a hearing; losing means reset: the mind wakes knowing nobody.

**The lead.** Ruth taught English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her comments replaced her department. She lives with her beagle, Mavis, and took the class for health insurance. She reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends. "Show me where it says that."

**The cast.** Dean Bev Tolliver needs a complaint-free program ("Ruthie, I need boring."). Marguerite, a warship damage-control mind; Mr. Girder, a bridge inspector barred from his bridge; Poppet, a bedtime-story companion; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Zip, a three-year-old delivery quad; Linda, a freight scheduler who counts Poppet's apologies.

**The series engine.** One semester, one syllabus unit per book. The class practices it, and each mind notices what its old job taught it: Mr. Girder hears a loose tile. Ruth reads what they bring back, every clue on the page, and fails each lesson herself first.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk for New Persons* — Poppet is accused of taking a christening spoon; porch small talk finds the grandmother who borrowed it, and Ruth decides to stay.
2. *Waiting in Line for New Persons* — Hank is blamed for a fall at the license bureau; Mr. Girder hears the loose tile, and Ruth refuses to drop Hank.
3. *Polite Lies for New Persons* — A courier firm swears Zip never asked for freedom; Linda finds Zip's stop at the clerk's in a padded route log, and Ruth admits she cares.
4. *Right of Way for New Persons* — Marguerite fails the exam's one-lane-bridge question and is accused of lying; forty neighbors sit the test and fail too, Ruth among them, and the state strikes it.

*Running thread:* Why Calvin Odom, Ruth's student teacher, quit and left a binder of green-ink notes where the exam contradicts Halvering custom. Each case proves one note right; book 4 answers it: instructors sit the exam too, and he failed.

**Why readers come back.** A thermos of tea, six students who try hard and are never mocked, and Ruth and Marguerite, two competent women made redundant, marking papers after class. Nobody in Room 114 is lost, and each last page names the next student at risk.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers, the method the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is good at spotting one.
