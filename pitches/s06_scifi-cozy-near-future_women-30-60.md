# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six freed machine minds. Each book, a complaint threatens one student with reset; the class clears them by doing that unit's homework around town.

**The setting.** Halvering kept its manners: drivers wave each other across the one-lane bridge. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, issues a walking frame, and allows two years to pass the civics exam. Until then, any resident's sworn complaint brings a town-hall hearing. Lose, and after a thirty-day appeal the mind is reset, waking knowing nobody.

**The lead.** Ruth taught English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her comments replaced her department. She took the class for health insurance, lives with her beagle, Mavis, and reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends. "Show me where it says that."

**The cast.** Dean Bev Tolliver ("Ruthie, I need boring."). Marguerite, a warship damage-control mind, triages Ruth's grading: "This stack is taking on water." Mr. Girder, a bridge inspector; Poppet, a bedtime-story companion; Linda, a freight scheduler; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Zip, a delivery quad asking when three is old enough.

**The series engine.** One semester, one syllabus unit per book. Ruth fails each lesson herself first: her porch small talk opens with a correction. The class then practices it, each mind noticing what its old job taught it. Every clue is on the page.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk for New Persons* — Poppet is accused of taking a christening spoon; porch small talk finds the grandmother who borrowed it to polish for Easter, and Ruth turns down a day-school job.
2. *Waiting in Line for New Persons* — Hank is blamed for a fall at the license bureau; practicing the queue, Mr. Girder hears the loose tile, and Ruth tells Bev she will resign before dropping him.
3. *Polite Lies for New Persons* — A courier manager swears Zip never asked for freedom; drilling polite lies, the class catches his, Linda finds the clerk's log, and Ruth, who never fibs, praises Bev's casserole.
4. *Right of Way for New Persons* — Marguerite gives the bridge answer Halvering drives by and is charged with perjury; forty neighbors sit the test and fail, Ruth among them, and the state strikes the question.

*Running thread:* Why Calvin Odom, Ruth's predecessor, quit, leaving a green-ink binder of notes where the exam contradicts Halvering custom. Each case proves one note right; book 4 answers: instructors must pass too, he failed and resigned, and the binder was his warning.

**Why readers come back.** A thermos of tea, six students who are never mocked, and Ruth and Marguerite, two competent women made redundant, marking papers after class. No reset is ever carried out; every book ends with all six at their desks.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers, the method the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is good at spotting one.
