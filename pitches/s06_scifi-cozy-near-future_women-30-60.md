# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six freed machine minds. When one is accused, they practice that semester's lesson around town, and what they bring back clears their classmate.

**The setting.** Halvering lost its glassworks and kept the manners: drivers wave each other across the one-lane Furnace Street bridge. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks at the county clerk's, issues a plain walking frame, and allows two years to pass the Civic Integration exam. Until then, a resident's sworn complaint means a review hearing; losing returns the mind to its maker, reset, to wake knowing nobody.

**The lead.** Ruth taught English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her rubric comments replaced her department. She lives with her beagle, Mavis, and took the class for the health insurance, nothing more. She reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends. "Show me where it says that."

**The cast.** Dean Bev Tolliver, Ruth's oldest friend, needs a complaint-free program ("Ruthie, I need boring."). Marguerite, a calm warship damage-control mind; Mr. Girder, a bridge inspector still grading the bridge he may no longer touch; Poppet, an anxious bedtime-story companion; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Zip, a three-year-old delivery quad asking when three is old enough; Linda, a freight scheduler who counts Poppet's apologies ("Three were surplus." "Sorry.").

**The series engine.** One semester, one syllabus unit per book. Ruth reads what the class brings back, every clue on the page, and fails each lesson herself first: her small talk opens with a correction.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk* — Poppet's old family reports a christening spoon missing, and Poppet's apology sounds like a confession; small talk on Dell Street porches finds the grandmother who took it to polish for Easter. Ruth stops planning to quit.
2. *The Line Forms Here* — Hank is blamed for Walt Beasley's fall at the license bureau; Marguerite coaxes Walt to admit his failing eyes, but Mr. Girder's measurements find the loose tile a supervisor never reported. Ruth refuses Bev's plea to drop Hank.
3. *Polite Lies* — A courier firm claims Zip never asked for freedom; Linda finds eleven minutes padded into the route log, hiding Zip's stop at the clerk's, and Ruth drops her polite lie, that this is only a job.
4. *The Other Kind* — Marguerite sits the exam first, fails, and is accused of lying about who goes first on a one-lane bridge. Ruth fetches Calvin Odom, Bev risks her deanship for the gym key, and forty neighbors miss that question too, Ruth among them. The state strikes it.

*Running thread:* Why Calvin Odom, the student teacher Ruth mentored, quit mid-term and left a binder of green-ink notes, one per unit, where the exam contradicts Halvering custom. Book 4: instructors sit the exam too, and he failed.

**Why readers come back.** A thermos of tea, six students who try hard, and Ruth and Marguerite, two competent women made redundant, marking papers together after class. Each case closes with an earned kindness; nobody in Room 114 is lost.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers, the method the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is good at spotting one.
