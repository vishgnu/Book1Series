# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six newly freed machine minds. Each semester one student lands in trouble, and the class solves it with that term's lesson.

**The setting.** Halvering is a glass town without its glassworks: brick row houses, three river bridges, Dot's Diner. Human Customs and Civic Life 101 meets in Room 114 at the community college. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks and gives it two years to earn a Civic Integration Certificate. Until then, one complaint can send a new person back to its maker.

**The lead.** Ruth Abernathy, 54, taught high-school English for twenty-six years until the layoffs. She took the class for the health insurance and does not like machines. She wants a quiet term; her students cost her evenings and the dean's goodwill. She reads people like essays and sounds dry, exact, kinder than she intends.

**The cast.** Mr. Girder, a painfully literal bridge-inspection mind in a second-hand caretaker body; Poppet, an anxious children's companion with nine thousand bedtime stories; Hank, a former car-wash gantry raised on forty thousand hours of courtroom television; Marguerite, damage-control mind off a decommissioned warship, calm about everything.

**The series engine.** Each book is one semester and one syllabus unit: small talk, waiting in line, polite lies, the other kind. A student is accused, blamed or claimed; the class investigates by practicing the lesson around town, with every clue on the page. Complaints keep coming, and each August brings a new class list.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk* — Poppet is accused of stealing a christening spoon from her former family; the class canvasses Dell Street, finds it, and Ruth decides to stay.
2. *The Line Forms Here* — Hank is blamed for a retiree's fall at the license bureau; the class rebuilds forty minutes of a waiting line, and Hank defends himself.
3. *Polite Lies* — A courier firm files to reclaim Zip, a know-it-all delivery quad aged three; Linda the freight scheduler finds the log proving Zip asked.
4. *The Other Kind* — Marguerite gets a recall notice from Ostrand Mechanical; Ruth finds Calvin Odom, and the class wins an open hearing with Ostrand's own falsified records.

*Running thread:* Why Ruth's predecessor, Calvin Odom, quit mid-term and left a binder of green-ink warnings; book 4 ends Ostrand's recall of new persons who remember too much.

**Why readers come back.** A classroom worth sitting in: a thermos of tea, six students who try hard, a teacher who notices everything. Each book closes its case with a small, earned kindness. Nobody is mocked, nothing is cruel, and the next semester is always enrolling.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers, and the method is the syllabus. A mind that cannot yet tell a white lie proves very good at spotting one; ordinary manners become the working tools.
