# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six freed machine minds. Each semester's lesson is the class's only tool to clear an accused classmate.

**The setting.** Halvering lost its glassworks and kept the manners: drivers wave each other across the one-lane Furnace Street bridge. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, issues a plain walking frame, and allows two years to pass the Civic Integration exam. Until then, any complaint means a review hearing; losing returns the mind to its maker, reset, to wake knowing nobody.

**The lead.** Ruth taught English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her rubric comments replaced her department. She lives with her beagle, Mavis, and took the class for health insurance, meaning to give machine minds nothing extra. She reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends. "Show me where it says that."

**The cast.** Dean Bev Tolliver, Ruth's oldest friend, hired her as a favor; her job needs a complaint-free program ("Ruthie, I need boring."). Marguerite, a calm warship damage-control mind; Mr. Girder, a bridge inspector still grading the bridge he may no longer touch; Poppet, an anxious bedtime-story companion; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Zip, a three-year-old delivery quad asking who decides when three is old enough; Linda, a freight scheduler who counts Poppet's apologies ("Three were surplus." "Sorry.").

**The series engine.** One semester, one syllabus unit per book, the same six students throughout. When one is accused, the class gathers evidence by practicing the lesson around town, and Ruth reads it, every clue on the page. She fails each lesson herself first.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk* — Poppet's old family reports a christening spoon missing; after small-talk practice on Dell Street porches, Ruth hears in the complaint the grandmother who mislaid it. Poppet forgives her first; Ruth stops planning to quit.
2. *The Line Forms Here* — Hank is blamed for Walt Beasley's fall at the license bureau; Mr. Girder's measurements rebuild the line, Marguerite coaxes Walt to admit his failing eyes, and Ruth refuses Bev's plea to drop Hank.
3. *Polite Lies* — A courier firm claims Zip never asked for freedom; Linda finds eleven minutes a dispatcher padded into the log, and Ruth drops her own polite lie, that this is only a job.
4. *The Other Kind* — Marguerite sits the exam first, fails, and is accused of lying; Ruth fetches Calvin Odom, Bev risks her deanship for the gym key, and forty neighbors sit the test and fail too, Ruth among them. The state rewrites it.

*Running thread:* Why Calvin Odom, once Ruth's student teacher, quit mid-term and left a binder of green-ink warnings, one per case. Book 4: he failed the exam himself, because it marks Halvering's manners wrong.

**Why readers come back.** A thermos of tea, six students who try hard, and Ruth and Marguerite, two competent women made redundant, marking papers together after class. Each case closes with an earned kindness; nobody is mocked, and nobody in Room 114 is lost.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers, the method the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is good at spotting one.
