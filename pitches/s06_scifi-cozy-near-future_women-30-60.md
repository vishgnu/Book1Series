# Night School for New Persons

**Genre:** Cozy science fiction (near future) · **Readers:** Women 30–60 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In 2050s Halvering, Ohio, laid-off English teacher Ruth Abernathy, 54, teaches night-school civics to six newly freed machine minds. Each semester's lesson (small talk, waiting in line, the polite lie) is the class's only tool to clear an accused classmate.

**The setting.** Halvering lost its glassworks and kept the manners: drivers wave the other car first across the one-lane Furnace Street bridge. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, with two years to pass the state's Civic Integration exam; until then, one complaint can send it back to its maker.

**The lead.** Ruth taught high-school English for twenty-six years, until grading software trained on her rubric comments replaced her department. She took the class for the health insurance and a quiet term with her beagle, Mavis; her students cost her evenings and strain her oldest friend, Dean Bev Tolliver. She reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends.

**The cast.** Mr. Girder, a literal-minded bridge inspector; Poppet, an anxious companion with nine thousand bedtime stories; Hank, a car-wash gantry raised on courtroom television; Marguerite, a warship damage-control mind, calm about everything; Zip, a delivery quad, aged three; Linda, a freight scheduler who counts Poppet's apologies ("Three were surplus." "Sorry.").

**The series engine.** One semester and one syllabus unit per book. When a student is accused, the six gather evidence by practicing the lesson around town, and Ruth reads it like essays, every clue on the page. Each August brings a new class list.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Small Talk* — Poppet's old family files a complaint over a missing christening spoon; after small-talk practice on Dell Street porches, Ruth hears in the letter the phrasing of the grandmother who mislaid it. Poppet forgives her first, and Ruth stops planning to quit.
2. *The Line Forms Here* — Hank is blamed for Walt Beasley's fall at the license bureau; Mr. Girder's measurements rebuild the line, queue practice at Dot's shows Walt hiding failing eyes, Marguerite coaxes him to say so, and Bev is put on notice.
3. *Polite Lies* — A courier firm claims Zip never asked for freedom; the class sorts kind lies from convenient ones, Linda finds the eleven minutes a tired dispatcher padded into the log, and the exam date is set.
4. *The Other Kind* — Marguerite sits the state exam first, fails, and is accused of lying on it; Ruth brings back Calvin Odom, Bev unlocks the college gym, and forty neighbors sit the same test and fail too. The state rewrites it.

*Running thread:* Why Calvin Odom quit mid-term and left a binder of green-ink warnings. One warning fits each case, and Odom is glimpsed at Dot's counter; book 4 answers it: he failed the exam himself, because it marks Halvering's manners wrong.

**Why readers come back.** A classroom worth sitting in: a thermos of tea, six students who try hard, a teacher who notices everything. Each case closes with a small, earned kindness; nobody is mocked, and nobody in Room 114 is lost.

**What makes it different.** The detectives are the newcomers and the method is the syllabus: a mind that cannot yet tell a white lie is very good at spotting one.
