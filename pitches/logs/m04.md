# m04: Keys Cut While You Wait

Revision log. Genre: Cozy fantasy mystery. Target readers: Women 25–45.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Locked from Inside. In the hill town of Pellory every door is charmed by the household that owns it, and the Lock Law is the oldest law there is: a room locked from within is private, and only a sworn locksmith may open it, before a witness. So when someone dies behind a locked door, the first person through it is Hester Wardlow, 33, who inherited her grandmother's shop on Latchkey Stairs along with its sign: Keys Cut While You Wait. Charmed locks follow rules a reader can learn: a lock can be bound to a hand, a word, an hour or a promise, never to two at once, and no charm can lock a door from the far side. Every case is a locked room that looks impossible until the bent rule is found. Hester has no magic beyond her trade: she has picks, wax, patience and a habit of talking to locks as if they were shy horses. Cast ideas: Old Bram, a retired burglar who sweeps her shop and knows how each lock would really be beaten; the constable sergeant who has to stand witness and hates stairs; the glazier next door; her grandmother's ledgers, which record every lock the shop ever made and who paid for it. Each book ends with a door opened and the town a little safer. Thread: the one lock in the shop cellar her grandmother never taught her to open, and made her promise to leave shut. Titles from the trade (Locked from Inside, Under Ward, The Spare Key, Double Throw).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Anela Deen, Night Latch (a locksmith who opens anything by touch; urban fantasy); Gigi Pandian, Under Lock & Skeleton Key (a cozy locked-room series about hidden rooms); Edna Curry's Lady Locksmith Mysteries (a locksmith sleuth without magic); Randall Garrett, Too Many Magicians (a rule-bound magical locked room, from memory). No cozy-fantasy locksmith or lock law was found. Avoid 'The Night Latch' and 'Skeleton Key' as book titles, and keep the lead without magic.

**First draft:** “Keys Cut While You Wait”, 512 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Hannah).** A charming locksmith shop and a clever lock-rule puzzle I would borrow, though the pitch tells me more about the locks than about the people I'd stay for. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 7, clarity 6.

- Keep: "a shop where a retired burglar makes the tea" and Bram himself, plus book 3's "learns why Odile hired a burglar"; "She talks to locks like shy horses and to suspects less gently" - the one line where I hear Hester's voice; Pellory's "nine hundred steps, slate roofs and a market on every landing" with a short list of lock rules I can actually solve from
- Problem 1: The logline is a mouthful. "the Lock Law lets only a sworn locksmith open a room locked from within" plus "first in when someone dies behind one" took me two reads. Why it matters: On a series page I skim the first line on my phone. If it makes me work, I scroll to the next cover. Suggested fix: Lead with the person and the shop: a locksmith with no magic, the only one the law allows to open a door someone died behind, and each time the lock was cheated.
- Problem 2: Warmth and banter are promised but not shown. Idris gets "brings soup unasked" and nothing else; nobody is funny on the page except the shy-horses line. No hint of romance either way. Why it matters: I come for the people and the bickering. Four dead bodies and a rule list read cooler and more puzzle-box than cozy. Suggested fix: Give one line of actual friction or banter between Hester and Bram or Dorcas, and say plainly whether Idris is a slow-burn love interest across the four books.
- Problem 3: The rules paragraph is dense and one rule confused me: "none can be locked from the far side" next to rooms "locked from within". I couldn't tell what that means. Why it matters: If I'm promised I can "spot the bent one first", I need to understand the rules on one read, half asleep. Suggested fix: State the rules as three plain sentences with one everyday example, e.g. a pantry that only opens at breakfast, and cut or reword the "far side" rule.
- Problem 4: Hester's want is vague: "the trust Pellory gave Odile". And book 1 already ends with Pellory no longer calling the shop Odile's, so her goal seems finished early. Why it matters: I need something personal pulling me to book 2 besides another body; the cellar ledger is intriguing but belongs to Odile, not Hester. Suggested fix: Give Hester a concrete personal stake that lasts four books: a doubt about what her grandmother really did, tied to the promise she made about the cellar, hinted from book 1.
- Reminded them of: Legends & Lattes; The Thursday Murder Club; Jonathan Creek; The Spellshop; Murder, She Wrote; Discworld's Ankh-Morpork guilds

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** The title and the retired burglar making tea got me, but the logline is a rule-heavy mouthful that nearly lost me before the charm showed up. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 6, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 8, fit 7, clarity 6.

- Keep: Title and shop sign 'Keys Cut While You Wait' plus 'a retired burglar makes the tea' - that is what I caught and what I would remember tomorrow; 'She talks to locks like shy horses and to suspects less gently' - the only line where I hear Hester, and it sold her; The engine: 'nobody else may open that door' and 'each solution is one rule, bent' - a fair armchair puzzle with a real reason cases keep coming
- Problem 1: The logline is one long sentence stacked with law and clauses: 'the Lock Law lets only a sworn locksmith open a room locked from within'. I would have swiped at 'locked from within'. Why it matters: In ten seconds I got a legal rule, not a person or a feeling. I did not yet know it was warm or funny. Suggested fix: Lead with Hester and the shop: a locksmith with no magic in a town of charmed doors, the only one the law lets open a locked room when someone dies behind it. Move the Lock Law name down.
- Problem 2: Four books, four deaths behind doors, and 'the one who opens a neighbor's worst morning'. The warmth (soup, tea, table) is tucked into later paragraphs and the top reads like straight murder mystery. Why it matters: I pick cozy for comfort. If the first lines say death and law, I assume it is colder than it is and move on. Suggested fix: Put one cozy image in the first two lines (the burglar making tea, soup unasked, the market on every landing) and say outright that the deaths happen offstage and gently.
- Problem 3: The lock rules paragraph is hard to parse on a phone: 'bound to a hand, a word, an hour or a promise, never two at once, and none can be locked from the far side'. I do not know what 'far side' means. Why it matters: The puzzle is the selling point, and if I cannot follow the rules at a glance I do not trust I can play along. Suggested fix: State three rules as plainly as possible with one tiny example, e.g. 'a door bound to an hour opens for anyone at that hour'. Cut or rephrase 'locked from the far side'.
- Problem 4: Hester's personal stakes and relationships are thin. Idris 'brings soup unasked' hints at romance but is never named as such, and the running thread ends on 'its last pages blank', which I did not understand. Why it matters: I binge for the people. I stopped not knowing who Hester loves, what she fears finding about Odile, or why blank pages should worry me. Suggested fix: Say plainly whether Idris is a slow-burn romance, and state the thread as a question with feeling: did Odile open doors she should not have, and is Hester expected to fill the blank pages?
- Reminded them of: Legends & Lattes (shop, found family); The Spellshop; Jonathan Creek (impossible locked rooms); Death in Paradise (fair-play puzzle, case per episode); The Thursday Murder Club (elderly ex-rogue sidekick); The Undertaking of Hart and Mercy (trade-based cozy world)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline rebuilt as three short sentences that lead with Hester, the shop and the burglar making tea; the Lock Law name moved down to the setting (both critics: rule-heavy mouthful, no warmth in the first lines).
- Changed: Lock rules restated as three plain sentences with the breakfast-pantry example; the confusing 'far side' rule and 'never two at once' are cut (both critics: rules hard to parse).
- Changed: Hester's want is now a lasting doubt about whether Odile earned the town's trust, tied to her promise about the cellar lock; the running thread is restated as that question, with the blank pages explained as left for Hester (cold reader: vague want finished in book 1; browser: blank pages unclear).
- Changed: Cast gains friction and a plain romance statement: Bram grades her lockwork and has never given an eight, Dorcas complains on every stair, Idris is named as a four-book slow burn (both critics: warmth and banter promised but not shown).
- Changed: Engine now says deaths stay offstage; to pay for the additions, the ledgers line in the cast, 'the town a little safer' and the 'neighbor's worst morning' clause are cut (browser: top reads colder than it is).
- Declined: Changing book 1's ending (Pellory stops calling the shop Odile's): with her want now the doubt about Odile, this is an early win rather than the goal completed.
- Declined: Reducing the four deaths to soften the tone: the locked-room death is the engine both critics praised, so the fix is offstage deaths and warmth up front.
- For the author to decide: Idris is now committed as a slow-burn romance across all four books, resolved in book 4. Confirm, or say if you would rather keep him a friend and the series romance-free.
- For the author to decide: I cut the rule that a door cannot be locked from the far side because both readers could not parse it. If a planned solution depends on it, it needs to come back in plainer words.

**Gate (reader: Hannah, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A sounds like a book I'd curl up with: Bram grading her lockwork (never an eight), Dorcas complaining on every stair, soup that becomes a four-book romance, the breakfast-pantry example, tea promised. B reads like a rulebook and drops the banter and the romance. What the losing version did better: B's line that the work makes Hester "the one who opens a neighbor's worst morning" gives real feeling, and its lock limits (never two bindings at once) set up book 4's double charm more fairly.
