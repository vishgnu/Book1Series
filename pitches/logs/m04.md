# m04: Keys Cut While You Wait

Revision log. Genre: Cozy fantasy mystery. Target readers: Women 25–45.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Locked from Inside. In the hill town of Pellory every door is charmed by the household that owns it, and the Lock Law is the oldest law there is: a room locked from within is private, and only a sworn locksmith may open it, before a witness. So when someone dies behind a locked door, the first person through it is Hester Wardlow, 33, who inherited her grandmother's shop on Latchkey Stairs along with its sign: Keys Cut While You Wait. Charmed locks follow rules a reader can learn: a lock can be bound to a hand, a word, an hour or a promise, never to two at once, and no charm can lock a door from the far side. Every case is a locked room that looks impossible until the bent rule is found. Hester has no magic beyond her trade: she has picks, wax, patience and a habit of talking to locks as if they were shy horses. Cast ideas: Old Bram, a retired burglar who sweeps her shop and knows how each lock would really be beaten; the constable sergeant who has to stand witness and hates stairs; the glazier next door; her grandmother's ledgers, which record every lock the shop ever made and who paid for it. Each book ends with a door opened and the town a little safer. Thread: the one lock in the shop cellar her grandmother never taught her to open, and made her promise to leave shut. Titles from the trade (Locked from Inside, Under Ward, The Spare Key, Double Throw).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Anela Deen, Night Latch (a locksmith who opens anything by touch; urban fantasy); Gigi Pandian, Under Lock & Skeleton Key (a cozy locked-room series about hidden rooms); Edna Curry's Lady Locksmith Mysteries (a locksmith sleuth without magic); Randall Garrett, Too Many Magicians (a rule-bound magical locked room, from memory). No cozy-fantasy locksmith or lock law was found. Avoid 'The Night Latch' and 'Skeleton Key' as book titles, and keep the lead without magic.

**First draft:** “Keys Cut While You Wait”, 512 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Hannah).** A charming locksmith shop and a clever lock-rule puzzle I would borrow, though the pitch tells me more about the locks than about the people I'd stay for. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 7, clarity 6.

- Keep: "a shop where a retired burglar makes the tea" and Bram himself, plus book 3's "learns why Odile hired a burglar"; "She talks to locks like shy horses and to suspects less gently" - the one line where I hear Hester's voice; Pellory's "nine hundred steps, slate roofs and a market on every landing" with a short list of lock rules I can actually solve from
- Problem 1: The logline is a mouthful. "the Lock Law lets only a sworn locksmith open a room locked from within" plus "first in when someone dies behind one" took me two reads. Why it matters: On a series page I skim the first line on my phone. If it makes me work, I scroll to the next cover. Suggested fix: Lead with the person and the shop: a locksmith with no magic, the only one the law allows to open a door someone died behind, and each time the lock was cheated.
- Problem 2: Warmth and banter are promised but not shown. Idris gets "brings soup unasked" and nothing else; nobody is funny on the page except the shy-horses line. No hint of romance either way. Why it matters: I come for the people and the bickering. Four dead bodies and a rule list read cooler and more puzzle-box than cozy. Suggested fix: Give one line of actual friction or banter between Hester and Bram or Dorcas, and say plainly whether Idris is a slow-burn love interest across the four books.
- Problem 3: The rules paragraph is dense and one rule confused me: "none can be locked from the far side" next to rooms "locked from within". I couldn't tell what that means. Why it matters: If I'm promised I can "spot the bent one first", I need to understand the rules on one read, half asleep. Suggested fix: State the rules as three plain sentences with one everyday example, e.g. a pantry that only opens at breakfast, and cut or reword the "far side" rule.
- Problem 4: Hester's want is vague: "the trust Pellory gave Odile". And book 1 already ends with Pellory no longer calling the shop Odile's, so her goal seems finished early. Why it matters: I need something personal pulling me to book 2 besides another body; the cellar ledger is intriguing but belongs to Odile, not Hester. Suggested fix: Give Hester a concrete personal stake that lasts four books: a doubt about what her grandmother really did, tied to the promise she made about the cellar, hinted from book 1.
- Reminded them of: Legends & Lattes; The Thursday Murder Club; Jonathan Creek; The Spellshop; Murder, She Wrote; Discworld's Ankh-Morpork guilds

Why it matters: In ten seconds I got a legal rule, not a person or a feeling. I did not yet know it was warm or funny. I stopped not knowing who Hester loves, what she fears finding about Odile, or why blank pages should worry me. ### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** Familiar cozy furniture around a genuinely fresh core: condition-bound locks and a lock law that makes a non-magical locksmith the only lawful way into a locked room. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: The charm rule: "A charm knows its condition was met, never who met it" plus the four bindings (hand, word, hour, promise), which makes every solution "one rule, bent"; The Lock Law as engine: "Hester is the one person the law lets through the door, so she never has to snoop"; The cellar lock "bound to Hester's own promise" and the ledger "of every door opened without a witness, its last pages left blank for Hester"
- Problem 1: Most generic element: "inherited her grandmother Odile's shop" with a secret about the dead relative. This is the default opening of the cozy shelf, magical or not. Why it matters: A binge reader has met the inherited shop plus family secret dozens of times; it makes the logline's frame sound interchangeable before the lock rules arrive. Suggested fix: Keep Odile, change the inheritance: Hester inherits Odile's sworn locksmith's oath and its unwitnessed-door ledger debt, not just premises. She stays because the oath binds the shop, which ties the trope to the Lock Law.
- Problem 2: The most distinctive element, the promise-bound lock, appears only in the running thread. Books 1 to 3 use hand, word and hour; no case turns on a promise. Why it matters: Hand, word and hour locks resemble biometric, password and time locks. A lock bound to a promise is the one binding with no real-world or genre equivalent (from memory). Suggested fix: Put a promise-bound lock in the logline and make book 4's "two charms" explicitly an hour and a promise, so the final case mirrors the cellar lock and Hester must break her own to explain the magistrate's.
- Problem 3: The cast is stock by role: "retired burglar" mentor who "makes the tea", grumbling sergeant, and the neighbor who "brings soup unasked" as slow-burn romance. Why it matters: The reformed-thief sidekick and soup-bringing craftsman love interest are shelf staples (from memory); nothing ties Idris or Quill to locks, the pitch's own territory. Suggested fix: Give each a stake in the lock rules: Idris glazes windows that by custom can never be charmed, the standing loophole in every case; Quill must witness each opening, so her stairs complaint is plot, not garnish.
- Problem 4: The rule-bound magical locked room solved by a licensed investigator sits close to Garrett's Lord Darcy (from memory), and the "what did grandmother do" thread ends on a ledger, a familiar reveal object. Why it matters: The resemblance is trope-level, not premise-level, but the pitch never states what in the ledger would surprise a reader who has guessed "she opened doors illegally". Suggested fix: State the ledger's twist in one line: each unwitnessed opening was made at the household's request to hide a kindness, not a crime, and the blank pages ask Hester whether she will keep that practice.
- Reminded them of: Randall Garrett, Too Many Magicians / Lord Darcy (rule-bound magical locked room; from memory); Anela Deen, Night Latch (locksmith lead, but magical and urban fantasy; per audit); Gigi Pandian, Under Lock & Skeleton Key (cozy locked-room series, family craft business; per audit); Edna Curry, Lady Locksmith Mysteries (non-magical locksmith sleuth; per audit); Travis Baldree, Legends & Lattes (shop plus found-family table plus slow-burn neighbor; from memory); John Dickson Carr, the Gideon Fell locked rooms (impossible-crime engine; from memory)
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances marked "per audit" come from the 15 November 2026 audit; all others are from memory and unverified. The individual book titles (Locked from Inside, Under Ward, The Spare Key, Double Throw) and the series title were not checked; "The Spare Key" and "Locked from Inside" are common phrases and likely to collide with existing titles.

**superfan-reader (Priya).** A real rules-based magic system and a clean locked-room engine, wrapped in a cast I have met a dozen times; I would borrow it for the locks. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 7, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: The lock rule: "A charm knows its condition was met, never who met it" plus "each solution is one rule, bent". This is the one thing I have not met, and it plays fair like a good TTRPG system.; Bram, who "grades Hester's lockwork out of ten; she has never had an eight", and the Lock Law that means "she never has to snoop".; The cellar lock "bound to Hester's own promise" and the ledger with "its last pages left blank for Hester": the running thread uses the magic system instead of sitting beside it.
- Problem 1: Idris Fenn is pure stock: the kind craftsman next door who "brings soup unasked" in a four-book slow burn. He has no want, no flaw and no link to locks or the cases. Why it matters: I have read this man in every cozy on the shelf. He is the line that sounds machine-made, and the romance is a quarter of why I binge. Suggested fix: Replace him first. Make the love interest someone whose trade rubs against hers: the charm-binder who sets the locks Hester picks, with a professional grudge about locksmiths, or give Idris a door he will not let her open.
- Problem 2: Hester herself is thinner than her shop. Inherited-grandmother's-business heroine with a family secret is the most common opening on this shelf; "talks to locks like shy horses" is her only personal detail. Why it matters: I follow a series for the person, not the puzzle. Right now Bram is more vivid in one clause than Hester is in a paragraph. Suggested fix: Give her one specific non-inherited thing: what she did before the shop, a bad habit, a wrong belief about people that the cases keep correcting. Say why having no magic costs her something in Pellory.
- Problem 3: Four murders in four books, three victims being a guardian, an innkeeper and a magistrate, in a town small enough to count its steps. The sergeant who grumbles about stairs is a one-joke stock foil. Why it matters: "Low stakes with real feelings" strains when neighbors keep dying; and the body count per capita starts to feel like Midsomer, not cozy. Suggested fix: Make one case, probably book 2, a non-death locked room: a theft, a vanished person, a will sealed in a promise-bound box. Give Dorcas a stake in the Lock Law beyond witnessing.
- Problem 4: The rules have an untested edge: who casts the charms, can a binding be changed, and what does a "promise" lock actually check? Pellory's detail is pretty but familiar: slate roofs, markets, stairs. Why it matters: As a player I look for the loophole straight away. If any household can rebind a lock, every solution could be "someone rebound it". Suggested fix: Add one sentence of hard limit: a charm is set once and cannot be rebound, or rebinding leaves a visible mark. Swap one generic town detail for something only a town of charmed doors would have.
- Reminded them of: Legends & Lattes (found family around a shop counter); The Undertaking of Hart and Mercy (trade-based secondary world romance); Lord Darcy stories by Randall Garrett (fair-play locked rooms with rule-bound magic); John Dickson Carr locked-room mysteries; The Spellshop by Sarah Beth Durst; Blades in the Dark (retired burglar, lock clocks, steep haunted-trade town feel)

**Rewrite: accepted (slight).**

- Changed: Promise lock moved to the center (both critics): it is in the logline, defined in the setting (opens when the promise breaks, whoever broke it), and book 4's two charms are now an hour and a promise, so Hester must break her own promise to solve the case.
- Changed: Inheritance reworked (auditor, superfan): Hester inherits Odile's oath plus one added promise, never to open a door unwitnessed; the cellar lock is bound to that promise, and the ledger's twist is stated (unwitnessed openings hid a household's kindness, not a crime).
- Changed: Idris replaced as a stock figure (superfan, auditor): no longer the soup-bringing glazier but the town binder who charms the locks Hester picks and takes it personally; Quill now has a stake, having witnessed for Odile for twenty years.
- Changed: Book 2 is now a non-death case, a will vanished from a word-bound strongroom (superfan: body count), and locks are charmed once to one thing, which closes the rebinding loophole and restores the single-binding limit that makes book 4 fair.
- Changed: Hester given a personal, non-inherited cost of having no magic (no charm takes from her hand, so she alone can lose her keys) and the restored line about opening a neighbor's worst morning; paid for by cutting the pantry example, the old cellar-promise sentences and one engine sentence.
- Declined: A new town detail unique to charmed doors (superfan): no room under the length cap, and the praised steps, slate roofs and landing markets line stays word for word.
- Declined: Windows that can never be charmed as a standing loophole (auditor): adds a mechanism to every case and weakens the short, solvable rule list.
- Declined: Further distancing from rule-bound magical locked-room predecessors (auditor): the resemblance is trope-level, and the no-magic sworn locksmith plus the promise lock already separate it.
- For the author to decide: The cellar lock now opens when Hester breaks her promise never to open a door unwitnessed, rather than a promise never to open the cellar. Confirm you want book 4's solution to require her to open one door without Quill.
- For the author to decide: Idris is now the town binder instead of the glazier with the soup. Confirm the romance should run on professional friction (he sets locks, she defeats them) rather than quiet kindness.

**Gate (editor, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (slight). B ties rules to payoff: the promise-lock rule sets up book 4, where Hester breaks her own promise, and the ledger answer is kind, not just blank. Idris as binder gives the romance friction, Quill links to Odile, and book 2's vanished will varies the cases. What the losing version did better: A's logline is more concrete (neighbor dies behind a locked door). Keep the pantry-at-breakfast example, Idris's unasked soup, Quill complaining on each stair, the fair-play line in the engine, and the cellar lock set up in the lead.
