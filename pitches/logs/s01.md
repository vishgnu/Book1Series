# s01: Under Pilotage

Revision log. Genre: Science fiction adventure (space). Target readers: Men and women 35–65.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Under Pilotage. The only fueling port in the Caird system lies inside the rings of its gas giant, and the one way in is the Passage: a lane through ice and shepherd moonlets that shifts by the hour. By treaty no ship may run it without a licensed pilot of the Passage Pilot Service, boarded from a cutter at the outer marker. Pilots are neutral, like doctors: they bring through any ship of any flag. On the bridge the pilot has the conn and nothing else: no authority over crew, cargo or quarrels. Lead: Maren Achterberg, 44, nineteen years a pilot, never lost a ship, dry, exact, a bad knee from a boarding-ladder fall, raising her nephew on the station. Each book is one 31-hour transit aboard one troubled ship where she is the only stranger: a liner whose captain is dying and whose first officer wants him gone, a grain hauler with stowaways, a warship that is on no register, a yacht with a passenger who is not there willingly. She cannot interfere, but nobody gets through without her. Cast ideas: her cutter coxswain; the Pilot Master; an apprentice; a port inspector. Thread: the Passage is getting worse on purpose; someone is nudging a shepherd moonlet. Real problem-solving, orbital mechanics that behave, competence under pressure.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> No boarding-pilot lead found. Nearest pieces: Ginger Booth, Spaceship Thrive (a captain crossing a gas giant's rings); the Pilots' Guild in C. J. Cherryh's Foreigner (the name only); Nevil Shute's novella Pilotage (the title only). Call it a pilot service, never a guild.

**First draft:** “Under Pilotage”, 514 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Tom).** A harbor pilot in space who holds the conn and nothing else: that is a real job with real limits, and I would borrow book 1 tonight. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: "holds the conn, nothing else" - the hero commands nothing and can only steer; her one lever is where the ship is and when; One transit per book, 31 hours, board at the outer marker and tie up at the Kettle, with navigation solved by "burns, margins and timing the reader can follow"; Maren herself: nineteen years, never lost a ship, the knee, "headings, numbers and dry understatement", and Ines at the helm the day she fell
- Problem 1: The villain's plan does not add up as stated. A fuel consortium closing the only lane to the only fuel port loses its own customers, and "paid beacons" cannot guide ships through a closed lane. Why it matters: I drop books when the scheme behind the plot makes no engineering or business sense. This is the four-book payoff. Suggested fix: Say the pushes make the lane too erratic for a human to call six hours ahead, so only the consortium's beacon net can run it. One clause in the running thread does it.
- Problem 2: Why the lane shifts "by the hour" and why a pilot can read it from "forty minutes of radar returns" when ship computers and tables cannot is asserted, not explained. Why it matters: Ring dynamics are slow and predictable in my head. If the pilot's edge over automation is hand-waved, the whole premise wobbles. Suggested fix: Add one sentence of mechanism: what stirs the ice (shepherd resonances, the Kettle's own mining, collisions) and why local judgment beats onboard prediction.
- Problem 3: Book 1 has the weakest hazard line. "A drift the tables cannot explain" is vague, and the captain-versus-first-officer quarrel is the most familiar of the four. Why it matters: Book 1 is the one I decide on. Books 2 and 3 sound better than the one I would actually borrow. Suggested fix: Name the concrete navigation problem in Outer Marker: what the liner must clear, by when, and what relieving the captain mid-lane would cost in the lane.
- Problem 4: Every ship arrives already in trouble, and the first officer and the yacht's owner both read as possible fools or villains. The pitch never says the crews are competent. Why it matters: I want capable people on both sides of the conn. A pilot who is only good because the ship's officers are bad gets old fast. Suggested fix: Give one ship's officer per book a line showing skill and a defensible reason for their position, starting with the Corazon Vale's first officer.
- Reminded them of: Nathan Lowell, Solar Clipper series (working merchant spacers); Mark Twain, Life on the Mississippi (river pilots reading a shifting channel); The Expanse (burns and physics that hold); C. J. Cherryh, Merchanter novels (stations, flags, neutral trade); Patrick O'Brian, Aubrey-Maturin (seamanship as plot); Columbia River bar pilots / Panama Canal pilotage

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A harbor pilot in space with one lever is a fresh, solid premise, but the opening lines describe a job, not a danger, so I nearly swiped. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 6, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: "The hero commands nothing. She cannot arrest, order or take sides; she can only steer." This is the real hook and it is what sold me.; The engine: "one ship, 31 hours, a crew in trouble, a lane closing around them", with a complete story and no cliffhanger each time.; The Passage and Kettle Station: a lane "that shifts by the hour", pilot boarded by cutter, any flag served. Concrete, believable, easy to picture.
- Problem 1: First ten seconds: my eye caught "pilots ships through the shifting ice lane" and "31 hours". I would have swiped at "holding the conn and no other authority" because it reads as a job description. Why it matters: I still did not know what goes wrong, what is at stake, or why her lack of authority is exciting. The best line sits at the very bottom. Suggested fix: Lead with the constraint as trouble: she cannot arrest, order or take sides, only steer, and this ship has a dying captain and a mutinous first officer in a lane that is closing.
- Problem 2: Stakes stay abstract. "A crew in trouble" and "a drift the tables cannot explain" never say what happens if she gets it wrong: nobody states that ships break up or people die in the Passage. Why it matters: I want tension. "Has never lost a ship" only matters if I can see what losing one looks like. Suggested fix: Add one concrete line of consequence in the setting or engine: what the ice does to a hull that misses its window, and how many are aboard the Corazon Vale.
- Problem 3: Maren is a résumé more than a person: 44, nineteen years, a knee, a nephew. "Dry understatement" is asserted but never shown, and Ines being "at the helm the day Maren fell" is left hanging. Why it matters: I follow a series for the person. I cannot yet hear her, and I do not know if she and Ines are friends or carrying a grudge. Suggested fix: Give one line of Maren's actual speech in the lead paragraph, and say in a clause what stands between her and Ines since the fall.
- Problem 4: The running thread ends on "a fuel consortium that wants paid beacons in place of pilots". After a warship and a moved moonlet, the villain is a faceless company, and book 4 is a yacht. Why it matters: Four books of build-up should pay off with someone I can dislike and a final transit that sounds like the hardest, not the smallest. Suggested fix: Name one person who fronts the consortium and put them aboard the Sable Wren, and state that the lane in book 4 is the narrowest it has ever been.
- Reminded them of: Nathan Lowell, Solar Clipper series (working-life space fiction); The Expanse (ring ice, physics that holds, minus the war); C. J. Cherryh, merchanter novels; Patrick O'Brian / Hornblower-style sea procedure; Murder on the Orient Express (sealed vessel, outsider aboard); Becky Chambers, Wayfarers (danger without cruelty)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now opens on the constraint (cannot arrest, order or take sides) and states the cost of a wrong heading; answers the browser's first-ten-seconds point. The duplicate sentence in 'What makes it different' was cut to pay for it.
- Changed: Setting adds one clause of mechanism (moonlets' pull plus the Kettle's mining outrun the tables) and one line of consequence (a missed window holes the hull in minutes); answers the cold reader on why a pilot beats tables and the browser on abstract stakes.
- Changed: Book 1 line made concrete: 2,100 aboard, a window past Hob nine minutes shorter than the tables say, and a first officer who is right about relieving the captain but wrong about when; answers the weak-hazard and competent-crew points and seeds Hob from book 1.
- Changed: Running thread and book 4 reworked: the consortium makes the lane too erratic for human pilots so ships must rent its beacon net, chair Corwin Hale, 58, is aboard the Sable Wren, and the lane is the narrowest on record; answers the villain-logic and faceless-payoff points.
- Changed: Lead gains one line of Maren's speech ("closer than I'd choose") and the cast says she and Ines never raise the fall; answers the resume-not-a-person point.
- Declined: A line of skill and defensible motive for one officer in every book: done for book 1 only, since doing all four would overrun the length limit.
- Declined: Naming collisions as a third cause of lane shift: two causes are enough to take in on one pass.
- For the author to decide: Is Corwin Hale, 58, acceptable as the consortium's face, and should he appear or be mentioned before book 4 so the payoff is planted?
- For the author to decide: The pitch is now roughly 550 words, above the 520 target though under the 580 cutoff; if it must come down, the candidates are the Ines clause and the passenger count.

**Gate (reader: Tom, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). B tells me why the lane shifts (moonlet pull, mining), what failure costs (holed in minutes, 2,100 aboard), and gives a real number: a window nine minutes short. The first officer being right but mistimed promises no idiots. The beacon-net motive makes engineering sense. What the losing version did better: A's logline is cleaner and plainer; B's opens on a clumsy negative. A also keeps book 4 leaner without naming the consortium chair, and its "cannot arrest, order or take sides" sits better in the closing section.
