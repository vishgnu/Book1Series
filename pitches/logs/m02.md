# m02: The Leaning Shadows Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Cozy paranormal mystery. Target readers: Women 45–75.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Where the Shadows Lean. Edie Marsh, 57, finds missing heirs for the Surrogate's Court of a fictional Finger Lakes county in upstate New York (lake towns, canal-era families, a courthouse records room she loves). She works on commission: no heir, no fee. She has one advantage she can never put in a report: in any room, whatever the light, she sees the shadows of blood relatives lean toward one another, more steeply the closer the tie. It shows that two people are kin, never how; it shows nothing of marriage, adoption or love; it needs a light and both people in the room; and it is not evidence, so everything she sees must then be proved on paper: censuses, baptism books, deeds, ships' lists, a DNA test somebody has to agree to. Each book is one estate with no known heir and one death that paid somebody: a grieving daughter whose shadow stands straight beside her mother's sister; two strangers at a graveside whose shadows cross like siblings; a gardener and the dead man's brother. Cast ideas: the Surrogate's Court clerk, her oldest friend; the county historian, who guards his archive like a dragon; her daughter, 29, a genetic counselor who thinks her mother is simply observant; the county coroner, a dairy farmer elected to the job. Edie was a foundling, left at the courthouse in 1969; her own shadow has never leaned toward anyone. Thread: the woman who can see everyone's family but hers finds, case by case, where her own line runs. Titles from light and shadow (Where the Shadows Lean, Noon Light, The Long Shadow, By Lamplight).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Diane Capri's Heir Hunter series, Chris Larsgaard's The Heir Hunter and R. Franklin James's The Inheritance (heir finders as sleuths, no paranormal element); Brynn Bonner's Family History Mysteries (a cozy genealogist with a psychic partner); Rett MacPherson's Torie O'Shea series (a genealogist sleuth, no paranormal element); Kika Hatzopoulou, Threads That Bind (a young-adult fantasy investigator who sees threads linking people to what they love). No heir finder with a kinship sight was found. 'Next of Kin' is taken by several crime novels and has already been dropped. The sight has already been changed from a thread of light to leaning shadows, and the cast and titles moved away from another pitch in this slate about a notary (no legal wording in titles, no digital nephew, no sheriff's detective).

**First draft:** “The Leaning Shadows Mysteries”, 522 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Margaret).** A quiet, grown-up idea with a gentle gift and a woman my age looking for her people; I would borrow book one, hoping the town turns out warmer than this page. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: Edie herself: 'a foundling left on the courthouse steps in March 1969, she has never seen her own shadow lean' and 'she talks to file boxes'; The gift's limits: 'it says two people are kin and stops', with the rest done by 'censuses, baptism books, deeds'; The ending promised in book 4: 'a kitchen where her shadow leans', and 'the right family gets the house'
- Problem 1: The cast is a list of jobs and ages. Only Ambrose, who 'guards his archive like a dragon', has any life. Dot is just 'oldest friend'; Walt is a farmer and coroner, nothing more. Why it matters: I read for people I would miss. From this page I would miss Edie and nobody else. Suggested fix: Give Dot, Walt and Nora one habit or one bit of friction with Edie each: what Dot nags her about, what Walt brings to the courthouse, what Nora and her mother argue over at supper.
- Problem 2: The warmth is promised in one short line, 'Church suppers, grape harvest, courthouse gossip', while most of the page is estates, dockets and DNA kits. Humor is claimed nowhere and shown only in the file boxes. Why it matters: It reads a little dry and legal. I want to know where Edie has her coffee and who sits down with her. Suggested fix: Add a regular gathering place and a recurring ritual: a diner booth, a Thursday supper, Dot's kitchen. Let one line of the pitch be funny in Edie's own dry voice.
- Problem 3: The four book summaries are packed so tight I stumbled. 'A stranger arrives grieving as Ruth Vandermark's lost daughter' took me two readings, and each book's death is hard to pick out. Why it matters: If I have to reread a sentence on the series page, I start to wonder whether the books will be hard going too. Suggested fix: For each book say plainly who died, who stands to inherit, and what Edie sees. Two short sentences each instead of one crowded one.
- Problem 4: 'Proving on paper what she sees costs her months and friendships' and the DNA kits make me worry about a lonely, cold heroine and laboratory work replacing the old books. Why it matters: I do not want a woman losing her friends over four books, and I prefer parish registers to test tubes. Suggested fix: Say which friendship is strained and that it mends. Keep DNA as a last confirming step, with the ledgers, deeds and Ambrose's archive doing the real finding.
- Reminded them of: Finding Your Roots (PBS); Long Lost Family; Nathan Dylan Goodwin's Forensic Genealogist mysteries; Louise Penny's Three Pines (for the village); Murder, She Wrote; Ghost Whisperer, but much quieter

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** The leaning shadows and the foundling who has never seen her own lean got me; the courthouse vocabulary and thin humor nearly lost me first. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 7, clarity 6.

- Keep: "the shadows of blood relatives lean toward each other" and its limits: "kin, or not", "needs a light and both people in the room"; "A foundling left on the courthouse steps in March 1969, she has never seen her own shadow lean" paid off by "a kitchen where her shadow leans"; Edie at 57, "Dry, patient, exact; she talks to file boxes", with Dot and Ambrose who "guards his archive like a dragon"
- Problem 1: In ten seconds my eye caught "finds missing heirs for a Finger Lakes Surrogate's Court" before the shadows. I nearly swiped at "Surrogate's Court"; the magic comes second, after a job title I had to decode. Why it matters: I stopped not knowing whether anyone gets murdered or whether it is funny. It sounded like probate paperwork, not a mystery to curl up with. Suggested fix: Lead with the image: "Edie Farrow sees the shadows of blood relatives lean toward each other." Then the job in plain words: she finds lost heirs for the county court. Say "suspicious death" outright.
- Problem 2: The murder is vague. "one death that paid somebody" is coy, and the book lines name heirs but no killers, suspects or danger; book 3 has no death mentioned at all. Why it matters: I borrow cozies for a whodunit. Four inheritance puzzles without a clear crime could feel like genealogy homework, however charming. Suggested fix: Give each book line its death and its question: who died, why it looks wrong, who benefits. For book 3, state how Hollis Ostrander died and who is suspected.
- Problem 3: Humor and warmth are promised but not shown. "Church suppers, grape harvest, courthouse gossip" is a list; only "talks to file boxes" and the dragon archive made me smile. No pet, no romance, no recurring comfort spot. Why it matters: Warmth is why I binge four books. This page reads a little dry and lonely; Edie's work "costs her months and friendships" sounds sad, not cozy. Suggested fix: Add one funny, concrete moment between Edie and Dot or Walt the dairy-farmer coroner, and one comfort anchor (a diner booth, a dog, a slow-burn companion). Soften "costs her friendships" to friction.
- Problem 4: Book titles blur: "Where the Shadows Lean", "Noon Light", "The Long Shadow", "By Lamplight" are pretty but interchangeable, and none says mystery or murder. Why it matters: On my phone I pick the next book by title and cover. These could be literary fiction or quiet romance; I would not remember which I had read. Suggested fix: Keep the light motif but add a crime or place hook per title, for example a death word or the estate name: "The Vandermark Shadow", "Drowned at Noon", "Murder by Lamplight".
- Reminded them of: Finding Your Roots / Long Lost Family (TV); Nathan Dane Goodwin's Forensic Genealogist series; Heir Hunters (BBC); Louise Penny's Three Pines (village warmth); Ghost Whisperer (limited gift); Paige Shelton cozy paranormals

**Rewrite: accepted (slight).**

- Changed: Logline now opens on the leaning shadows, gives the job in plain words (no 'Surrogate's Court') and says 'suspicious death' outright (ten-second-browser, problem 1).
- Changed: Each book line now states who died, why it looks wrong, what Edie sees and that she names the killer, in short sentences; book 3 gets its death (both critics).
- Changed: Retitled all four books with a crime or place hook while keeping the light motif: Shadow on the Stairs, Drowned at Noon, The Greenhouse Shadow, Death by Lamplight (ten-second-browser, problem 4).
- Changed: Dot and Walt each get one warm, funny habit, a Thursday diner pie anchors the comfort, and 'costs her months and friendships' is softened to 'tries her friends' patience' (both critics on warmth and loneliness).
- Changed: DNA is now only a confirming step after ledgers, deeds and Ambrose's archive (cold-reader, problem 4). To pay for the additions I cut two setting details, the lead's repeated name and age, and one clause in 'Why readers come back'; by my count the page is still about 555 words, over the 520 target but under the 580 limit.
- Declined: A pet or slow-burn romance for Edie: it adds a subplot the page has no room for, and Walt's coffee leaves that door open.
- Declined: A quoted funny line in Edie's own voice and a supper argument with Nora: no room at this length; the humor is carried by Dot's and Walt's habits instead.
- Declined: Spelling out which friendship is strained and mends: softening the cost to 'tries her friends' patience' removes the worry in fewer words.
- For the author to decide: The new book titles trade some of the lyrical quality for a clear crime signal; do you want that trade, or a middle form that keeps 'Where the Shadows Lean' for book 1?
- For the author to decide: Should Walt become Edie's slow-burn companion across the four books? One critic asked for it, and it would change the cast line and book 4's ending.

**Gate (reader: Margaret, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (slight). A gives me people to miss: Dot reading mail upside down, Walt bringing coffee, Thursday pie at the diner. Each book plainly has a death and a killer named, so I know it is a proper mystery. B's "costs her friendships" and "a death that paid somebody" feel colder. What the losing version did better: B's titles are gentler and more inviting (Where the Shadows Lean, By Lamplight); "so you can solve it alongside her" is a welcome promise; the stone courthouse, eleven-mile lake and the brother expecting the canal mansion add place and motive.
