# m02: The Leaning Shadows Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Cozy paranormal mystery. Target readers: Women 45–75.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Where the Shadows Lean. Edie Marsh, 57, finds missing heirs for the Surrogate's Court of a fictional Finger Lakes county in upstate New York (lake towns, canal-era families, a courthouse records room she loves). She works on commission: no heir, no fee. She has one advantage she can never put in a report: in any room, whatever the light, she sees the shadows of blood relatives lean toward one another, more steeply the closer the tie. It shows that two people are kin, never how; it shows nothing of marriage, adoption or love; it needs a light and both people in the room; and it is not evidence, so everything she sees must then be proved on paper: censuses, baptism books, deeds, ships' lists, a DNA test somebody has to agree to. Each book is one estate with no known heir and one death that paid somebody: a grieving daughter whose shadow stands straight beside her mother's sister; two strangers at a graveside whose shadows cross like siblings; a gardener and the dead man's brother. Cast ideas: the Surrogate's Court clerk, her oldest friend; the county historian, who guards his archive like a dragon; her daughter, 29, a genetic counselor who thinks her mother is simply observant; the county coroner, a dairy farmer elected to the job. Edie was a foundling, left at the courthouse in 1969; her own shadow has never leaned toward anyone. Thread: the woman who can see everyone's family but hers finds, case by case, where her own line runs. Titles from light and shadow (Where the Shadows Lean, Noon Light, The Long Shadow, By Lamplight).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Diane Capri's Heir Hunter series, Chris Larsgaard's The Heir Hunter and R. Franklin James's The Inheritance (heir finders as sleuths, no paranormal element); Brynn Bonner's Family History Mysteries (a cozy genealogist with a psychic partner); Rett MacPherson's Torie O'Shea series (a genealogist sleuth, no paranormal element); Kika Hatzopoulou, Threads That Bind (a young-adult fantasy investigator who sees threads linking people to what they love). No heir finder with a kinship sight was found. 'Next of Kin' is taken by several crime novels and has already been dropped. The sight has already been changed from a thread of light to leaning shadows, and the cast and titles moved away from another pitch in this slate about a notary (no legal wording in titles, no digital nephew, no sheriff's detective).

**First draft:** “The Leaning Shadows Mysteries”, 522 words.

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** A well-limited, visually fresh gift on a thin shelf (heir finding), wrapped in a very familiar small-town cozy shell and a foundling arc readers will guess early. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 7, engine 8, character 7, setting 5, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: The gift as a one-bit fact with hard limits: "kin, or not... needs a light and both people in the room"; "The gift is a question, never an answer"; Commission heir finder for the county court ("no heir, no fee") with paper proof: "censuses, baptism books, deeds"; "consented DNA only confirms"; "she has never seen her own shadow lean" plus Nora, the genetic counselor daughter who "thinks her mother is simply observant"
- Problem 1: Most generic element: the shell. "Thursday pie at the diner", "courthouse gossip", vineyards, feuding old families and a friendly coroner are the standard small-town cozy kit; Finger Lakes wine country is itself a used cozy setting (from memory). Why it matters: A shelf reader has met this town dozens of times; the setting adds nothing only this series could have, so the premise carries all the freshness alone. Suggested fix: Build the town on the buried asset: the "dry canal bed" and "six canal-era families". Swap the diner for the courthouse records basement or a canal-society reading room, and make the feuds about lock-tender and towpath land titles.
- Problem 2: The running thread, a foundling genealogist searching for her own parents, is the genre's default arc for a genealogist lead, and "Midwife... one birth ledger missing" plus "a 1969 entry" telegraphs the answer by book 4's blurb. Why it matters: Adoptee-seeks-birth-family is the most-met plot in genealogical fiction and DNA-reveal television (from memory); a guessable answer weakens the reason to finish four books. Suggested fix: Keep the foundling, change the question: in book 2 her DNA kit returns matches in two of the six families that paper says never joined. The thread becomes which record was falsified and by whom, not merely who her mother was.
- Problem 3: The most distinctive element, the sight's blind spots ("shows nothing of marriage, adoption or love"), sits in the engine paragraph but drives only book 1; books 2 and 3 use the sight as a plain positive pointer to secret children. Why it matters: A gift that just reveals hidden offspring resembles any psychic-genealogy cozy, such as Bonner's; the limit is what separates this from that shelf. Suggested fix: Make each case turn on a different limit: book 2, the legal heir is adopted and the sight wrongly argues against her; book 3, a lean Edie cannot explain proves a half-relation, with the paper trail overturning her first reading.
- Problem 4: All four cases share one template: an elderly or solitary property owner dies at home, a secret or false child surfaces, Edie "names heir and killer". Three of four hinge on an unrecorded child. Why it matters: Sameness reads as formula on a binge, and the secret-illegitimate-heir motive is the oldest inheritance plot in the mystery genre, from Golden Age novels onward (from memory). Suggested fix: Vary the kinship puzzle: one book where the sight shows the acknowledged heir is no blood kin at all, one where two families each claim the same ancestor, one where the estate is a debt nobody wants to inherit.
- Reminded them of: Brynn Bonner, Family History Mysteries (genealogist plus psychic partner; shared trope, not shared device); Kika Hatzopoulou, Threads That Bind (visible kin/affection links; residual echo despite the change to shadows); Diane Capri, Heir Hunter series / Chris Larsgaard, The Heir Hunter (profession only); Rett MacPherson, Torie O'Shea series (small-town genealogist sleuth); Heir Hunters, BBC documentary series (commission probate research; from memory); Nathan Dylan Goodwin, Morton Farrier forensic genealogist series (document-led proof, lead with own parentage mystery; from memory)
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances rest on the supplied 15 November 2026 audit plus memory; items marked (from memory) are unverified. No existing work found or recalled that combines heir finder, kinship sight and shadow device, so resemblance is trope-level, not premise-plus-setting-plus-device. Finger Lakes cozy saturation and title availability (e.g. 'Shadow on the Stairs', 'Death by Lamplight' sound likely to be in use) need the web check.
