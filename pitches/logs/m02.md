# m02: The Leaning Shadows Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Cozy paranormal mystery. Target readers: Women 45–75.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Where the Shadows Lean. Edie Marsh, 57, finds missing heirs for the Surrogate's Court of a fictional Finger Lakes county in upstate New York (lake towns, canal-era families, a courthouse records room she loves). She works on commission: no heir, no fee. She has one advantage she can never put in a report: in any room, whatever the light, she sees the shadows of blood relatives lean toward one another, more steeply the closer the tie. It shows that two people are kin, never how; it shows nothing of marriage, adoption or love; it needs a light and both people in the room; and it is not evidence, so everything she sees must then be proved on paper: censuses, baptism books, deeds, ships' lists, a DNA test somebody has to agree to. Each book is one estate with no known heir and one death that paid somebody: a grieving daughter whose shadow stands straight beside her mother's sister; two strangers at a graveside whose shadows cross like siblings; a gardener and the dead man's brother. Cast ideas: the Surrogate's Court clerk, her oldest friend; the county historian, who guards his archive like a dragon; her daughter, 29, a genetic counselor who thinks her mother is simply observant; the county coroner, a dairy farmer elected to the job. Edie was a foundling, left at the courthouse in 1969; her own shadow has never leaned toward anyone. Thread: the woman who can see everyone's family but hers finds, case by case, where her own line runs. Titles from light and shadow (Where the Shadows Lean, Noon Light, The Long Shadow, By Lamplight).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Diane Capri's Heir Hunter series, Chris Larsgaard's The Heir Hunter and R. Franklin James's The Inheritance (heir finders as sleuths, no paranormal element); Brynn Bonner's Family History Mysteries (a cozy genealogist with a psychic partner); Rett MacPherson's Torie O'Shea series (a genealogist sleuth, no paranormal element); Kika Hatzopoulou, Threads That Bind (a young-adult fantasy investigator who sees threads linking people to what they love). No heir finder with a kinship sight was found. 'Next of Kin' is taken by several crime novels and has already been dropped. The sight has already been changed from a thread of light to leaning shadows, and the cast and titles moved away from another pitch in this slate about a notary (no legal wording in titles, no digital nephew, no sheriff's detective).

**First draft:** “The Leaning Shadows Mysteries”, 522 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Margaret).** A quiet, grown-up idea with a gentle gift and a woman my age looking for her people; I would borrow book one, hoping the town turns out warmer than this page. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: Edie herself: 'a foundling left on the courthouse steps in March 1969, she has never seen her own shadow lean' and 'she talks to file boxes'; The gift's limits: 'it says two people are kin and stops', with the rest done by 'censuses, baptism books, deeds'; The ending promised in book 4: 'a kitchen where her shadow leans', and 'the right family gets the house'
- Problem 1: The cast is a list of jobs and ages. Only Ambrose, who 'guards his archive like a dragon', has any life. Dot is just 'oldest friend'; Walt is a farmer and coroner, nothing more. Why it matters: I read for people I would miss. From this page I would miss Edie and nobody else. Suggested fix: Give Dot, Walt and Nora one habit or one bit of friction with Edie each: what Dot nags her about, what Walt brings to the courthouse, what Nora and her mother argue over at supper.
- Problem 2: The warmth is promised in one short line, 'Church suppers, grape harvest, courthouse gossip', while most of the page is estates, dockets and DNA kits. Humor is claimed nowhere and shown only in the file boxes. Why it matters: It reads a little dry and legal. I want to know where Edie has her coffee and who sits down with her. Suggested fix: Add a regular gathering place and a recurring ritual: a diner booth, a Thursday supper, Dot's kitchen. Let one line of the pitch be funny in Edie's own dry voice.
- Problem 3: The four book summaries are packed so tight I stumbled. 'A stranger arrives grieving as Ruth Vandermark's lost daughter' took me two readings, and each book's death is hard to pick out. Why it matters: If I have to reread a sentence on the series page, I start to wonder whether the books will be hard going too. Suggested fix: For each book say plainly who died, who stands to inherit, and what Edie sees. Two short sentences each instead of one crowded one.
- Problem 4: 'Proving on paper what she sees costs her months and friendships' and the DNA kits make me worry about a lonely, cold heroine and laboratory work replacing the old books. Why it matters: I do not want a woman losing her friends over four books, and I prefer parish registers to test tubes. Suggested fix: Say which friendship is strained and that it mends. Keep DNA as a last confirming step, with the ledgers, deeds and Ambrose's archive doing the real finding.
- Reminded them of: Finding Your Roots (PBS); Long Lost Family; Nathan Dylan Goodwin's Forensic Genealogist mysteries; Louise Penny's Three Pines (for the village); Murder, She Wrote; Ghost Whisperer, but much quieter

Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 7, clarity 6.

Why it matters: On my phone I pick the next book by title and cover. These could be literary fiction or quiet romance; I would not remember which I had read. Suggested fix: Keep the light motif but add a crime or place hook per title, for example a death word or the estate name: "The Vandermark Shadow", "Drowned at Noon", "Murder by Lamplight".

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** A well-limited, visually fresh gift on a thin shelf (heir finding), wrapped in a very familiar small-town cozy shell and a foundling arc readers will guess early. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 7, engine 8, character 7, setting 5, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: The gift as a one-bit fact with hard limits: "kin, or not... needs a light and both people in the room"; "The gift is a question, never an answer"; Commission heir finder for the county court ("no heir, no fee") with paper proof: "censuses, baptism books, deeds"; "consented DNA only confirms"; "she has never seen her own shadow lean" plus Nora, the genetic counselor daughter who "thinks her mother is simply observant"
- Problem 1: Most generic element: the shell. "Thursday pie at the diner", "courthouse gossip", vineyards, feuding old families and a friendly coroner are the standard small-town cozy kit; Finger Lakes wine country is itself a used cozy setting (from memory). Why it matters: A shelf reader has met this town dozens of times; the setting adds nothing only this series could have, so the premise carries all the freshness alone. Suggested fix: Build the town on the buried asset: the "dry canal bed" and "six canal-era families". Swap the diner for the courthouse records basement or a canal-society reading room, and make the feuds about lock-tender and towpath land titles.
- Problem 2: The running thread, a foundling genealogist searching for her own parents, is the genre's default arc for a genealogist lead, and "Midwife... one birth ledger missing" plus "a 1969 entry" telegraphs the answer by book 4's blurb. Why it matters: Adoptee-seeks-birth-family is the most-met plot in genealogical fiction and DNA-reveal television (from memory); a guessable answer weakens the reason to finish four books. Suggested fix: Keep the foundling, change the question: in book 2 her DNA kit returns matches in two of the six families that paper says never joined. The thread becomes which record was falsified and by whom, not merely who her mother was.
- Problem 3: The most distinctive element, the sight's blind spots ("shows nothing of marriage, adoption or love"), sits in the engine paragraph but drives only book 1; books 2 and 3 use the sight as a plain positive pointer to secret children. Why it matters: A gift that just reveals hidden offspring resembles any psychic-genealogy cozy, such as Bonner's; the limit is what separates this from that shelf. Suggested fix: Make each case turn on a different limit: book 2, the legal heir is adopted and the sight wrongly argues against her; book 3, a lean Edie cannot explain proves a half-relation, with the paper trail overturning her first reading.
- Problem 4: All four cases share one template: an elderly or solitary property owner dies at home, a secret or false child surfaces, Edie "names heir and killer". Three of four hinge on an unrecorded child. Why it matters: Sameness reads as formula on a binge, and the secret-illegitimate-heir motive is the oldest inheritance plot in the mystery genre, from Golden Age novels onward (from memory). Suggested fix: Vary the kinship puzzle: one book where the sight shows the acknowledged heir is no blood kin at all, one where two families each claim the same ancestor, one where the estate is a debt nobody wants to inherit.
- Reminded them of: Brynn Bonner, Family History Mysteries (genealogist plus psychic partner; shared trope, not shared device); Kika Hatzopoulou, Threads That Bind (visible kin/affection links; residual echo despite the change to shadows); Diane Capri, Heir Hunter series / Chris Larsgaard, The Heir Hunter (profession only); Rett MacPherson, Torie O'Shea series (small-town genealogist sleuth); Heir Hunters, BBC documentary series (commission probate research; from memory); Nathan Dylan Goodwin, Morton Farrier forensic genealogist series (document-led proof, lead with own parentage mystery; from memory)
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances rest on the supplied 15 November 2026 audit plus memory; items marked (from memory) are unverified. No existing work found or recalled that combines heir finder, kinship sight and shadow device, so resemblance is trope-level, not premise-plus-setting-plus-device. Finger Lakes cozy saturation and title availability (e.g. 'Shadow on the Stairs', 'Death by Lamplight' sound likely to be in use) need the web check.

**superfan-reader (Carol).** An heir hunter whose gift only says "kin, or not" is new to me; the town around her is furniture I have sat on many times. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 7, setting 6, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: The limited gift: "kin, or not" and it "needs a light and both people in the room" - a question, never an answer, so the puzzle stays fair; The job itself: finding lost heirs for the county court on commission, "no heir, no fee" - I have not met this sleuth before, and the docket refills itself; Edie the foundling who "has never seen her own shadow lean", with the small steps per book (opens file, mails DNA kit, archive, the kitchen)
- Problem 1: The town is stock: "Thursday pie at the diner", lake, vineyards, grape harvest, feuding founding families, courthouse gossip. Only the "dry canal bed behind Main Street" is fresh, and nothing happens there. Why it matters: I have read this lake town a hundred times. The job is new; the place it sits in should feel as particular as the job. Suggested fix: Replace the diner pie first. Make the gathering place the courthouse records room or a surrogate's-court estate auction day, and use the canal bed (lock houses, towpath, old canal families) in at least two cases.
- Problem 2: Four books, same skeleton: an older person dies at home, a stranger or unrecorded child appears, a shadow leans or doesn't, "Edie names heir and killer". Three of four victims are elderly property owners. Why it matters: Binge readers notice the template by book two. If I can predict the shape, I stop borrowing. Suggested fix: Vary the case type: one book where the shadow leans toward the wrong person (kin but not heir), one where the estate is a business or debt nobody wants, one where the dead person is the heir.
- Problem 3: The supporting cast are familiar types with one tic each: nosy best friend clerk, archive-guarding historian, skeptical grown daughter, and Walt the coroner who "brings Edie coffee to death scenes" is the handsome sheriff in overalls. Why it matters: Nobody here wants anything or stands in Edie's way. Cozies live on the circle of friends, and these are props so far. Suggested fix: Give each a stake: Dot belongs to one of the six families, Ambrose is hiding something in the 1969 records, Nora's genetics work collides with the gift. Let Walt be a friend with his own trouble, not a suitor.
- Problem 4: The motive is the same every time: someone kills for an inheritance. And the foundling answer is guessable now: Edie will belong to one of the six canal families via the midwife's ledger. Why it matters: If I can name the book 4 reveal from the pitch, the running thread will not pull me through three books. Suggested fix: Plant a harder question early: in book 1 Edie's shadow fails to lean toward the family everyone assumes is hers. Make at least two killers' motives about hiding kinship, not grabbing money.
- Reminded them of: Heir Hunters (BBC series); Finding Your Roots; Long Lost Family; Paige Shelton's Country Cooking School ghost cozies (limited-gift style); Ellery Adams small-town cozies; Nathan Dylan Goodwin's Forensic Genealogist series

**Rewrite: rejected (slight).** It ran over the length limit and was trimmed by a line editor before the gate.

- Changed: Setting rebuilt on the canal (lock houses, towpath titles, six lock-tending families, first-Thursday estate sale in the courthouse basement); diner pie, vineyards, grape harvest and Finger Lakes label cut. Answers both critics' stock-town point; stone courthouse and eleven-mile lake restored from the earlier version.

- Changed: Running thread hardened: Edie's DNA kit matches two families that records say never joined, so the question becomes who falsified the record as well as who left her; book 4 turns on an altered 1969 entry. Answers the guessable-foundling problem.

**Gate (editor, blind A/B)** preferred the earlier version, so the rewrite was dropped (slight). A keeps the cozy promises on the page: Dot reading mail upside down, Walt's coffee at death scenes, Thursday pie, grape harvest. Every book plainly names heir and killer. B trades that warmth for towpath-title mechanics, and its books 2 and 3 summaries are murky about the resolution. What the losing version did better: B's engine varies the cases: each turns on a blind spot of the gift, and Edie misreads a lean in book 3. Its cast is tied to the plots (Dot born a Brandt, Ambrose's locked 1969 box), and the monthly courthouse estate sale is a good gathering place.
