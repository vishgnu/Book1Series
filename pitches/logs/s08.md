# s08: The Century Unit

Revision log. Genre: Science fiction mystery. Target readers: Men and women 40–70.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Century Unit. Port Sable, 2157. Since the Maintenance arrived in 2049 nobody in the city has died of old age, and the law has followed: murder has no time limit, and no file is closed because the witnesses are gone. They are not gone. They are a hundred and forty and look forty. The Century Unit reopens homicides more than a hundred years old when something surfaces: a letter, a renovation, a keepsake sold at auction. Lead: Inspector Dessa Varga, 154, who was a 27-year-old constable in 2030. The rule that makes every case hard: bodies last, memories do not. After a century people remember what they have told themselves, so every witness's account is a well-worn story, and the evidence that can still be trusted is physical and archival: paper, cloth, bone, and the dead formats of the 2020s and 2030s (phones, cloud accounts, doorbell video) that the Unit's archivist coaxes back to life. Each book is one reopened case from a different decade, with everyone involved still alive, changed and reachable: the widower who has remarried four times, the suspect who became a judge, the victim's child who is now older than the victim ever was. Cast ideas: an archivist who restores dead devices and hoards their chargers; a partner of only 61, treated as the baby; a pathologist who knew the victims as living people; a superintendent who wants the Unit's budget. Thread: her first partner, who walked out during the 2030 case and has been missing for 127 years in a city where nobody dies.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Keith Brooke and Eric Brown, Wormhole (2022; a cold-case detective in 2190 reopens an 80-year-old murder); the television series Ad Vitam (a 120-year-old police officer in a society without death, a current case); Brian Stableford, Architects of Emortality (a police whodunit among near-immortals); Peter F. Hamilton, Great North Road (a police procedural set in 2143); Elly Griffiths, The Frozen People (a cold-case team reaching very old crimes by time travel). No work with the full premise was found. Lead with the memory rule and the dead-format archivist, which none of these has.

**First draft:** “The Century Unit”, 507 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Frank).** A cold-case squad that trusts the receipt over the witness is my kind of shop; I'd borrow book 1, but I want to see a courtroom and a custody log. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 7, character 7, setting 7, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: "She asks for the receipt before the story, in short, dry questions, then waits." That is a real interviewer, and a lead with a code.; The one rule and what it does to the work: "clear memory reaches back seventy years", so the Unit "trusts paper, cloth, bone and dead devices" and witnesses are "certain and wrong".; The basement crew and the small true details: Pell who "hoards chargers", Roux who "knew some victims alive", the widower who "has remarried four times".
- Problem 1: Nothing says what happens after the evidence "names the killer". No prosecutor, no court, no sentence. What does a conviction cost a person who cannot die of old age? Why it matters: A procedural that stops at the name is half a case. Without a consequence I don't know what any of these suspects stand to lose. Suggested fix: Add one line on the law in Port Sable: who charges a 116-year-old homicide, what proof the court accepts when memory is void, and what the sentence is for someone who lives indefinitely.
- Problem 2: In book 4 Varga's own first homicide is reopened and it "makes her a witness", yet the pitch reads as if she still works it. She would be off that case the same day. Why it matters: This is exactly the wrong procedure I snort at. A detective investigating a case she is a witness in taints every piece of evidence. Suggested fix: Say plainly that Okafor takes the lead in book 4 and Varga sits on the other side of the table. That also pays off "Okafor stops being the baby".
- Problem 3: Varga is a police inspector with archive access and has wanted to find Hale for 127 years. The pitch never says why she hasn't simply looked him up. Why it matters: A missing partner is the first file any cop would pull. If there is no reason she failed, the running thread feels held back by the author. Suggested fix: Give the obstacle in one clause: his records sealed or lost in the 2049 changeover, a changed name, or her own notebook showing she was told not to look and forgot it.
- Problem 4: The evidence turns up in a wall, at auction, in harbor mud. No word on chain of custody or how a revived phone is authenticated. And why is a doorbell camera inside a wall? Why it matters: Found objects with a hundred-year gap in custody are a defense lawyer's dream. If the series skips that, the "fair-play" promise is soft. Suggested fix: Make provenance part of the engine: one line that each case must first prove the object is what it claims to be, with Pell's paperwork as a recurring hurdle. Explain the doorbell as walled up deliberately.

- Problem 1: The team frame is the most generic element: a basement cold-case unit with a dry veteran, a young sergeant, an archivist, a pathologist and a superintendent who wants files shut. Why it matters: This is the shape of Department Q, Cold Case, Unforgotten and New Tricks (from memory); shelf readers have met it most often, and here Brandt's objection is the stock one. Why it matters: The audit found no prior work with the dead-format archivist; a pitch should lead with what nobody else has, and vary it so it does not repeat. Suggested fix: Name the format in each book and make reviving it the middle act: a memory card needing a vanished reader, a thermal ferry ticket fading, a ledger in dead software, a locked phone. - Problem 4: "a harbor city of fog, ferries and brick row houses behind a sea wall" is stock noir weather, and "The Maintenance" is a generic capitalized-noun treatment, close to Ad Vitam's regeneration and Stableford's emortals. Why it matters: Nothing in the city itself shows the memory rule; the setting could host any moody procedural, which weakens the claim to difference. Suggested fix: Show a city built around forgetting: public memory registries, notaries who witness diaries, families with keepers of the retold stories, a trade in forged keepsakes.
