# s08: The Century Unit

Revision log. Genre: Science fiction mystery. Target readers: Men and women 40–70.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Century Unit. Port Sable, 2157. Since the Maintenance arrived in 2049 nobody in the city has died of old age, and the law has followed: murder has no time limit, and no file is closed because the witnesses are gone. They are not gone. They are a hundred and forty and look forty. The Century Unit reopens homicides more than a hundred years old when something surfaces: a letter, a renovation, a keepsake sold at auction. Lead: Inspector Dessa Varga, 154, who was a 27-year-old constable in 2030. The rule that makes every case hard: bodies last, memories do not. After a century people remember what they have told themselves, so every witness's account is a well-worn story, and the evidence that can still be trusted is physical and archival: paper, cloth, bone, and the dead formats of the 2020s and 2030s (phones, cloud accounts, doorbell video) that the Unit's archivist coaxes back to life. Each book is one reopened case from a different decade, with everyone involved still alive, changed and reachable: the widower who has remarried four times, the suspect who became a judge, the victim's child who is now older than the victim ever was. Cast ideas: an archivist who restores dead devices and hoards their chargers; a partner of only 61, treated as the baby; a pathologist who knew the victims as living people; a superintendent who wants the Unit's budget. Thread: her first partner, who walked out during the 2030 case and has been missing for 127 years in a city where nobody dies.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Keith Brooke and Eric Brown, Wormhole (2022; a cold-case detective in 2190 reopens an 80-year-old murder); the television series Ad Vitam (a 120-year-old police officer in a society without death, a current case); Brian Stableford, Architects of Emortality (a police whodunit among near-immortals); Peter F. Hamilton, Great North Road (a police procedural set in 2143); Elly Griffiths, The Frozen People (a cold-case team reaching very old crimes by time travel). No work with the full premise was found. Lead with the memory rule and the dead-format archivist, which none of these has.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Frank).** A cold-case squad that trusts the receipt over the witness is my kind of shop; I'd borrow book 1, but I want to see a courtroom and a custody log. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 7, character 7, setting 7, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: "She asks for the receipt before the story, in short, dry questions, then waits." That is a real interviewer, and a lead with a code.; The one rule and what it does to the work: "clear memory reaches back seventy years", so the Unit "trusts paper, cloth, bone and dead devices" and witnesses are "certain and wrong".; The basement crew and the small true details: Pell who "hoards chargers", Roux who "knew some victims alive", the widower who "has remarried four times".
- Problem 1: Nothing says what happens after the evidence "names the killer". No prosecutor, no court, no sentence. What does a conviction cost a person who cannot die of old age? Why it matters: A procedural that stops at the name is half a case. Without a consequence I don't know what any of these suspects stand to lose. Suggested fix: Add one line on the law in Port Sable: who charges a 116-year-old homicide, what proof the court accepts when memory is void, and what the sentence is for someone who lives indefinitely.
- Problem 2: In book 4 Varga's own first homicide is reopened and it "makes her a witness", yet the pitch reads as if she still works it. She would be off that case the same day. Why it matters: This is exactly the wrong procedure I snort at. A detective investigating a case she is a witness in taints every piece of evidence. Suggested fix: Say plainly that Okafor takes the lead in book 4 and Varga sits on the other side of the table. That also pays off "Okafor stops being the baby".
- Problem 3: Varga is a police inspector with archive access and has wanted to find Hale for 127 years. The pitch never says why she hasn't simply looked him up. Why it matters: A missing partner is the first file any cop would pull. If there is no reason she failed, the running thread feels held back by the author. Suggested fix: Give the obstacle in one clause: his records sealed or lost in the 2049 changeover, a changed name, or her own notebook showing she was told not to look and forgot it.
- Problem 4: The evidence turns up in a wall, at auction, in harbor mud. No word on chain of custody or how a revived phone is authenticated. And why is a doorbell camera inside a wall? Why it matters: Found objects with a hundred-year gap in custody are a defense lawyer's dream. If the series skips that, the "fair-play" promise is soft. Suggested fix: Make provenance part of the engine: one line that each case must first prove the object is what it claims to be, with Pell's paperwork as a recurring hurdle. Explain the doorbell as walled up deliberately.
- Reminded them of: Cold Case (TV); New Tricks (TV); Unforgotten (TV); Michael Connelly's Bosch, Open-Unsolved Unit books; Asimov, The Caves of Steel; Ben H. Winters, The Last Policeman

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** "Every witness is alive and misremembers" got my borrow; the page delivers the puzzle but never tells me what is at stake today. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 6, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: First ten seconds: "Every witness is alive and misremembers" plus "Inspector Dessa Varga, 154" stopped my thumb. The series idea in six words, and the later "witnesses a century older than their alibis" delivers on it.; The object openings and evidence rule: "a letter in a wall, a coat at auction, a phone in harbor mud" and "trusts paper, cloth, bone and dead devices". I can see four books, and Midge Pell who "hoards chargers".; "the detective's own memory is on trial", paid off by book 4 making Varga "a witness" who "sent Hale away". A real reason to finish the set, with no cliffhangers needed.
- Problem 1: No present-day stakes. I stopped not knowing what happens to a killer caught after 116 years, or who is hurt if the case stays shut. Book 1's payoff is "the Unit keeps its budget". Why it matters: A humane tone is fine, but I still need something to worry about. Budget survival is office politics, not a reason to borrow tonight. Suggested fix: Add one line on what a conviction costs someone with centuries left to live, and give book 1 a human consequence: the widower's fifth wife, or Ines's child, waiting on the answer.
- Problem 2: My swipe point: "The Maintenance, which arrived in 2049, renews the body but not the mind: clear memory reaches back seventy years". With 2157, 108, 154, 27 and 2030 already, it reads as arithmetic homework. Why it matters: On a phone at night I will not do subtraction. The rule is the best thing here and it arrives buried in dates. Suggested fix: State the rule once in plain words before any year: bodies renew, memory holds about seventy years, everything older is a story people tell. Cut Varga's 2030 age from the lead; keep 154.
- Problem 3: Varga is a method, not yet a person. "She wants to find Corin Hale" is a plot want, and Hale is absent from books 1 and 2. Brandt "wants the Unit's budget" is a stock boss. Why it matters: I binge for company. Okafor and Pell are sketched warmer than the lead I am supposed to follow for four books. Suggested fix: Give Varga one private cost of drifting memory (a notebook entry in her own hand she cannot believe) and plant a small Hale trace in books 1 and 2. Give Brandt a reason beyond money.
- Problem 4: "murder has no time limit" is sold as part of the future, but murder has no statute of limitations in the US now. And a "doorbell camera found in a wall" made me wonder why footage was not online. Why it matters: The promise is one rule followed honestly. Readers my age notice a fake novelty and a clue that should not exist, and trust drops. Suggested fix: Cut "murder has no time limit" or make it the real twist: suspects never die, so no case can be closed by death. Say in six words why the doorbell kept its footage locally.
- Reminded them of: Cold Case (TV); New Tricks (TV); Unforgotten (TV); Department Q; The Caves of Steel (Asimov); The Postmortal (Drew Magary)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now states the rule in plain words before any date (bodies no longer age, memory holds seventy years); cut 'murder has no time limit', the 108-year figure and Varga's 2030 age (browser: arithmetic homework, fake novelty).
- Changed: Added the law and the stakes: courts refuse testimony older than seventy years, and a conviction is forty years confined, over half the memory a person keeps; the engine now ends in court and notes that no suspect ever dies, so no case closes itself (both critics: no consequence).
- Changed: Book 4 now says Okafor leads and Varga sits as a witness (cold reader: tainted procedure).
- Changed: Provenance is part of the engine (Pell must first prove the object is what it claims); the doorbell is bricked into the wall with its memory card (both critics: custody, why footage survived).
- Changed: Varga gets an obstacle and a private cost (Hale's file lost when Maintenance records came in; notebook entries she cannot believe); book 1 pays off for Ines's daughter instead of the budget; Hale's signature appears in book 2; Brandt now opposes reopening old griefs rather than wanting money.
- Declined: Naming who prosecutes a century-old homicide: a prosecutor would add a character the page has no room for; the court rule and sentence carry the point.
- Declined: A Hale trace in book 1 as well as book 2: one planted trace fits the length limit, and book 1 needed its line for the daughter.
- For the author to decide: The sentence is my invention: forty years confined, measured against a seventy-year memory. Confirm the figure and whether confinement suits the humane tone, or choose another penalty.
- For the author to decide: Brandt's motive is now principle (old griefs are better left shut) instead of budget. Confirm, since it makes her an opponent with an argument rather than an office obstacle.

**Gate (reader: Frank, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A gives the place rules a cop respects: courts refuse testimony older than seventy years, Pell must authenticate the object first, evidence names the killer in court, a forty-year sentence. ### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** A familiar cold-case-basement shape made new by the seventy-year memory rule and the dead-format archivist; the lead's missing-partner thread and the fog harbor are the borrowed parts. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 5, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: The memory rule with legal consequences: "courts refuse older testimony" and a sentence of forty years is "over half the memory a person keeps"; Midge Pell and the authentication step: "First Pell must prove the object is what it claims"; our own doorbell cameras and phones as archaeology; "no suspect ever dies, so no case closes itself" and "witnesses a century older than their alibis, met with patience, not contempt"
- Problem 1: The team frame is the most generic element: a basement cold-case unit with a dry veteran, a young sergeant, an archivist, a pathologist and a superintendent who wants files shut. Why it matters: This is the shape of Department Q, Cold Case, Unforgotten and New Tricks (from memory); shelf readers have met it most often, and here Brandt's objection is the stock one. Suggested fix: Give Brandt a rule-born motive: reopening forces people to learn deeds they no longer remember doing. Replace the stock pathologist with a role only this world has, e.g. a court memory-assessor who certifies the seventy-year line.
- Problem 2: The running thread, the partner who "walked out" and a detective whose "notebooks hold entries in her own hand she cannot believe", echoes Memento, Unforgotten's guilt-forgotten suspects and the amnesiac-detective line (Disco Elysium), all from memory. Why it matters: A missing partner plus self-doubting investigator is a familiar series spine; book 4's reveal "she sent Hale away" is guessable from the lead paragraph. Suggested fix: Make the thread a legal question only this world poses: can Varga answer for an act beyond her memory line? Let book 4 turn on her own notebooks being authenticated by Pell as evidence against her.
- Problem 3: The most distinctive element, Pell reviving dead formats, sits in the cast list as a support role, and three of four hooks are a camera or phone while one is a coat. Why it matters: The audit found no prior work with the dead-format archivist; a pitch should lead with what nobody else has, and vary it so it does not repeat. Suggested fix: Name the format in each book and make reviving it the middle act: a memory card needing a vanished reader, a thermal ferry ticket fading, a ledger in dead software, a locked phone. Consider Pell as co-lead beside Varga.
- Problem 4: "a harbor city of fog, ferries and brick row houses behind a sea wall" is stock noir weather, and "The Maintenance" is a generic capitalized-noun treatment, close to Ad Vitam's regeneration and Stableford's emortals. Why it matters: Nothing in the city itself shows the memory rule; the setting could host any moody procedural, which weakens the claim to difference. Suggested fix: Show a city built around forgetting: public memory registries, notaries who witness diaries, families with keepers of the retold stories, a trade in forged keepsakes. Name the treatment after what it costs, not what it does.
- Reminded them of: Ad Vitam (TV): very old police officer in a society without death; shared trope, different engine; Brian Stableford, Architects of Emortality: police whodunit among near-immortals; Keith Brooke and Eric Brown, Wormhole: future detective reopens an 80-year-old murder; Department Q / Cold Case / Unforgotten (from memory): basement cold-case unit, object or remains reopening an old death; Memento (from memory): investigator relying on own written notes he cannot trust; John Scalzi, Lock In (from memory): one speculative medical rule followed through a police procedural
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances drawn from the supplied audit plus memory; entries marked (from memory) are unverified. The resemblance is at trope level (cold-case unit, long-lived police), not premise plus setting plus device: none of the named works pairs a hard memory horizon with courts rejecting testimony and a dead-format archivist. Titles not checked.

**superfan-reader (Karen).** I'd borrow it for the evidence-only rule and the living, wrong witnesses, but the courtroom logic is unexplained and the lab solves the cases instead of Varga. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 7, character 6, setting 6, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: The one thing I have not met: 'Every witness is alive and misremembers', so 'courts refuse older testimony' and the Unit 'trusts paper, cloth, bone and dead devices'. A case that must turn on evidence.; 'First Pell must prove the object is what it claims'. Authentication as step one is real procedure and almost nobody writes it.; No drunk loner: Varga 'asks for the receipt before the story', and book 4 puts her in the witness chair with Okafor leading.
- Problem 1: The court side is waved through. 'The evidence names the killer in court', but the defendant cannot remember the act or help their own defense, and the exhibit sat in a wall or harbor mud for a century. Why it matters: I work in litigation. Competence, chain of custody and authentication are where these cases would be fought, and the pitch skips all three. Suggested fix: State Port Sable's rule in one line: what a century-old exhibit must meet to be admitted, and how a defendant with no memory is tried. Make each book's ending pass that test.
- Problem 2: Varga does not solve anything on this page. 'Pell restores the format, Roux reads the bone, and the evidence names the killer.' The lab does the work and witnesses are always wrong, so interviews carry no information. Why it matters: I want a smart investigator reasoning in front of me. If testimony is always worthless I will skim every interview by book 2. Suggested fix: Make the misremembering itself a clue: Varga reads how each retold story has drifted, and who benefited from the drift, then points Pell at what to test. Give her one deduction per book.
- Problem 3: Stock pieces pile up: basement cold-case unit, hoarding archivist, pathologist, foggy harbor, vanished partner, detective's first case as finale, and 'Superintendent Calla Brandt, who believes century-old griefs are better left shut'. Why it matters: The boss who wants it left alone is in every cold-case series I have read. She has no age, no argument, nothing to do. Suggested fix: Replace Brandt first, with a recurring defense advocate who argues that a person with no memory of the act is not the person who did it. A real opponent with a real point.
- Problem 4: The book 4 answer is printed in the pitch and signposted from the start: 'as she tells it', notebooks 'she cannot believe', then 'she sent Hale away'. And nothing presses on any case. Why it matters: If I can call the series answer from book 1, I am reading for confirmation. With no deadline there is little reason to turn pages fast. Suggested fix: Keep 'she sent him away' but add a second fact the phone reveals that changes one earlier verdict. Give each case a clock, such as a demolition date or a decaying format.
- Reminded them of: Department Q (Jussi Adler-Olsen); Unforgotten (TV); New Tricks (TV); Cold Case (TV); The Caves of Steel (Asimov); Memento (film)

**Rewrite: accepted (slight).**

- Changed: Replaced Superintendent Brandt with defense advocate Tobias Quill, 140, who argues a client with no memory of the act is not the one who did it; every case must now survive him in court (both critics: stock boss, court logic waved through).
- Changed: Moved Pell to the center: co-lead in the logline, first in the cast, and each book now names its dead format (unreadable memory card, fading thermal ticket, ledger in dead software, locked phone) with decay as the clock (auditor: distinctive element buried; superfan: no pressure).
- Changed: Gave Varga the deduction: she reads how each retold story has drifted and who gained, then tells Pell what to test, so interviews carry information (superfan: the lab solves the cases).
- Changed: Rebuilt the setting around forgetting (diary notaries, family story-keepers, forged keepsakes), renamed the treatment the Forfeit with its 2049 date, dropped fog, ferries and the basement, and added the admission rule that exhibits are read in open court (auditor: stock noir city; superfan: admissibility).
- Changed: Sharpened the running thread into a legal question (must Varga answer for an act beyond her memory), with her notebooks authenticated against her in book 4 and a second fact from the phone that adds a name to the book 2 Stroud verdict (both critics: guessable ending).
- Declined: Replacing pathologist Roux with a court memory-assessor: Roux who 'knew some victims alive' and the bone evidence were praised, and a new role would add a mechanism the page has no room for.
- Declined: Removing the vanished partner and the first-case finale: 'the detective's own memory is on trial' and Varga as witness are the praised reason to finish the set, so the thread was reframed instead of replaced.
- Declined: Restoring 'murder has no time limit' and the '27-year-old constable in 2030' line from the older version: cut for length, since book 4's title and the 2049 date already orient the reader.
- For the author to decide: Book 4 now adds a name to the Stroud verdict from book 2. Decide whether that second culprit is fairly clued in book 2 itself, so the amendment reads as earned and book 2 still closes completely.
- For the author to decide: Pell is now co-lead in the logline. Decide whether the books carry two points of view (Varga and Pell) or stay with Varga alone, since that changes how the authentication middle act is written.

**Gate (editor, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (slight). B's setting grows from the premise (diary notaries, forged keepsakes), Varga actually detects by reading story drift, and Quill's no-memory defense gives every trial an opponent and sharpens the book 4 question. A's fog-and-ferries city and obstructive superintendent are stock. What the losing version did better: Warmth and place: the Customs House basement, "the Unit's basement company", Okafor's arc ("stops being the baby"), and Hale's lost file. A also avoids B's book 4 "name missing from the Stroud verdict", which undercuts book 2's closure.
