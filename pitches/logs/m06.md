# m06: The Sound-Effects Girl

Revision log. Genre: Historical mystery. Target readers: Women and men 45–75.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Sound-Effects Girl. Cincinnati, 1947. Hazel Novak, 31, is the only woman sound-effects artist at the fictional radio station WQC, the Voice of the Queen City: by day she makes the doors, teacups and footsteps of the soap operas, by night the gunshots and stabbed cabbages of the thrillers. Her ear is her trade: she can tell a .38 from a backfire, crepe soles from leather, linoleum from pine. Radio is changing under her feet. Magnetic tape has just arrived from Germany, and for the first time a voice can be cut, moved and played back as if it were live, so a broadcast is no longer proof that anyone was anywhere. Each case turns on something heard: a death during a live show, a ransom call with the wrong streetcar bell behind it, a recording with one splice too many. The reader gets every sound she gets. Cast ideas: the station's chief engineer, who distrusts tape and loves it; her uncle, a police sergeant who half-listens to her; a soap company's sponsor man; a script girl who knows every actor's lies. Thread: the station's very first reel of tape, recorded by accident during a rehearsal, holds forty seconds of two people planning something; Hazel knows one voice and spends four books finding the other. Titles could be sounds (The Second Door, Footsteps on Gravel, One Splice Too Many).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Cheryl Honigford, The Darkness Knows and two sequels (Chicago, 1938, radio-station murders, the lead is a radio actress); the film Radioland Murders (Chicago, 1939, killings during a live broadcast); Jean Sheldon, She Overheard Murder (a postwar Chicago radio actress). No sound-effects sleuth found. The seed has already been moved from Chicago in 1938 to Cincinnati in 1947 and the arrival of tape.

**First draft:** “The Sound-Effects Girl”, 514 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Diane).** A smart, well-researched premise with real atmosphere and honest puzzles; I would borrow book one, though Hazel herself is still more skill set than person. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 6.

- Keep: The central idea: 'a voice on the air stops proving anyone was there' — tape arriving in 1947 as the death of the live-broadcast alibi; Concrete sound clues per book: 'the air check holds two door slams and Hazel made one'; 'the operator heard gravel and his yard is brick'; Studio-floor texture and Hazel's position: 'a gravel box and a wheeled door frame', 'got the sound table in 1942 when the men left and has not given it back'
- Problem 1: Hazel is a job and an ear, not yet a person. 'She talks dry and exact' is told, not shown; I get no home, no history, no opinion about anything but sounds. Why it matters: I follow a sleuth across four books for her point of view on the world, not for her skill at telling linoleum from pine. Suggested fix: Give one line of Hazel's actual voice and one private fact: where she lives, what the war cost her, what she thinks of the soap serials she makes noises for.
- Problem 2: The running thread is hard to parse. 'Pruitt plays her reel one' and 'settling a price with a voice too far from the microphone to name' took me two readings to untangle. Why it matters: This is the reason to read all four, and I nearly missed that her teacher's death is the series mystery. Suggested fix: State it plainly up front: Hazel's teacher Gus died of a 'fall'; an accidental rehearsal tape caught him bargaining with someone unidentified three weeks earlier. Then let each book add one fact.
- Problem 3: The thread seems to sit still in books 2 and 3. Only 'her uncle starts listening' and 'night shows go to tape' move; nothing about Gus or reel one advances. Why it matters: As a binge reader I want each book to hand me a new piece, or the thread feels bolted on at the ends. Suggested fix: Add to each of books 2 and 3 one concrete discovery about reel one: a background sound placed, a date fixed, a suspect ruled out.
- Problem 4: The supporting cast is a list of ages and one trait apiece. Voss 'reads every script' and Dot 'tracks every actor's lies' are functions; the uncle 'half-listens' and nothing more. Why it matters: A station is a workplace full of grudges and loyalties; that human friction is half of what I read period mysteries for. Suggested fix: Say what each wants from Hazel or fears from her: Voss can pull the sponsorship, Pruitt's machine may put her out of work, the uncle got her the job or opposed it.
- Reminded them of: Remember WENN (TV series); Radio Days; Maisie Dobbs series (Jacqueline Winspear); Blow Out / The Conversation (sound as evidence); Max Allan Collins-style midcentury media mysteries; Phryne Fisher / Miss Fisher (period woman sleuth, lighter)

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A radio sound-effects woman solving crimes by ear in 1947 is a premise I would borrow, though the logline's tail and the reel-one thread made me work too hard. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: The title and 'solves each case by ear': I knew the job, the woman and the gimmick in three seconds.; The four case clues, each a single wrong sound: 'two door slams and Hazel made one', 'the operator heard gravel and his yard is brick'.; 'got the sound table in 1942 when the men left and has not given it back' plus 'a woman right more often than she is heard'.
- Problem 1: First ten seconds: my eye caught the title, 'Cincinnati, 1947' and 'solves each case by ear'. I nearly swiped at 'a voice on the air stops proving anyone was there', which I had to read twice. Why it matters: The logline's second half is abstract and tangled; late at night on a phone I do not reread, I move on. Suggested fix: End the logline on something concrete: 'in the year tape recording arrives and a murderer can be on the air live while standing somewhere else.'
- Problem 2: When I stopped I did not know what kind of crimes, or whether anyone dies. The first body is in the book list, far down the page. 'each case' is all the logline gives. Why it matters: I borrow mysteries for a murder and a puzzle; a page that hides the corpse reads like a radio history novel. Suggested fix: Put book one's case in the top lines: an actor dies mid-broadcast and the recording holds two door slams where Hazel made only one.
- Problem 3: The reel-one thread is hard to follow. 'Pruitt plays her reel one' is cryptic before the thread is explained, and 'settling a price with a voice too far from the microphone to name' took three readings. Why it matters: The running thread is my reason to read four books, and here it is the least clear part of the page. Suggested fix: Explain it before the book list in plain words: her teacher Gus was recorded by accident arguing over money with an unidentified man, three weeks before his fatal fall. Then name it reel one.
- Problem 4: Hazel is a skill set and a dry voice, but I get no home life, no loss, nothing personal beyond the job. Gus as 'Hazel's teacher' is stated, not felt. The cast are one-line functions. Why it matters: I stay four books for the people. Right now I admire her ear but do not yet care about her. Suggested fix: Give one personal line: who she lives with or lost, what Gus was to her, and one spoken example of the dry voice. Hint at warmth or friction with Dot or her uncle.
- Reminded them of: Maisie Dobbs (Jacqueline Winspear); Remember WENN; Radio Days; Phryne Fisher / Miss Fisher's Murder Mysteries; Berlin Alexanderplatz-era 'The Conversation' style splice plot (The Conversation, 1974); Agent Carter (the radio-show episodes)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now says 'solves murders by ear' and ends on a concrete image (a killer heard live while standing elsewhere), answering the browser's points about the abstract tail and the hidden corpse; book 2's bookkeeper is now dead so every book has a death.
- Changed: Running thread restated in plain words (Gus taped by accident arguing over money with an unidentified man three weeks before his fatal fall) and 'reel one' replaced by 'Gus's reel' throughout, answering both critics on the hard-to-parse thread.
- Changed: Gus introduced in the lead section as the man who taught Hazel her trade, so the book list reads clearly before the thread line.
- Changed: Books 2 and 3 each add one fact about the reel (freight-elevator hum placed; studio log dates it and rules Voss out), answering the cold reader's point that the thread sat still.
- Changed: Hazel gets a private fact (war widow) and a spoken line; each cast member now has a stake in her (Pruitt's machine could replace her, the uncle got her the job, Voss can cancel shows), answering both critics on character. Paid for by cutting 'linoleum from pine', 'her uncle starts listening' and 'night shows go to tape'.
- Declined: Putting book one's full case in the top lines: the logline cap is 45 words, so I put murder and the tape hook there and left the two door slams in the arc.
- Declined: Explaining the thread before the book list: the template fixes section order, so Gus is set up in the lead instead.
- Declined: Home life and Hazel's opinion of the soap serials: no room within the length cap beyond 'war widow' and one spoken line.
- For the author to decide: 'War widow' is my invention to give Hazel a private fact in two words; confirm it, or name a different loss or home detail.
- For the author to decide: Book 3 now rules Voss out of the reel by placing him in Chicago; say if you would rather keep him a live suspect until book 4 and clear someone else.

**Gate (reader: Diane, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A gives Hazel a voice ("That was a door. It wasn't mine"), a war widow's stake, and a cast who each threaten her. Its Gus thread advances with a concrete clue per book and promises an answer in book 4. B's cast is vague and its thread is static. What the losing version did better: "Radio's oldest alibi, being on the air, stopped holding" is the sharper line. Pruitt trusting the machine less than he loves it is a human touch, as is "linoleum from pine". "Forty seconds" of tape is nicely exact.
