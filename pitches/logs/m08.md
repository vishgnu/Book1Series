# m08: The Night Notary

Revision log. Genre: Urban amateur-sleuth mystery. Target readers: Women and men 40–65.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Witness My Hand. Gloria Ibarra, 47, a former superior-court clerk, works nights across Los Angeles County as a mobile notary: loan signings at kitchen tables, powers of attorney at hospital bedsides, affidavits in the county jail. She cannot give legal advice and she is not police. All the law asks of her is to check identity, watch the signature, take the thumbprint and write it in her journal, and to refuse if the signer does not understand or is being pushed. That refusal is her whole power. Each book starts with a signing that is wrong: a deed signed over at 2 a.m. by a man who is dead by morning, a jailhouse recantation, a dying widow's sudden marriage license. Her journal (names, times, thumbprints) is the evidence trail. Cast ideas: a night chaplain at the county hospital; a bail-bonds clerk who owes her favors; her 19-year-old nephew, who digitizes her journals; a sheriff's fraud detective who wishes she would go home; her father, whom she looks after by day. Thread: six years ago she notarized a forged signature that cost a family their house; the signing mill behind it is still working, and each case is a step closer to who runs it. Titles from notarial wording (Witness My Hand, Sworn Before Me, Under Penalty of Perjury, Personally Appeared).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> No novel or series with a notary sleuth was found in seven searches (moderate confidence). Stock notarial wording makes the titles hard to search.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Karen).** A paper-trail sleuth whose journal is the evidence is exactly my kind of mystery; I would borrow book 1 tonight, though Gloria herself is still mostly a resume. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: "Her only legal power is to say no and write it down" and the journal as the reader's clue sheet: clues "in ink where the reader can check it"; Gloria's background and voice: nineteen years a court clerk, "dry, exact, polite", sober, with a father to look after instead of a bottle; Book 4's hook: a deed with her seal "but has no line in her journal", leading to the supervisor who trained her
- Problem 1: The running thread ends "its clients still unnamed". After four books I am promised an answer, and that phrase reads like a hedge or a setup for book 5. Why it matters: I was told the old wrong is "answered in book four". A half-answer after four books is the kind of thing that makes me drop an author. Suggested fix: Cut "its clients still unnamed" or say plainly what is settled: who ran the mill, what happens to them, and whether the Batiste family gets the house or a judgment.
- Problem 2: How a journal line becomes a solved murder is thin. "Her time entry breaks an alibi" in book 1 is the whole investigation as pitched; a notary's journal proves presence and time, little else. Why it matters: I work with exhibits all day. One time entry does not carry 65,000 words, and I cannot tell what Gloria actually does between the signing and the answer. Suggested fix: Add one line on method: she compares entries across nights, pulls recorded deeds at the county recorder, matches thumbprints and ID numbers, and what she finds Tsai can use in court.
- Problem 3: Gloria is a job description and a caregiving duty. "She wants one six-year-old signing put right" is stated, but not why it cost her or whether she was at fault. Why it matters: I follow a series for the person. I need to know what she did wrong or was blamed for before I care about four books of setting it right. Suggested fix: Say in one sentence what happened: she notarized the impostor's deed, the Batistes lost the house, and what it cost her, whether guilt, a complaint against her commission, or her court job.
- Problem 4: The opening logline is clumsy: "may only check ID, watch the pen and write it down" made me reread to see that "may only" means legally limited to. Why it matters: It is the first thing I read, and the best idea on the page arrives clearer in the last paragraph than in the first. Suggested fix: Lead with the plain version from the end: no badge, no law license, no gun; she can refuse a signing and write it down, and in each book that record is the evidence.
- Reminded them of: Michael Connelly's The Lincoln Lawyer (LA legal work run from a car); Sue Grafton's Kinsey Millhone series (California, methodical record-keeping); Naomi Hirahara's Mas Arai mysteries (older LA civilian sleuth); Better Call Saul (elder-law and paper-fraud detail); Only Murders in the Building (civilians outworking a reluctant detective)

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A fresh, believable sleuth job with a clever clue device; the opening tells me the rules before the trouble, and Gloria herself stays a bit cool on the page. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: The job and its limit: "Her only legal power is to say no and write it down" and "No badge, no law license, no gun: a seal, a journal, an ink pad."; The journal as fair-play clue sheet: "The clues are entries the reader has already seen: a name, a time, a thumbprint."; Night Los Angeles in concrete rooms plus the book 1 case: "loan papers on a Pacoima kitchen table" and "A widower deeds away his house at 2 a.m. and dies by morning."
- Problem 1: First ten seconds: my eye caught the title and "mobile notary working nights". I nearly swiped at "may only check ID, watch the pen and write it down" — a job description, with no crime, death or danger yet. Why it matters: When I stopped I did not know anyone dies, what Gloria stands to lose, or why a notary would be solving anything. The best line is buried in book 1's entry. Suggested fix: Lead the logline with the book 1 image: a widower signs away his house at 2 a.m. and is dead by morning, and the only honest record of that night is Gloria's journal. Then state the limit.
- Problem 2: Gloria reads as a résumé: "clerked in superior court for nineteen years", "dry, exact, polite". The six-year-old signing that drives her is only explained at the bottom, and her own part in it is never stated. Why it matters: I binge a series for the person. I need to feel her guilt or anger about the Batiste house, not just be told she "wants one six-year-old signing put right". Suggested fix: In the lead paragraph say plainly what happened: she notarized an impostor's signature, a family lost their home, and she still keeps that journal page. Add one line showing her dry voice in action.
- Problem 3: The running thread's answer is flat and hedged: "the probate supervisor who trained her" appears nowhere in the cast, and "its clients still unnamed" sounds like the big question is left partly open. Why it matters: A villain I have never met is not a payoff, and the brief promised the thread is answered. The hedge makes me suspect a fifth-book tease. Suggested fix: Name the supervisor in the cast as a mentor Gloria still trusts and calls for advice in books 1–3. Cut "its clients still unnamed" or say clearly that the mill is shut down in book 4.
- Problem 4: Some shop talk blurs the stakes: "her commission and $15,000 bond are exposed", "signing mill", "Lonnie's ledger names the company paying her fee". Book 3's "issues" a marriage license also sounds off; I thought a clerk does that. Why it matters: On a phone I will not stop to decode terms, and this series sells itself on accuracy. One detail that smells wrong makes me doubt the rest. Suggested fix: Say it in plain words: if her record is wrong she loses her license and her living. Define the mill once as a company that manufactures signatures for house theft. Have the procedure in book 3 checked.
- Reminded them of: Michael Connelly's The Lincoln Lawyer (working LA law from a car); Naomi Hirahara's Mas Arai mysteries; Sue Grafton's Kinsey Millhone series; Richard Osman's Thursday Murder Club (older sleuths, same reader age); Better Call Saul (elder-law and paperwork fraud); Only Murders in the Building (civilian sleuths, city setting)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now opens on the book 1 image (widower signs at 2 a.m., dead by morning) and states her limit in plain words; answers both critics on the clumsy 'may only' opening and the missing crime.
- Changed: The lead now says what happened six years ago (she notarized an impostor's deed, the Batistes lost their house, she keeps the page) and adds one line of her voice; answers 'Gloria is a resume'.
- Changed: Helen Marsh, the supervisor who trained Gloria, is now in the cast as a trusted mentor, replacing nephew Mateo; answers 'a villain I have never met is not a payoff'.
- Changed: Running thread and book 4 now end plainly: the mill is shut down, Helen is charged, the Batistes win a judgment; 'its clients still unnamed' is cut and the mill is defined once in plain words.
- Changed: Engine gains one line of method (comparing entries across nights, pulling recorded deeds, handing Tsai court-ready proof) and drops the bond jargon; book 3 now specifies a confidential marriage license, which authorized California notaries may issue.
- Declined: Returning the Batiste house itself: a money judgment is more realistic six years after a recorded transfer, and accuracy is a genre promise.
- Declined: Replacing the limit line in 'What makes it different' with the logline version: both critics named that paragraph a strength, so it stays word for word.
- For the author to decide: Book 3 rests on Gloria being a notary approved by the county clerk to issue confidential marriage licenses (California Family Code); please confirm this and the bedside circumstances before drafting.
- For the author to decide: Mateo was cut from the cast to make room for Helen Marsh within the word limit. Do you want him kept as a minor character in the books, or restored to the pitch in place of Lonnie or Ruth?

**Gate (reader: Karen, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A opens on a dead widower and a 2 a.m. deed, gives Gloria a concrete old mistake (the Batiste page she keeps) and a line of real voice. Helen is planted in the cast, so book 4 plays fair. B's logline and backstory are vague. What the losing version did better: B does not name the book 4 culprit outright, which spares me the spoiler. The $15,000 bond is a precise, true stake. "One old wrong, answered in book four" is a clean reason to keep reading.
