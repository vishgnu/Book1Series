# s05: Off by One

Revision log. Genre: Cozy science fiction (space). Target readers: Women and men 30–60.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Off by One. In the 2260s the Outer Reaches hold a census every ten years, and the law says a person must be seen and asked; no sensor counts. Assembly seats and the air subsidy both follow the headcount. Enumeration District 114 is 2,300 habitats strung across forty astronomical units, and its senior enumerator is Nell Abara, 53: twenty-nine years in the service, unbribable, a knitter, widow of a pilot named Ade, flying the old patrol cutter Tally with a galley she is proud of. Her new apprentice, Davi, 22, has a statistics degree and thinks the whole thing could be done by satellite. The enumerator's oath: everyone counts, once. Each book is one habitat where the count is wrong. One heartbeat more than the register. Forty names drawing air who have not breathed in years. A community that refuses to be counted at all. A ship that swears it is a town. Finding out why means sitting in kitchens, reading ration books and noticing who sets an extra place. Small strange societies, each with its own good reasons. Thread: one name counted twice in the same week, in two habitats nine astronomical units apart, by two honest enumerators; Nell has one census and four books to find out how. In the background, the Reaches keep their Assembly seats only if the total clears a threshold. Kind, curious, funny; nobody is a villain for being odd.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> No space-census series found. Census-taker leads elsewhere: Clifford Simak, Census (1944); Frederik Pohl, The Census Takers (1956, the census as a cull); China Miéville, This Census-Taker; Jesse Ball, Census (a traveling census taker and his son); the Doctor Who audio Order of the Daleks (a Galactic Census assessor with a probationary assessor). Keep the stakes mechanical and the tone comic, away from Ball's elegiac register.

**First draft:** “Off by One”, 509 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Aisha).** A census taker whose only power is to knock and ask is an idea I have not met; I would borrow book 1, though the twin reveal worries me. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 7, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: The premise and its rule: "no sensor, satellite or proxy counts. A person must be seen and asked" with stakes of "air and votes, never murder"; "Every society has a sound reason for its wrong number" — each case is a small anthropology, especially Saint Brine's forty paperless ice-cutters and Quiet Harbor's debtors who refuse any ledger; Nell at 53: "twenty-nine years unbribed", "the galley her late husband Ade, a pilot, fitted for two", noticing "the ration book in new handwriting"
- Problem 1: The running thread resolves as "twin sisters sharing one register line". That is the first guess any reader makes about one person seen twice, and the pitch gives it away. Why it matters: Four books of waiting for the oldest answer in mystery fiction; a series promising a puzzle that "plays fair" should not end on one I solved on the series page. Suggested fix: Keep Junia Vasko seen twice, but make the answer a social fact of the Reaches: why a family or habitat needs one line for two people, and what Nell's oath costs them. Do not state it in the pitch.
- Problem 2: The rule that carries everything, why "no sensor, satellite or proxy counts", is never explained. Davi's objection that "a satellite could finish by lunch" is reasonable and goes unanswered. Why it matters: I work with measurement; a rule with no reason reads as author convenience, and Davi becomes a strawman set up to be taught a lesson. Suggested fix: Add one sentence of history: a past census falsified by sensor data or proxies, habitats erased or invented, so that being seen and asked is a hard-won right rather than a quaint custom.
- Problem 3: Books 1 and 2 share a shape: hidden people, kind enumerator registers them, all is well. "Every case ends with the count made right" promises that Nell is never wrong and counting never harms. Why it matters: Being counted is not always a kindness. If the oath is never tested, the warmth turns sugary and I stop believing these people by book 2. Suggested fix: Let one case cost Nell something: the correct count drops a habitat's subsidy or the Reaches below four million, and she must choose. Book 3's refusers are the place to make her doubt the ledger itself.
- Problem 4: The supporting cast are job titles with one adjective: "the honest enumerator next district over", "mail-barge skipper and gossip". Imogen Park has a budget but no want. Davi's arc seems finished in book 1. Why it matters: I come back for people. Only Nell is one yet, and an apprentice who learns his lesson in the first book has nowhere to go for three more. Suggested fix: Give each a want that rubs against Nell's: Park needs the four million whatever the truth, Tobias envies her record, Davi keeps being partly right about data through book 3.
- Reminded them of: Becky Chambers, Monk and Robot / Wayfarers; Ursula K. Le Guin's Hainish envoy stories; The No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency; Malka Older, The Mimicking of Known Successes; Citizen Sleeper (game); Call the Midwife

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A census taker with a kettle and a right to knock is exactly my shelf; the cases sound warm, but the pitch leads with numbers before it leads with Nell. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: "The detective is a census taker. Her only power is the right to knock and ask" plus the oath "everyone counts, once"; Book 1's case: "registers 212 and Nell counts 213", a hidden newborn and an air fine. Small, humane, instantly clear; Nell's details: "the galley her late husband Ade, a pilot, fitted for two", "asks twice, and knits while she waits", "sixty people and one kettle"
- Problem 1: First ten seconds: my eye caught "census enumerator" and "2,300 habitats". I nearly swiped at "every person must be seen and asked" because it reads like a regulation. The warmth arrives much later. Why it matters: At ten seconds I did not know it was cozy, that there was a kettle, or that anyone was likeable. Census sounds like paperwork. Suggested fix: Open the logline with the book 1 image and Nell herself: the register says 212, she counts 213, and she puts the kettle on to find out who the extra heartbeat is.
- Problem 2: The pitch gives away every solution: the hidden newborn, the forty ice-cutters, and the twin sisters behind the running thread. Why it matters: I was promised "a puzzle that plays fair" and then handed the answers. Twins is also the first guess anyone would make. Suggested fix: State each book's wrong number and what is at risk, not the answer. Keep the Junia Vasko question open on the page, and consider a less guessable resolution than twins.
- Problem 3: Number overload: 2260s, 53, 2,300, forty astronomical units, sixty, four million, twenty-nine years, 212, 213, forty, ninety, 611, nine astronomical units. Why it matters: On a phone late at night the figures blur together, and the ones that matter (212 versus 213) lose their punch. Suggested fix: Keep the case numbers and "2,300 airlocks to knock on". Cut or soften the rest: drop the astronomical units, the four-million threshold detail and most ages from the top half.
- Problem 4: Nell's want is abstract ("one clean census") and nothing personal is at risk for her across four books. Book 3 is solved by Davi and book 4 ends on a "ruling". Why it matters: I come back for a person, not a headcount. I want to know what Nell gets or loses besides a tidy ledger. Suggested fix: Give Nell a personal line through the series: her last census before retirement, or whether the Tally stays a galley for one. Let book 4 answer that alongside the seats.
- Reminded them of: Becky Chambers, Wayfarers and Monk & Robot; The No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency; Murder, She Wrote (without the murder); Victoria Goddard, The Hands of the Emperor; Citizen Sleeper

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now opens on 212 versus 213, Nell and the kettle instead of the regulation and the habitat count (ten-second-browser: warmth arrived too late).
- Changed: Running thread no longer resolves as twins and the answer is withheld: it is now a custom of the Reaches that tests Nell's oath; book 2's solution is also withheld (both critics: guessable, given away).
- Changed: Added one clause of history for the seen-and-asked rule, a census run from orbit that cut a living town's air (cold-reader: rule read as author convenience).
- Changed: Counting now costs: the engine admits a right count can hurt, book 3 makes Nell doubt her oath, and book 4's honest ruling may lose the Reaches their seats (cold-reader: sugary, oath never tested).
- Changed: Nell gets a personal line (last census, whether the Tally stays a galley for one) and Park, Tobias and Davi get wants that rub against hers; cut the 2260s, district number, astronomical units, the four-million figure and two ages (ten-second-browser: number overload, abstract want).
- Declined: Hiding book 1's hidden newborn: both critics named that case as a strength, and it is the hook the logline now leads with.
- Declined: Taking book 3's solution away from Davi: the cold reader asked that he stay partly right about data through book 3, so he finds the lawful way while Nell carries the doubt.
- For the author to decide: The Junia Vasko answer is now only described as 'a custom of the Reaches' and no longer twins. You need to choose the actual custom (for example, a shared register line that a family or habitat passes between two people) before book 1 is outlined, so the clues can be planted fairly.
- For the author to decide: Book 4 now implies Nell retires or chooses a home. Decide whether she stays aboard the Tally, since that determines how easily the series continues past four books.

**Gate (reader: Aisha, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). B opens on the case itself (212 vs 213, kettle on), gives the seen-and-asked rule a believable cause, and puts real cost on honesty: Park needs a high count, Nell doubts her oath, the ruling may lose seats. People have friction; A's are flat and its cases pre-solved. What the losing version did better: A's four-million seat threshold makes the book 4 stakes concrete, and "forty astronomical units" gives scale. A also names the twin-sister answer, which plays fair, though withholding it as B does makes me want book 4 more.
