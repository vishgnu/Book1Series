# s10: In Kind

Revision log. Genre: First-contact science fiction. Target readers: Women and men 30–65.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: In Kind. The Senders have never landed and never spoken. Every four hundred days a sealed capsule comes down beside a decommissioned weather station on a fictional sub-Antarctic island, carrying one object; the Exchange has forty days to put one object in the capsule and send it back. Nobody knows the language, only that replies have consequences. After a seed sample went up, the next gift was a living flower extinct since 1912. After a flag, nothing came for three cycles. Lead: Tobias Eze, 58, Senior Assessor, thirty years a customs appraiser and museum registrar: he reads objects (wear, repair, provenance, intent) the way a detective reads a room. With him: Dr. Saoirse Lim, 31, who believes in grammar; a station chief counting the days to her pension; a military liaison who wants the reply to be a warning; the cook. Each book is one cycle: one gift to understand (a clay cup bearing a nine-thousand-year-old human thumbprint; a clock counting down to an unknown date; a child's shoe, never worn; a box that is empty and too heavy) and one human scheme to steal, fake or hijack the reply. The gift's meaning and the right reply are settled within the book. Thread: the four gifts are one sentence, and the last reply is the answer to it.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Andrew Lane and Nigel Foster, Netherspace (aliens nobody can talk to; object-for-object trades with no visible logic); Clifford Simak, The Visitors (1980; inscrutable visitors who pay in gifts). No timed single-object exchange or appraiser lead found. The aliens have already been renamed. Keep one object, forty days and the reading of provenance central.

**First draft:** “In Kind”, 514 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Aisha).** Contact by exchanged objects, read by a registrar, is an idea I haven't met; I'd borrow book 1, but the repeated sabotage plot and tidy final sentence worry me. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 6, character 6, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: Contact through objects with consequences: 'A seed sample brought a living flower extinct since 1912. A flag brought three cycles of silence.'; Tobias as a 58-year-old registrar who 'reads wear, repair, provenance and intent' and 'countersigned the flag' -- competence plus a real mistake, not a lone genius; The gifts themselves, especially the nine-thousand-year-old human thumbprint and the box 'too heavy by exactly the mass of Earth's last three replies'
- Problem 1: The running thread is spelled out as a neat English sentence ('we kept this, the keeping has a term...') and 'the aliens prove to be keepers, like him.' Why it matters: That makes them museum curators with human motives. The strangeness I came for drains out, and the pitch spoils its own puzzle. Suggested fix: Leave the sentence partly unreadable on the page: Tobias's reading is the best fit to evidence, with one clause that does not resolve. Cut 'like him'.
- Problem 2: Every book has the same human plot: 'Someone tries to steal, fake or hijack the reply; Tobias stops it with evidence.' Replica swap, shell casing, planted hoax, order to cut. Why it matters: By book 3 I will see the saboteur coming. The object puzzle is fresh each time; the opposition is not. Suggested fix: Vary the pressure: one book where the staff honestly disagree on the reading, one where Tobias's own first reading is wrong and Saoirse's evidence corrects him.
- Problem 3: The cast is a set of job titles. Saoirse is 'certain the gifts have grammar', Pruitt 'wants the reply to be a warning', Ingrid is 'fourteen months from pension'. Tobias gets a lost marriage and nothing else. Why it matters: I want people I believe. Pruitt reads as the stock hawk colonel, and Saoirse as the assistant who is there to be told. Suggested fix: Give Saoirse a reading that beats Tobias's at least once, and Pruitt a reason a sensible person would share. Say what Tobias risks personally beyond regret over the flag.
- Problem 4: Nothing says how a nine-thousand-year-old human thumbprint came to be with the senders, or what the forty-day tests actually look like. 'Reasoning the reader can follow' is promised, not shown. Why it matters: I work in a lab. If the dating and provenance are hand-waved, I stop trusting the puzzle, and the thumbprint is the biggest claim on the page. Suggested fix: Add one concrete line of method for book 1: what Tobias measures, what it rules out, and that the question of how they got the cup is live in the series.
- Reminded them of: Arrival / Story of Your Life (Ted Chiang); Roadside Picnic (Strugatsky); Rendezvous with Rama; Contact (Sagan); The Southern Reach trilogy; The Dig (isolated-site expert procedural)

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** Familiar inscrutable-gift contact made fresh by the appraiser's craft and the forty-day single-object rule; the cast and the remote station are the stock parts. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: Provenance as the method: "catches a replica swap by a four-gram difference" and "He sounds like a condition report"; The rule and its price: one object in, one back within forty days; "A flag brought three cycles of silence"; "An empty box is too heavy by exactly the mass of Earth's last three replies"; "Tobias fills it"
- Problem 1: Most generic element: the contact-team triangle. A young linguist "whose readings sometimes beat his" plus Colonel Pruitt, the liaison who wants a warning sent, is the Arrival / Story of Your Life line-up (from memory). Why it matters: Linguist-versus-colonel is the pairing this shelf has met most often, and a linguist has no language to work on in a series with "no translated speech". Suggested fix: Make Saoirse a textile and objects conservator or forensic materials analyst, which the stitch evidence already implies. Replace Pruitt with a treasury or insurance indemnity officer who must price each reply and can withhold the object.
- Problem 2: "The Counting Clock" is the stock piece among the four gifts: an alien countdown read as threat or promise recalls The Three-Body Problem's countdown and Contact's decoded machine message (both from memory). Why it matters: It is the only gift whose puzzle is a signal to decode rather than wear and making to read, so the appraiser's craft goes idle for one book. Suggested fix: Swap it for an object that carries time physically: a half-burned candle or a tally stick with notches cut by two different hands, the date derived from burn rate or tool marks.
- Problem 3: Most distinctive element, the registrar's reading of "wear, repair, provenance and intent", sits in the lead paragraph, while the logline sells capsule, island and deadline, which resemble Netherspace's trades and Roadside Picnic's unexplained artefacts (from memory). Why it matters: On the logline alone a browser files this as another inscrutable-alien-gift story and never meets the piece no comparable title has. Suggested fix: Put the method in the logline: "A retired customs appraiser has forty days to read one alien gift the way he reads a forgery, by its wear, weight and repairs, and choose what Earth sends back."
- Problem 4: Small staff in huts on a polar-adjacent island with seals and "constant wind" is a familiar isolated-station frame (The Thing, The Terror, Annihilation's Southern Reach, from memory), and the island does not affect the puzzles. Why it matters: The setting reads as borrowed atmosphere; only Hana's "pots from island clay" ties place to the object vocabulary, and she is a one-line extra. Suggested fix: Make the island the supply of replies: a rule that what goes back must be made or mended on Thrale. That puts Hana's kiln and clay at the center and explains the much-mended bowl.
- Reminded them of: Netherspace (Lane and Foster): object-for-object trades with untalkable aliens; The Visitors (Simak): inscrutable visitors who pay in gifts; Arrival / Story of Your Life: linguist versus military over an alien gift (from memory); Roadside Picnic: unexplained alien artefacts studied by an institute (from memory); Rendezvous with Rama: aliens who never explain themselves (from memory); The Three-Body Problem: unexplained countdown (from memory)

### Round 3: Series engine

**series-editor.** A tight, repeatable object-puzzle engine with a real stake and a strong book 4; book 2 is thin and the running answer is promised but never shown. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 7, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 7.

Why it matters: A second book that only repeats the pattern is where binge readers stop; it also leaves Tobias passive in his own series. Why it matters: I cannot judge whether book 4 pays off four books, or whether each book really 'adds one piece', without seeing the pieces. Why it matters: Without carried consequences this is four cases, not an arc; the box's weight is the only link between books. Why it matters: No-cliffhanger series still need a visible final result and a credible book 5; this pitch shows neither.
