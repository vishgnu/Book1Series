# m10: The Camas Fork Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Western mystery. Target readers: Men and women 45–70.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: First in Time. Marty Voss, 54, quit the sheriff's office after a shooting and took the job nobody wants: ditch rider for the Camas Fork Canal Company on Idaho's Snake River Plain (potatoes, sugar beets, dairies, lava rock). From April to October she drives 62 miles of canal with a shovel, a measuring stick and keys to three hundred headgates, deciding who gets how much water under a law older than the state: first in time, first in right. Water theft is the valley's oldest sin, and a ditch rider sees every field, pond and back gate; she has no badge, only the right to shut your water off. Each book starts with water that is not where it should be (a headgate chained open in a drought, a measuring flume shaved by half an inch, a pond that fills at night) and ends in a death, and in who needed the water and why. Cast ideas: the young sheriff who used to be her trainee; the state watermaster; a third-generation potato grower; a hydrologist newcomer the old families distrust; a water lawyer. Four books, one water year: spring turn-on, the July call on the river, harvest, winter in water court. Thread: the 1911 decree that ranks every right in the valley rests on a ledger no one has seen since. Titles from water law (First in Time, Call on the River, Beneficial Use, Use It or Lose It).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Cindi Myers, Conspiracy in the Rockies (Colorado, a rancher dead in an irrigation ditch, a deputy who enforces water rights; romantic suspense); Judith Van Gieson, Ditch Rider (Albuquerque ditches, a lawyer sleuth); Patricia McLinn, Last Ditch (a Wyoming irrigation dispute). No ditch-rider sleuth found. The seed has already been moved from Colorado to Idaho and no longer opens on a body in the ditch. Avoid 'Ditch Rider' and 'Last Ditch' as titles.

**First draft:** “The Camas Fork Mysteries”, 516 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Bill).** A ditch rider with a padlock is the right detective for this country and I'd borrow book 1, but the water law has soft spots I'd catch. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 6, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: The ditch rider as detective: "no badge, only the right to lock a headgate" and "states the measurement before the opinion"; The small true tampering details: "a headgate chained open, a flume shaved half an inch, a pond that fills at night"; Titles straight out of water law (First in Time, Call on the River, Beneficial Use, Use It or Lose It) and the well-data fight with the old families
- Problem 1: It mixes canal company shares with priority rights. A "shareholder" on one canal takes a pro-rata cut in a short year; priority runs between diversions on the river, not between neighbors on Marty's ditch. Why it matters: "Every suspect's place in line was fixed in 1911" is wrong if they all hold shares in the same canal. That is the false note that stops me. Suggested fix: Decide which it is: several ditches and private rights off the Camas Fork with separate priority dates, or one company where the fight is over shares, rotation and theft. Keep the watermaster's calls at the river.
- Problem 2: A 1911 decree as the last word ignores the Snake River Basin Adjudication, which re-decreed every right on the plain by 2014. And Idaho watermasters are elected by a district's users, not a "state watermaster". Why it matters: Anybody who sat through the adjudication knows a lost surveyor's ledger could not quietly reorder 214 rights today. The running thread rests on it. Suggested fix: Make the ledger evidence in a challenge to how the adjudication carried the 1911 dates forward, or set the valley on a tributary with its own unsettled decree. Call Hinckley the district watermaster.
- Problem 3: Four deaths over water in one county in one year, and one rider covering "62 miles of canal" and "three hundred headgates" alone. Both are stretched. Why it matters: Water fights are mostly shovels, lawyers and grudges that last forty years. Too many bodies in one season and it stops feeling like a real valley. Suggested fix: Spread the books over two or three irrigation seasons, or make one death old or doubtful. Give Marty a section of a larger system, with other riders named.
- Problem 4: Marty's past is the usual one: "a shooting the review board cleared and she has not". "It costs her neighbors" is unclear, and I don't know whether she farms or has family. Why it matters: I've met that haunted ex-deputy in a dozen series. What I'd follow is her standing in the valley, and the pitch doesn't tell me. Suggested fix: Say plainly what locking a gate costs her: whose crop she shorted and who quit speaking to her at the co-op. Give her ground, stock or kin tied to a junior right.
- Reminded them of: Joe Pickett novels (C.J. Box); Longmire (Craig Johnson); Anna Pigeon mysteries (Nevada Barr); The Milagro Beanfield War; Chinatown; Leaphorn and Chee (Tony Hillerman)

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A ditch rider with a padlock and a dead body below a chained headgate is a fresh, credible western mystery; the lead's heart is thinner than her job. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 9, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: "water turns up where it should not be, and someone dies over it" - the line my eye caught in the first ten seconds, and the rest of the page delivers on it; The job itself: "keys to three hundred headgates", "whose only power is a padlock", "reads a wet bank like a crime scene"; One valley through a year with plain rules I can follow: "first in time, first in right", nine inches of rain, a case closed per book, no cliffhangers
- Problem 1: In ten seconds I had "Marty Voss, 54, a former deputy" and pictured a man. "She" arrives three paragraphs down. I also did not know what a ditch rider does. Why it matters: I swipe on a guess. A woman of 54 riding canal is a selling point for half this readership, and the logline hides it. Suggested fix: Put "she" in the logline and gloss the job in six words: "the woman who opens and locks every farmer's water gate along 62 miles of canal."
- Problem 2: Marty is a resume and a method. "A shooting the review board cleared and she has not" is the standard ex-cop wound, and nothing says who she lives with, loves, or stands to lose. Why it matters: I binge four books for the person, not the canal. Longmire and Pickett have family and home; she has a measuring habit. Suggested fix: Add one concrete personal tie and one stake in the valley: a sister farming junior ground, a grown son, a house on the canal. Say who was shot and whether that family still lives there.
- Problem 3: "It costs her neighbors: no badge, only the right to lock a headgate" reads garbled. I read it twice and still am not sure whether she loses neighbors or her neighbors pay. Why it matters: It is the one sentence meant to show what the job costs her, and it is where I would have swiped away in the lead paragraph. Suggested fix: Say it plainly: "She has no badge now, only the right to lock a headgate, and every gate she locks belongs to someone she has known thirty years."
- Problem 4: The book lines and running thread lean on unexplained trade terms: "a call dries up junior fields", "forfeiture fight", "decree ranks 214 valley rights". The ledger thread sounds like a records dispute, not a danger. Why it matters: I like learning the water rules, but I need to feel who gets ruined if the ledger says otherwise, or book 4 sounds like a hearing. Suggested fix: Tie the ledger to people already named: if it turns up, Ruth Aldecoa's family right may drop behind a neighbor's. Swap one term per book line for plain words, e.g. "the state shuts off the newer farms."
- Reminded them of: Longmire (Craig Johnson); Joe Pickett (C.J. Box); Anna Pigeon (Nevada Barr); The Milagro Beanfield War; Chinatown; Yellowstone

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now shows the lead is a woman and says what a ditch rider does in plain words (ten-second-browser, problem 1).
- Changed: The valley is now fourteen ditches off one river, each with its own priority date, so the 1911 line runs between ditches and not between shareholders on one canal; Hinckley is the district watermaster (cold-reader, problems 1 and 2).
- Changed: The garbled cost sentence is replaced with the plain version, and Marty gets a stake: the shot man's family still farms on her section and her brother Gene farms under the newest ditch (both critics on the thin lead).
- Changed: The ledger thread now names who is ruined: Ruth Aldecoa's ditch or Gene Voss's came first. Book lines 2 and 4 swap 'call', 'junior' and 'forfeiture' for plain words (ten-second-browser, problem 4).
- Changed: Marty's beat is cut from 62 miles and three hundred headgates to her own 24-mile section, and book 3's death is now old bones from the 2001 drought, so the year has three fresh deaths (cold-reader, problem 3).
- Declined: Spreading the books over two or three irrigation seasons: one valley through a single year is a strength the other critic named.
- Declined: Replacing the shooting backstory outright: kept it but tied it to a family still on her section, since the fix asked for was her standing in the valley and there was no room for a new history.

**Gate (reader: Bill, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A gets the water right: priority runs between fourteen ditches on a river, not inside one canal. 24 miles is a believable ride; 62 miles and 300 gates is not. The brother on the newest ditch against Ruth on the oldest, and the dead man's family on her section, make it personal. What the losing version did better: "Snake River Plain" places it quicker. "Shareholder" and "a call dries up junior fields" are the words we actually use. "Every suspect's place in line was fixed in 1911" is a sharper closing line than "every ditch's".

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** A fresh job and a real legal engine carry it; the lead's backstory and the sheriff pairing are stock, and the padlock, the best thing here, is underused. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 7, engine 8, character 5, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 8.

- Keep: "The detective is a ditch rider whose only power is a padlock on a headgate": the most distinctive element; no close match in the audit or in my memory.; Prior appropriation as plot machine: "first in time, first in right", the 1911 decree and the 214 ranked rights give motive, clue and running thread in one.; Each book opening on "water that is not where the delivery sheet says" (chained headgate, shaved flume, pond filling at night) with evidence in cubic feet per second.
- Problem 1: Most generic element: "a sheriff's deputy for 22 years, quit after a shooting the review board cleared and she has not". The ex-cop haunted by a cleared shooting is the shelf's most worn backstory. Why it matters: It makes Marty resemble Longmire, Joe Pickett and every haunted ex-lawman (from memory), and it has nothing to do with water, so it dilutes the series' one fresh idea. Suggested fix: Make the wound a water one: in the 2001 drought, as a deputy, she enforced the shutoff that broke a neighbour's farm. Keep "the man's family still farms on her section"; it also ties her to book 3's bones.
- Problem 2: "Sheriff Caleb Ortega, 36, her former trainee, who needs her eye and resents it" and "put Marty back beside the sheriff" turn the ditch rider into a standard unofficial police consultant. Why it matters: Retired cop helping a younger sheriff is the default shape of rural mystery; once she works beside a badge, the padlock stops mattering. Suggested fix: Keep Ortega but put them at odds over jurisdiction: he works the death, she works the water, and each book's break comes from something only the ditch rider can lawfully do, such as locking, measuring or cutting off a gate.
- Problem 3: The padlock is named in "What makes it different" but never acts in any of the four book lines; no case turns on Marty locking, refusing to lock, or finding her own lock cut. Why it matters: The signature device sits at the edge of the pitch as a slogan. Readers remember a device they see used, not one they are told about. Suggested fix: Give each book a lock beat in its one-line summary: book 1 the chain is on her own gate; book 2 she must lock out her brother Gene; book 4 her lock records become the evidence that dates the ledger.
- Problem 4: Book openers lean on the familiar: "a 71-year-old farmer drowned below it" still sits close to the audit's rancher dead in a ditch, and "a lawyer lies dead in the dry canal" repeats the body-in-the-ditch image. Why it matters: The audit flagged body-in-ditch as the point of overlap with Conspiracy in the Rockies; two of four books still stage it, and the valley-water-conspiracy shape recalls Chinatown (from memory). Suggested fix: Move both deaths off the canal: book 1's farmer dies in his potato cellar while his gate runs open miles away; book 4's lawyer dies at the county records vault. Let the water be the clue, not the scene.
- Reminded them of: Cindi Myers, Conspiracy in the Rockies (shared trope: water-rights death; from the audit); Judith Van Gieson, Ditch Rider (title and ditch milieu only; from the audit); Patricia McLinn, Last Ditch (irrigation dispute; from the audit); C.J. Box, Joe Pickett series (low-authority rural official as sleuth; from memory); Craig Johnson, Longmire series (haunted lawman, plainspoken voice; from memory); Chinatown (water diverted at night, land and rights fraud; from memory)
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances beyond the three audit titles are from memory and unverified. None shares premise plus setting plus signature device; overlap is at trope level. Book titles are legal terms of art and likely reused elsewhere; check 'First in Time' and 'Beneficial Use' in the later round.

**superfan-reader (Frank).** The ditch rider with a padlock and a priority list is new to me and I'd borrow it; the haunted ex-deputy and resentful young sheriff I've met a hundred times. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 6, setting 9, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: "The detective is a ditch rider whose only power is a padlock on a headgate" - a real job with real, limited authority; that is the one thing I have not seen.; "First in time, first in right" and the 1911 decree ranking 214 rights: a setting with its own rules that generate motive every dry summer.; "States the measurement before the opinion" and readers seeing "each gauge reading when Marty does" - that is how a good investigator talks, and it is fair play.
- Problem 1: Marty's backstory is stock: ex-cop who "quit after a shooting the review board cleared and she has not." Every third series lead on my shelf carries that same shooting. Why it matters: It's the first thing I'd skim. The job is fresh; the wound is off the rack, and it makes her sound like everyone else. Suggested fix: Replace it first. Have her leave over something water-shaped: she served a shutoff or eviction order on a neighbor in a drought year, lawfully, and it ruined him. Keep the family on her section.
- Problem 2: No word on who has jurisdiction. A ditch rider finds a body; then what? Sheriff, coroner, state water department, the watermaster's authority versus hers are never sorted out. "Back beside the sheriff" is hand-waving. Why it matters: A civilian working a death scene beside a sheriff is where I snort. Chain of custody and her standing need to hold up. Suggested fix: State her lane: she documents water, signs delivery sheets and measurement logs, testifies as a witness; Ortega works the death. Let her paperwork be the evidence, and say whether he deputizes or contracts her.
- Problem 3: "Someone dies over it" in every book, four deaths in one year in one small county, plus old bones. Sheriff Ortega "needs her eye and resents it" is the stock former-trainee friction. Why it matters: A county that size sees a homicide every few years. Four in twelve months strains belief and the resentful protege is tired. Suggested fix: Make one book's death an accident or old crime someone is covering, and one a theft or fraud case that turns lethal late. Give Ortega a specific grievance: she left him holding the shooting file.
- Problem 4: The running thread turns on a missing ledger found in book 4 and "entered as evidence." A lost document surfacing is an old device, and a 115-year-old ledger overturning an adjudicated decree is a legal stretch. Why it matters: Decrees are final for a reason. If the law is wrong here, the readers who know water will quit, and I'd doubt the rest. Suggested fix: Plant pieces of the ledger in books 1-3 so its finding is earned, and say what it legally can do: support a fraud claim or reopen one disputed priority date, not rerank the whole river.
- Reminded them of: Craig Johnson's Longmire (young-old lawman friction, land and weather); C.J. Box's Joe Pickett (low-authority official who sees every back gate); Tony Hillerman's Leaphorn and Chee (setting with its own rules); Nevada Barr's Anna Pigeon (working job as the detective's access); Chinatown (murder over water rights and a dry-year diversion); The Milagro Beanfield War (ditch priority as the whole fight)
- Search note: No search done; comparisons are from memory of the shelf.

**Rewrite: accepted (slight).**

- Changed: Backstory: the cleared shooting is replaced by a water wound, the 2001 drought shutoff Marty served as a deputy that cost a neighbor his farm; it now ties to book 3's bones (both critics, most generic element).
- Changed: Jurisdiction: Ortega works the death, Marty works the water as an unbadged witness whose measurements, locks and signed log make the break; the resentful-trainee pairing and 'back beside the sheriff' are gone (both critics).
- Changed: Padlock moved to the center: her lock is cut in book 1, she locks out her brother in book 2, her lock log dates the ledger in book 4, and the logline restores 'keys to three hundred headgates' (auditor).
- Changed: Deaths moved off the canal and varied: potato cellar in book 1, records vault in book 4, a theft that turns lethal in book 2, an old death in book 3 (auditor on body-in-ditch overlap, superfan on four fresh homicides).
- Changed: Ledger made earned and legally modest: pages surface in books 1-3 and it reopens one priority date instead of reranking the river; restored 'Snake River Plain', 'shareholder', 'a call dries up junior fields' and 'every suspect's place in line' from the carried notes (superfan).
- Declined: Making one book a pure fraud or accident case with no death: the logline's 'someone dies over it' is the most praised line, so the deaths were varied in kind instead.
- Declined: Ortega's grievance that she left him holding the shooting file: the shooting no longer exists, and his objection is now the jurisdiction line itself.
- Declined: Stating whether Ortega deputizes or contracts her: she is neither, only a witness with a log, and spelling out more would cost words the page does not have.
- For the author to decide: Are the bones in book 3 the neighbor Marty shut off in 2001, or someone else from that drought? The pitch leaves it open; the first makes her a party to the case, which changes how Ortega can use her testimony.
- For the author to decide: The claim that a recovered ledger can reopen one priority date under a 1911 decree should be checked against Idaho adjudication practice (fraud or clerical-error grounds) before it is fixed in the book 4 outline.

**Gate (editor, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (slight). B ties everything to water: Marty's wound is a 2001 shutoff, not a stock shooting; she locks out her own brother; book 3's bones are hers; ledger pages surface each book. The cellar death with a headgate open four miles off is a better puzzle. What the losing version did better: A explains priority in plain words (oldest ditch filled before a newer gets a drop), names her a former deputy in the logline, makes Ortega her resentful ex-trainee, keeps "March wind to first frost", and says book 4 settles the thread.
