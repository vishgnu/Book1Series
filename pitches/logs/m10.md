# m10: The Camas Fork Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Western mystery. Target readers: Men and women 45–70.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: First in Time. Marty Voss, 54, quit the sheriff's office after a shooting and took the job nobody wants: ditch rider for the Camas Fork Canal Company on Idaho's Snake River Plain (potatoes, sugar beets, dairies, lava rock). From April to October she drives 62 miles of canal with a shovel, a measuring stick and keys to three hundred headgates, deciding who gets how much water under a law older than the state: first in time, first in right. Water theft is the valley's oldest sin, and a ditch rider sees every field, pond and back gate; she has no badge, only the right to shut your water off. Each book starts with water that is not where it should be (a headgate chained open in a drought, a measuring flume shaved by half an inch, a pond that fills at night) and ends in a death, and in who needed the water and why. Cast ideas: the young sheriff who used to be her trainee; the state watermaster; a third-generation potato grower; a hydrologist newcomer the old families distrust; a water lawyer. Four books, one water year: spring turn-on, the July call on the river, harvest, winter in water court. Thread: the 1911 decree that ranks every right in the valley rests on a ledger no one has seen since. Titles from water law (First in Time, Call on the River, Beneficial Use, Use It or Lose It).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Cindi Myers, Conspiracy in the Rockies (Colorado, a rancher dead in an irrigation ditch, a deputy who enforces water rights; romantic suspense); Judith Van Gieson, Ditch Rider (Albuquerque ditches, a lawyer sleuth); Patricia McLinn, Last Ditch (a Wyoming irrigation dispute). No ditch-rider sleuth found. The seed has already been moved from Colorado to Idaho and no longer opens on a body in the ditch. Avoid 'Ditch Rider' and 'Last Ditch' as titles.

**First draft:** “The Camas Fork Mysteries”, 516 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Bill).** A ditch rider with a padlock is the right detective for this country and I'd borrow book 1, but the water law has soft spots I'd catch. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 6, setting 8, fit 8, clarity 7.

- Keep: The ditch rider as detective: "no badge, only the right to lock a headgate" and "states the measurement before the opinion"; The small true tampering details: "a headgate chained open, a flume shaved half an inch, a pond that fills at night"; Titles straight out of water law (First in Time, Call on the River, Beneficial Use, Use It or Lose It) and the well-data fight with the old families
- Problem 1: It mixes canal company shares with priority rights. A "shareholder" on one canal takes a pro-rata cut in a short year; priority runs between diversions on the river, not between neighbors on Marty's ditch. Why it matters: "Every suspect's place in line was fixed in 1911" is wrong if they all hold shares in the same canal. That is the false note that stops me. Suggested fix: Decide which it is: several ditches and private rights off the Camas Fork with separate priority dates, or one company where the fight is over shares, rotation and theft. Keep the watermaster's calls at the river.
- Problem 2: A 1911 decree as the last word ignores the Snake River Basin Adjudication, which re-decreed every right on the plain by 2014. And Idaho watermasters are elected by a district's users, not a "state watermaster". Why it matters: Anybody who sat through the adjudication knows a lost surveyor's ledger could not quietly reorder 214 rights today. The running thread rests on it. Suggested fix: Make the ledger evidence in a challenge to how the adjudication carried the 1911 dates forward, or set the valley on a tributary with its own unsettled decree. Call Hinckley the district watermaster.
- Problem 3: Four deaths over water in one county in one year, and one rider covering "62 miles of canal" and "three hundred headgates" alone. Both are stretched. Why it matters: Water fights are mostly shovels, lawyers and grudges that last forty years. Too many bodies in one season and it stops feeling like a real valley. Suggested fix: Spread the books over two or three irrigation seasons, or make one death old or doubtful. Give Marty a section of a larger system, with other riders named.
- Problem 4: Marty's past is the usual one: "a shooting the review board cleared and she has not". "It costs her neighbors" is unclear, and I don't know whether she farms or has family. Why it matters: I've met that haunted ex-deputy in a dozen series. What I'd follow is her standing in the valley, and the pitch doesn't tell me. Suggested fix: Say plainly what locking a gate costs her: whose crop she shorted and who quit speaking to her at the co-op. Give her ground, stock or kin tied to a junior right.
- Reminded them of: Joe Pickett novels (C.J. Box); Longmire (Craig Johnson); Anna Pigeon mysteries (Nevada Barr); The Milagro Beanfield War; Chinatown; Leaphorn and Chee (Tony Hillerman)

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A ditch rider with a padlock and a dead body below a chained headgate is a fresh, credible western mystery; the lead's heart is thinner than her job. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 9, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: "water turns up where it should not be, and someone dies over it" - the line my eye caught in the first ten seconds, and the rest of the page delivers on it; The job itself: "keys to three hundred headgates", "whose only power is a padlock", "reads a wet bank like a crime scene"; One valley through a year with plain rules I can follow: "first in time, first in right", nine inches of rain, a case closed per book, no cliffhangers
- Problem 1: In ten seconds I had "Marty Voss, 54, a former deputy" and pictured a man. "She" arrives three paragraphs down. I also did not know what a ditch rider does. Why it matters: I swipe on a guess. A woman of 54 riding canal is a selling point for half this readership, and the logline hides it. Suggested fix: Put "she" in the logline and gloss the job in six words: "the woman who opens and locks every farmer's water gate along 62 miles of canal."
- Problem 2: Marty is a resume and a method. "A shooting the review board cleared and she has not" is the standard ex-cop wound, and nothing says who she lives with, loves, or stands to lose. Why it matters: I binge four books for the person, not the canal. Longmire and Pickett have family and home; she has a measuring habit. Suggested fix: Add one concrete personal tie and one stake in the valley: a sister farming junior ground, a grown son, a house on the canal. Say who was shot and whether that family still lives there.
- Problem 3: "It costs her neighbors: no badge, only the right to lock a headgate" reads garbled. I read it twice and still am not sure whether she loses neighbors or her neighbors pay. Why it matters: It is the one sentence meant to show what the job costs her, and it is where I would have swiped away in the lead paragraph. Suggested fix: Say it plainly: "She has no badge now, only the right to lock a headgate, and every gate she locks belongs to someone she has known thirty years."
- Problem 4: The book lines and running thread lean on unexplained trade terms: "a call dries up junior fields", "forfeiture fight", "decree ranks 214 valley rights". The ledger thread sounds like a records dispute, not a danger. Why it matters: I like learning the water rules, but I need to feel who gets ruined if the ledger says otherwise, or book 4 sounds like a hearing. Suggested fix: Tie the ledger to people already named: if it turns up, Ruth Aldecoa's family right may drop behind a neighbor's. Swap one term per book line for plain words, e.g. "the state shuts off the newer farms."
- Reminded them of: Longmire (Craig Johnson); Joe Pickett (C.J. Box); Anna Pigeon (Nevada Barr); The Milagro Beanfield War; Chinatown; Yellowstone

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now shows the lead is a woman and says what a ditch rider does in plain words (ten-second-browser, problem 1).
- Changed: The valley is now fourteen ditches off one river, each with its own priority date, so the 1911 line runs between ditches and not between shareholders on one canal; Hinckley is the district watermaster (cold-reader, problems 1 and 2).
- Changed: The garbled cost sentence is replaced with the plain version, and Marty gets a stake: the shot man's family still farms on her section and her brother Gene farms under the newest ditch (both critics on the thin lead).
- Changed: The ledger thread now names who is ruined: Ruth Aldecoa's ditch or Gene Voss's came first. Book lines 2 and 4 swap 'call', 'junior' and 'forfeiture' for plain words (ten-second-browser, problem 4).
- Changed: Marty's beat is cut from 62 miles and three hundred headgates to her own 24-mile section, and book 3's death is now old bones from the 2001 drought, so the year has three fresh deaths (cold-reader, problem 3).
- Declined: Spreading the books over two or three irrigation seasons: one valley through a single year is a strength the other critic named.
- Declined: Replacing the shooting backstory outright: kept it but tied it to a family still on her section, since the fix asked for was her standing in the valley and there was no room for a new history.
- Declined: Naming the other ditch riders and the basin adjudication on the page: both add names and mechanisms a first-time reader would have to decode.
- For the author to decide: The Snake River Basin Adjudication re-decreed these rights by 2014. Should book 4 be a challenge to how that adjudication carried the 1911 dates forward, or should the valley sit on an invented tributary with its own unsettled decree? The pitch currently leaves this unstated.
- For the author to decide: The logline lost 'keys to three hundred headgates', which one critic liked, to make her beat credible. Do you want a true headgate count for a 24-mile section put back in?

**Gate (reader: Bill, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). A gets the water right: priority runs between fourteen ditches on a river, not inside one canal. 24 miles is a believable ride; 62 miles and 300 gates is not. The brother on the newest ditch against Ruth on the oldest, and the dead man's family on her section, make it personal. What the losing version did better: "Snake River Plain" places it quicker. "Shareholder" and "a call dries up junior fields" are the words we actually use. "Every suspect's place in line was fixed in 1911" is a sharper closing line than "every ditch's".
