# m05: The Open Class Mysteries

Revision log. Genre: Culinary cozy mystery. Target readers: Women 55–75.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Open Class. Dot Engelhardt, 67, spent thirty years in the test kitchen of a big flour mill and now spends each summer driving a 1994 Winnebago around the county fairs of a fictional Upper Midwest state as a certified open-class foods judge: pies, preserves, breads. This year her 19-year-old granddaughter Kayla comes along to drive and earn college money. A county fair is a closed world for one week (the same families, the same rivalries, 4-H kids, the midway company, the demolition derby), and Dot's palate is evidence: she can taste store-bought jam in a homemade jar, tell lard from shortening, date a sourdough, and say which entry came out of which kitchen. Each book is one fair and one death passed off as an accident (the dairy barn, the grandstand, the Ferris wheel), and the answer runs through the exhibit hall. The four books are one summer: county fairs in June, July and August, then the State Fair. Thread: the same traveling midway company works all four fairs, and its ride inspector is the first to die. Cast ideas: a widowed livestock judge who keeps turning up; a fair-board secretary who knows everything; a deputy who shows hogs. No judge is murdered and nobody is poisoned by a pie.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Joanne Fluke, Key Lime Pie Murder (a baking judge at a Minnesota county fair, one book); Livia J. Washburn's Fresh-Baked Mysteries (a retired-teacher sleuth, baking contests, recipes); Kylie Logan's Chili Cook-Off Mysteries (a traveling contest circuit, a new venue per book). No traveling fair-judge series found. Titles with 'Blue Ribbon' are crowded. The seed's lead has already been changed from a retired teacher to a retired test-kitchen home economist.

**First draft:** “The Open Class Mysteries”, 514 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Linda).** A fair judge my own age who solves murders by tasting pie crust is fresh and smart; I'd borrow book 1, though I'd miss a hometown and a pet. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 7, setting 9, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: Dot's palate as the detective's tool: 'She can tell lard from shortening and one kitchen from another', with clues like 'a sourdough younger than its story'; The fair as a town that lasts a week, with the built-in clock: 'nothing leaves the hall until Sunday release, after which the evidence gets eaten'; Dot herself, 67 and competent, who 'talks like a recipe card: short, in order', plus Harlan who 'keeps turning up with coffee'
- Problem 1: The running thread is muddy. 'Someone local took its money', 'names the paymaster', and Walt's entry numbers: I can't tell what the midway company is actually up to. Why it matters: If I can't follow the big mystery on the series page, I assume it will be a tangle of bribes and bookkeeping, not a puzzle I can play along with. Suggested fix: Say in one plain sentence what Voss & Daughter is hiding and what the entry numbers have to do with it, without giving away who is behind it.
- Problem 2: 'The village is new each book' means the townsfolk I'd get attached to are left behind every time the Winnebago pulls out. Only four or five people travel. Why it matters: Recurring neighbors are half of why I binge a series. A new cast each book feels like starting over four times. Suggested fix: Show a traveling circuit family: the same exhibitors, rival bakers, carnies and the hog deputy turning up at every fair, so faces and grudges carry from June to the State Fair.
- Problem 3: No pet anywhere, and almost no humor on the page. 'Bickering over the map' is the only light moment promised; the rest reads rather dry and stern. Why it matters: I want to smile while I read. A motorhome with no dog or cat in it is a missed chance, and a terse sleuth can turn cold. Suggested fix: Put an animal in the Winnebago or the hog pens with a name and a habit, and give one line of Dot's dry wit so I can hear that she's funny.
- Problem 4: The deaths sound grim for a cozy: a man 'dies under the Ferris wheel', a bookkeeper 'dies in the butter cooler'. Nothing says how gently these are handled. Why it matters: I stop at gore. A body crushed under a ride or frozen in a cooler could go either way, and I can't tell from here. Suggested fix: Add a phrase making clear the deaths happen off the page and are found quietly, and keep the attention on the jam and the alibi rather than the body.
- Reminded them of: Joanne Fluke's Hannah Swensen mysteries; Diane Mott Davidson's Goldy Schulz series; Murder, She Wrote; Jessica Beck's Donut Shop mysteries; The Great British Baking Show (the judging tent); Leslie Budewitz's Food Lovers' Village mysteries

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A fair judge in a Winnebago who solves murders by taste is exactly my shelf; the midway money plot is muddy, but I would borrow book 1. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 7, setting 9, fit 8, clarity 6.

- Keep: First ten seconds caught: 'judges pies, preserves and breads at Upper Midwest fairs from a 1994 Winnebago' and 'the proof otherwise is on her judging table'. I knew the whole series at once.; The hall rule: 'entries carry a number, never a name' and 'after which the evidence gets eaten'. A built-in clock and it made me smile.; Clues by palate with concrete examples: 'store-bought jam breaks an alibi', 'a sourdough younger than its story', plus 'Dot and Kayla bickering over the map'.
- Problem 1: Swipe point: 'Cases keep arriving because Voss & Daughter Amusements works all four fairs, and in each county someone local took its money.' I could not tell what the money was for. Why it matters: It reads like a bookkeeping crime, not a cozy. After ten seconds I still did not know what the midway is actually doing wrong. Suggested fix: Say the scheme in one plain sentence: what Voss pays locals to do or overlook (skipped ride inspections, rigged ribbons) and why that leads to a death at each fair.
- Problem 2: Dot is all skill and little warmth. 'She talks like a recipe card: short, in order' is clever but cool, and no line shows her being funny or fond. Why it matters: I stay with a series for the woman, not the method. I did not yet know why I would love her. Suggested fix: Add one line of Dot's actual voice, a dry remark to Kayla or Harlan, and say why this summer with her granddaughter matters now.
- Problem 3: 'Saying what she tastes costs neighbors ribbons and alibis' confused me. She travels between counties, so whose neighbors are these, and how does a ribbon cost an alibi? Why it matters: It is the sentence carrying her personal stakes, and I had to read it twice on a small screen. Suggested fix: Split it: speaking up embarrasses the people who feed and park her each week, and a judge who makes trouble is not invited back next year.
- Problem 4: The four book lines all follow one pattern: a death, a food clue, a small character note. Three falls or fairground accidents in a row, and no victim besides Walt has a name or face. Why it matters: Binge readers notice sameness fast. I want to know each fair has its own flavor and a victim I care about. Suggested fix: Give each book one distinguishing human detail: who the victim was to the town, and one fair-specific pleasure (demolition derby, butter sculpture) the case turns on.
- Reminded them of: Joanne Fluke's Hannah Swensen mysteries; Diane Mott Davidson's Goldy Schulz series; Murder, She Wrote; The Great British Bake Off judging tent; Leslie Budewitz's Food Lovers' Village mysteries; Livia J. Washburn's Fresh-Baked mysteries

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).** It ran over the length limit and was trimmed by a line editor before the gate.

- Changed: Series engine and running thread now state the scheme plainly: Voss pays one local per fair to sign ride-safety papers unread, disguised as a $500 sponsor's prize, and Walt's entry numbers are those prize winners (both critics: muddy money plot).
- Changed: Circuit family: the setting and closing sections say the same bakers, carnies and hog men follow all four fairs, and Lorraine now enters pie at every fair instead of staying in Tamarack (cold reader: townsfolk left behind).
- Changed: Added Oleo, a basset hound who howls at Ferris wheels and steals corn dogs, plus one line of Dot's dry voice in place of 'short, in order' (both critics: no pet, little humor or warmth).
- Changed: Lead: stakes sentence split into plain terms (embarrassing her hosts, not invited back) and the summer given a reason, Kayla moves to Seattle in September (ten-second browser).
- Changed: Arc and engine: deaths are 'found', off the page and quietly; each victim has a role in their town and the three falls are varied (milk house, derby booth, butter sculpture) (both critics: grimness, sameness).
- Declined: Naming and characterizing every victim in the arc lines: the length cap allows a role per victim, not a portrait.
- Declined: A fixed hometown for Dot: the week-long traveling fair is the strength both critics named, so the circuit neighbors answer the attachment worry instead.
- For the author to decide: Lorraine changed from Tamarack's fair-board secretary to a pie exhibitor who travels the whole circuit; confirm, or keep her on the board and add a separate rival baker.
- For the author to decide: The payoff is now a $500 sponsor's prize chosen by the midway, not by Dot; confirm this mechanism, since books 1 to 4 will hang clues on it.

**Gate (reader: Linda, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). B gives me a basset hound named Oleo, townsfolk who follow the circuit so I see them again, Dot's actual voice in one funny line, and deaths kept quietly off the page. A's village resets each book, which loses the recurring neighbors I read for. What the losing version did better: A's closing image "Then the Winnebago pulls out" and "dill pickles held to the light" are lovely. A's engine is simpler; B's ride-safety papers and $500 sponsor's prize read a bit like paperwork.
