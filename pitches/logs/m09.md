# m09: Cause of Failure

Revision log. Genre: Techno-mystery. Target readers: Men and women 40–65.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: Factor of Safety. Elias Brandt, 52, was a principal at a big structural engineering firm until a parking garage he had flagged in peer review came down and the firm let him carry it. Now he works out of a rented bay on the Duluth waterfront as a forensic engineer for insurers, small towns and the occasional plaintiff: he says why it fell. He does not work alone, and the series is the three of them: Naomi Adeyemi, 29, a metallurgist with a portable lab and no patience, and Dutch Kowalczyk, 68, a retired crane operator who knows how steel is really put up. Each book is one failure that everyone calls an accident (a ski-lift tower, a grain elevator, an ore-dock conveyor, a small dam's spillway gate) and the physical evidence that says a person made it fail, or let it: beach marks on a fracture face, the wrong bolt grade, a weld inspection signed on a day it rained. The reader sees every piece of evidence and gets the engineering explained plainly. Thread: the garage collapse. Someone changed a drawing after it was sealed, and each case hands Elias a piece of how. Titles from engineering terms (Factor of Safety, Load Path, Fatigue Limit, Single Point of Failure).

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Aileen Schumacher's Tory Travers series (a structural-engineer sleuth in New Mexico; collapse as accident or sabotage); John Billheimer's Owen Allison series (a federal failure analyst in West Virginia); Robbie Parsons, Fracture Critical (a Pittsburgh bridge inspector, a kidnap thriller). The seed has already been moved from Pittsburgh to Duluth, keeps bridges out of the four cases and leans on the three-person team, which none of these has.

**First draft:** “Cause of Failure”, 506 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Raj).** Forensic engineering as detective work, with clues I could check myself; I would borrow book 1, though the villain and stakes are thinner than the metallurgy. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 7, character 6, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: Checkable physical clues: "a weld inspection signed on a rain day", "beach marks on its shaft date the cracking", forged grade stamps. These are real and I can verify them.; "No insurer pays until an engineer signs a cause, and the signature decides who pays" - a clear rule that gives every case money, pressure and motive.; The trio of engineer, metallurgist with a van lab and retired crane operator, on a specific Duluth working shore at 30 below. "The reader learns to read steel."
- Problem 1: There is no opponent. "The evidence names a person" every time, but the pitch never says what kind of person or why: fraud, cost-cutting, cover-up? All four cases read as falsified paperwork. Why it matters: A puzzle needs a mind on the other side. If the answer is always 'someone faked a record', I will have the pattern solved by book 2. Suggested fix: Give each book a different motive and method in a clause (insurance fraud, a contractor's shortcut, a sabotaged competitor), and hint that someone is actively working against the team's findings.
- Problem 2: Every case just happens to lead back to Elias's ramp: an inspector "from Elias's ramp", a fabricator whose files "hold the ramp's original drawings". Three coincidences in one small region. Why it matters: I check plausibility. Unconnected failures all pointing at one man's old case feels arranged unless there is a common cause I am told to expect. Suggested fix: State the link up front: one inspection firm, supplier or records contractor touches all these structures. Then the thread is a pattern Elias uncovers, not luck.
- Problem 3: Martin Vance "still takes his calls" and the firm is Thorsby Vance. He is the obvious suspect for the sealed sheet from the first page. Why it matters: If I can guess the book 4 answer from the series page, the running thread gives me no reason to read four books. Suggested fix: Either add a second credible candidate with equal access to the sealed sheet, or make the 'how' the real question and say so: the who is suspected early, the proof is the hard part.
- Problem 4: Low present-tense pressure. "The violence is over before page one" and the work is testing parts in a bay. Nothing in the pitch says what happens if they are slow or wrong. Why it matters: I want momentum as well as cleverness. Lab work alone, however accurate, can read like a report with no clock on it. Suggested fix: Add a deadline or cost per book: a claim window closing, a sister structure still in service that may fail the same way, or Elias's licence at risk if he signs the wrong cause.
- Reminded them of: Air Crash Investigation / Mayday (TV); Engineering Disasters / Seconds from Disaster (TV); Henry Petroski, To Engineer Is Human; Nevil Shute, No Highway; CSI-style forensic procedurals; Kathy Reichs's Temperance Brennan series

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** A grown-up procedural where steel is the witness; I would borrow book 1, though the lead's grievance is clearer than the man and the villains are faceless. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 8, engine 8, character 6, setting 8, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: "the metal shows the accident had an author" - the line that stopped my thumb in the first ten seconds; The trade trio (engineer, metallurgist with a van lab, 68-year-old crane operator) and clues like "a weld inspection signed on a rain day"; Duluth working shore at 30 below, plus "no insurer pays until an engineer signs a cause, and the signature decides who pays"
- Problem 1: In ten seconds I caught 'blamed for a parking ramp collapse' and 'the accident had an author', but nearly swiped at 'telling insurers why things fell'. I still did not know whether anyone dies or is in danger. Why it matters: Insurance paperwork sounds like my day job. Without a body or a threat in the first lines, it reads as a claims dispute, not a mystery. Suggested fix: Put the human cost in the logline: name the dead or injured in the failure and say the signature Elias is asked for would clear whoever caused it.
- Problem 2: "a sealed drawing made the detail he flagged look approved" took me two reads. The core injustice is buried in a clause and assumes I know what sealing a drawing means. Why it matters: This is the thread meant to pull me through four books; if I have to decode it on a phone, I will not carry it. Suggested fix: Say it flat: Elias warned in writing that a connection was unsafe; after the collapse, the stamped drawing on file showed his approval of it. Someone changed the sheet.
- Problem 3: Elias is a grievance and a speech habit: 'short sentences, units attached'. No family, no flaw, no guilt about the two dead, nothing he gets wrong. Renee and Martin Vance are one-line functions. Why it matters: I binge a series for the people. Four books with a man who only wants a sheet of paper back could run cold. Suggested fix: Give Elias one personal stake and one fault: someone he owes in Duluth, doubt about whether he pushed hard enough, and hint whether Martin Vance is friend or suspect.
- Problem 4: "The evidence names a person" every time, but no culprit, motive or pushback appears. All four book lines end on a ramp clue, so the cases look like delivery vehicles for the thread. Why it matters: A mystery needs someone who does not want to be found. Otherwise it is four lab reports, and book 1's own case feels secondary. Suggested fix: For each book add who benefits and what they do to stop the signature: a resort owner, a co-op board, a threatened contract. Let book 1 state its own culprit question.
- Reminded them of: Henry Petroski, To Engineer Is Human; Seconds from Disaster / Engineering Disasters (TV); Nevada Barr's Anna Pigeon (A Superior Death); William Kent Krueger's Cork O'Connor; Kathy Reichs' Temperance Brennan; CSI (early seasons)

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline: put the deaths up front (the ramp killed two; someone is dead in every book) and cut 'telling insurers why things fell' (browser: reads as a claims dispute).
- Changed: The lead: stated the injustice flat (he warned in writing, the stamped drawing showed his approval, someone changed the sheet) and added one fault, his doubt that he pushed hard enough (browser: two-read clause, lead is only a grievance).
- Changed: The setting: named one inspection firm, Northline Testing, that covers most of the shore and Elias's ramp, so the ramp links are a pattern rather than coincidence (cold reader).
- Changed: Engine and arc: added a 60-day clock and a culprit with money at stake who fights the finding; each book now has its own motive (cheap steel, bought inspections, a hidden defect before a sale, billing for work not done) (both critics: no opponent, same pattern, low pressure).
- Changed: Running thread: now 'which of three people with access', so Martin Vance is not the only candidate and the how carries equal weight (cold reader).
- Declined: Family or a personal debt in Duluth for Elias: adds a subplot the length cap cannot carry; the doubt about the two dead does the work.
- Declined: Per-book acts of sabotage against the team and a sister structure about to fail: would crowd each arc line and push toward thriller action; the engine carries pushback and the clock once.
- Declined: Changing elements because of the listed resemblances: they are shelf neighbors (documentary TV, forensic procedurals), not a copied character, place or device.
- For the author to decide: Who are the three people with access to sheet S-401 (Vance, the other name partner, a Northline reviewer)? Is Vance guilty, or the decoy?
- For the author to decide: The logline now promises a death in every case. Confirm that fits books 2-4, or soften to 'someone is hurt or dead'.

**Gate (reader: Raj, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). B gives each case a culprit and motive (cheap steel, bought weld passes, hidden cracking, billed-for grease), a 60-day clock, an opponent who fights back, and a fair-play thread: three suspects, one testing firm linking everything. A's cases stop at the clue. What the losing version did better: A's logline is cleaner: "works from a rented bay on the Duluth waterfront telling insurers why things fell" is more concrete than B's "signs the cause". A's lead paragraph is also tighter, and it does not promise a death in every book.
