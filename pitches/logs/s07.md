# s07: The Right to Sign

Revision log. Genre: Dystopian science fiction. Target readers: Women and men 25–55.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Right to Sign. In the Lakes Commonwealth adulthood is a license. Every five years each citizen sits the Assessment (money, civics, a stability index), and those who fail become Wards: a licensed Guardian company takes over their bank account, their lease and their front-door key. Wards can work and marry but cannot sign. Lawyers are barred from Assessment tribunals, so appeals are argued by certified lay advocates, most of them former Wards. Lead: Ines Calder, 37, a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four, now an advocate working from the back office of a laundromat in the port city of Meridian; exact, funny when she is angry, owns one good jacket. Each book is one client and thirty days to a hearing: a baker with dyscalculia whose shop sits on a rezoned block; a 71-year-old union archivist who failed memory and holds records someone wants; a young coder who refuses to sit the test at all. She investigates why this person failed and who gains, then argues it in a room built to say no. Each hearing is won or lost within the book. Thread: the Assessment's pass mark moves by postcode to feed the Guardian firms, and in book four Ines's own renewal comes back failed. One changed rule, followed through into rent, work, love and paperwork.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Luke Kennard, The Transition (adults who fail at adulthood enter a supervised program); Jessamine Chan, The School for Good Mothers (state fitness assessment by metrics). No periodic adult license or lay-advocate series found. 'Of Sound Mind' is taken as a title and has already been dropped. Lead with tribunal casework and never use a re-education facility.

**First draft:** “The Right to Sign”, 509 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Jess).** Adulthood as a revocable license, fought with leases and score sheets, is exactly the plausible system I want; I'd borrow book 1, though the villain is still faceless. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: The rule itself: "adulthood is a license renewed by exam" and "Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign." Scary because it sounds like a policy memo.; Ines as former Ward who knows "which form gets filed out of order", plus Marian Voss who "has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page" - an opponent with integrity, not a cartoon.; Book 2's "loses the appeal, saves the archive" and the ending where "the Assessment stands with one published mark" - wins cost something, no tidy revolution.
- Problem 1: "Who profits" has no face. Guardian companies are a storefront on every third block, but no named firm, executive or person stands opposite Ines across the four books. Why it matters: That is one step from the vague evil system I hate. I want to know who I am rooting against by book 1. Suggested fix: Name one Guardian firm and one person in it who appears in every book, with a reasonable-sounding motive (fees per Ward, rezoned property) stated in the pitch.
- Problem 2: Ines's personal stake is thin until book 4. Sam, "her brother, still a Ward", gets one line and no role in any book. She loses "sleep and rent", which is mild. Why it matters: I binge for the person, not just the cases. Three books of other people's problems before hers arrive is a long wait. Suggested fix: Give Sam a thread that moves each book - e.g. his Guardian is the firm in the running thread, or his own renewal date is ticking - and say so in the arc.
- Problem 3: Book 1's payoff reads small: she "notes the local pass mark was 74, not 70". Noting is not acting, and four books of hearings could feel like the same shape repeated. Why it matters: Book 1 decides whether I borrow book 2. I need her to do something with that number, and I need each book to feel different. Suggested fix: Have Ines use the 74 in book 1 at a cost (burns Teo's leak, angers Voss). Signal one distinct mode per book: eviction clock, archive heist-by-paperwork, a client who refuses, herself.
- Problem 4: How the Commonwealth got here, and why people accept it, is absent. "A stability index" is the creepiest phrase on the page and is never explained. Why it matters: Plausibility is the whole sell for me. One sentence of origin makes it feel like next decade instead of a thought experiment. Suggested fix: Add one line: what crisis or sales pitch brought the Assessment in (debt protection? elder fraud?) and what the stability index actually measures.
- Reminded them of: I, Daniel Blake; Severance (the TV show); The Handmaid's Tale (the loss of bank accounts part); Papers, Please; Better Call Saul (elder guardianship cases); I Care a Lot

**ten-second-browser (a subscription reader scrolling on a phone).** "Adulthood is a license renewed by exam" stopped my thumb; the page delivers a clean case-per-book dystopia, though it reads cooler and more procedural than it needs to. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: The rule in one line: "adulthood is a license renewed by exam" plus "Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign." I understood the whole world and what is at stake in ten seconds.; Ines's own stake and book 4: "was a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four", brother Sam "still a Ward", then "unable to sign her own filing". Also Marian Voss, who "has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page".; The promise of a verdict inside every book, with a loss in book 2 ("loses the appeal, saves the archive") and the clock: "a hearing thirty days out".
- Problem 1: In my first ten seconds I caught the license line and "thirty days", but not what losing your signature costs. "Win back their signature" sounds like paperwork until the setting paragraph mentions the "front-door key". Why it matters: The logline is all I read before deciding. I nearly swiped at "lay advocate" because the danger was still abstract. Suggested fix: Put the concrete loss in the logline: fail, and a Guardian company holds your bank account, your lease and your front-door key. Ines has thirty days to get them back.
- Problem 2: The running thread is a statistics question: "Why the pass mark moves by postcode". Book 1 ends on "notes the local pass mark was 74, not 70", and the finale ends with "one published mark". Why it matters: That is correct and plausible, but it is not what makes me borrow book 2 at midnight. I want a person behind the number. Suggested fix: Tie the thread to Sam: say his failure was one of the moved marks, and that proving the postcode scheme is how Ines might get her brother his signature back in book 4.
- Problem 3: Sam Calder gets one clause, "her brother, still a Ward", and then vanishes from all four book lines. Teo Brandt "leaks schedules" with no reason or risk given. Why it matters: These are the two relationships that would make me care between cases, and the page tells me nothing about either. Suggested fix: Give Sam one line of friction with Ines (why she has not won his case) and Teo one line of motive and cost. Show Sam's status changing or mattering in book 4.
- Problem 4: The book lines read like case summaries. "Ines wins a narrow ruling that puts raw score sheets on record" has no danger in it, and nothing says what the Guardian firms do to Ines for winning. Why it matters: Institutional tension is what I came for, and the page never shows the institution pushing back at her before book 4. Suggested fix: Add one retaliation per book line: an audit of her advocate standing, the Blue Drum's lease under review, Teo's leaks traced. Make book 4's failed renewal clearly the price of books 1 to 3.
- Reminded them of: The Handmaid's Tale (rights removed by paperwork); I, Daniel Blake; Better Call Saul (scrappy back-office advocate); Papers, Please; Severance; The Measure by Nikki Erlick

**Rewrite: accepted (clear).**

- Changed: Logline now states the concrete loss (bank account, lease, front-door key) before introducing Ines; answers the ten-second-browser's point that 'win back their signature' read as paperwork.
- Changed: Named the opposition: Paul Rennick, 54, and Lakehead Guardian, paid monthly per Ward, present in the cast, every book line and the running thread; answers the cold-reader's 'who profits has no face'. Dot Okafor was cut from the cast to pay for the words.
- Changed: Sam now carries the thread: he scored 73 against a moved mark, Ines has lost his appeal twice, his sheet surfaces in book 3 and he signs his own lease in book 4; answers both critics on thin personal stake.
- Changed: Book 1 has Ines use the 74 in the hearing rather than note it, and each book line ends with Lakehead's retaliation (audit, lease review, Teo fired), so book 4's failed renewal reads as the price of books 1 to 3.
- Changed: Setting gains one origin clause (fraud protection for the elderly, thirty years ago) and says what the stability index measures; Teo gets a motive (his mother is a Ward).
- Declined: A distinct labeled 'mode' per book (eviction clock, heist-by-paperwork): the cases already differ in kind, and labels would cost words the retaliation beats needed.
- Declined: Having Ines's book 1 win anger Voss: it would undercut Voss as the fair opponent who reads every page, which the cold-reader named as a strength.
- Declined: Warming the tone away from procedural: the administrative coolness is the named point of difference; the added heat comes from Sam and Rennick instead.
- For the author to decide: Dot Okafor was cut from the cast line to fit Rennick within the word limit; the Blue Drum stays. Decide whether Dot returns at the expense of Teo's motive or the origin clause.
- For the author to decide: Book 4 now says Lakehead paid for the postcode marks. Decide who inside the Assessment office took the money, or whether that stays open for later books.

**Gate (reader: Jess, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (clear). B names who profits: Lakehead bills per Ward, so the evil is a business model, not a vague system. The stability index built from missed payments and address changes is scarily plausible. Sam's 73 and Lakehead's retaliation after each win make me want the next book. What the losing version did better: Dot Okafor taking rent in ironing and gossip, and "one good jacket", gave Ines warmth and a home base. A's "Ines is good company" line and shorter, cleaner book summaries read faster.
