# s07: The Right to Sign

Revision log. Genre: Dystopian science fiction. Target readers: Women and men 25–55.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Right to Sign. In the Lakes Commonwealth adulthood is a license. Every five years each citizen sits the Assessment (money, civics, a stability index), and those who fail become Wards: a licensed Guardian company takes over their bank account, their lease and their front-door key. Wards can work and marry but cannot sign. Lawyers are barred from Assessment tribunals, so appeals are argued by certified lay advocates, most of them former Wards. Lead: Ines Calder, 37, a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four, now an advocate working from the back office of a laundromat in the port city of Meridian; exact, funny when she is angry, owns one good jacket. Each book is one client and thirty days to a hearing: a baker with dyscalculia whose shop sits on a rezoned block; a 71-year-old union archivist who failed memory and holds records someone wants; a young coder who refuses to sit the test at all. She investigates why this person failed and who gains, then argues it in a room built to say no. Each hearing is won or lost within the book. Thread: the Assessment's pass mark moves by postcode to feed the Guardian firms, and in book four Ines's own renewal comes back failed. One changed rule, followed through into rent, work, love and paperwork.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Closest found: Luke Kennard, The Transition (adults who fail at adulthood enter a supervised program); Jessamine Chan, The School for Good Mothers (state fitness assessment by metrics). No periodic adult license or lay-advocate series found. 'Of Sound Mind' is taken as a title and has already been dropped. Lead with tribunal casework and never use a re-education facility.

**First draft:** “The Right to Sign”, 509 words.

### Round 1: First contact

**cold-reader (Jess).** Adulthood as a revocable license, fought with leases and score sheets, is exactly the plausible system I want; I'd borrow book 1, though the villain is still faceless. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 7.

- Keep: The rule itself: "adulthood is a license renewed by exam" and "Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign." Scary because it sounds like a policy memo.; Ines as former Ward who knows "which form gets filed out of order", plus Marian Voss who "has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page" - an opponent with integrity, not a cartoon.; Book 2's "loses the appeal, saves the archive" and the ending where "the Assessment stands with one published mark" - wins cost something, no tidy revolution.
- Problem 1: "Who profits" has no face. Guardian companies are a storefront on every third block, but no named firm, executive or person stands opposite Ines across the four books. Why it matters: That is one step from the vague evil system I hate. I want to know who I am rooting against by book 1. Suggested fix: Name one Guardian firm and one person in it who appears in every book, with a reasonable-sounding motive (fees per Ward, rezoned property) stated in the pitch.
- Problem 2: Ines's personal stake is thin until book 4. Sam, "her brother, still a Ward", gets one line and no role in any book. She loses "sleep and rent", which is mild. Why it matters: I binge for the person, not just the cases. Three books of other people's problems before hers arrive is a long wait. Suggested fix: Give Sam a thread that moves each book - e.g. his Guardian is the firm in the running thread, or his own renewal date is ticking - and say so in the arc.
- Problem 3: Book 1's payoff reads small: she "notes the local pass mark was 74, not 70". Noting is not acting, and four books of hearings could feel like the same shape repeated. Why it matters: Book 1 decides whether I borrow book 2. I need her to do something with that number, and I need each book to feel different. Suggested fix: Have Ines use the 74 in book 1 at a cost (burns Teo's leak, angers Voss). Signal one distinct mode per book: eviction clock, archive heist-by-paperwork, a client who refuses, herself.
- Problem 4: How the Commonwealth got here, and why people accept it, is absent. "A stability index" is the creepiest phrase on the page and is never explained. Why it matters: Plausibility is the whole sell for me. One sentence of origin makes it feel like next decade instead of a thought experiment. Suggested fix: Add one line: what crisis or sales pitch brought the Assessment in (debt protection? elder fraud?) and what the stability index actually measures.
- Reminded them of: I, Daniel Blake; Severance (the TV show); The Handmaid's Tale (the loss of bank accounts part); Papers, Please; Better Call Saul (elder guardianship cases); I Care a Lot

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** A genuinely fresh administrative dystopia whose licence-and-lay-advocate premise is its own; the villain and the corruption thread are the stock parts and should be sharpened. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 7, setting 6, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: "Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign." The signature as the unit of adulthood is the most distinctive element and gives every book a concrete, physical stake.; The advocate who was a Ward and whose brother "scored 73" against a local mark of "74 not 70": a one-point injustice that carries the whole running thread.; Tribunal casework with lawyers barred, a chair who "has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page", and a lost appeal in book 2.
- Problem 1: Most generic element: "book 4 proves Lakehead paid for it". A for-profit company bribing the system, fronted by Paul Rennick who "bills monthly per Ward", is the stock corporate villain of every legal thriller. Why it matters: Readers of this shelf have met the corrupt contractor most often (from memory: Grisham, I Care a Lot). It turns a systemic dystopia into one bad actor. Suggested fix: Make the postcode marks legal: set by an actuarial formula Lakehead lobbied for openly, published nowhere. Book 4 exposes a lawful rule, not a bribe, so the single published mark is a real concession.
- Problem 2: The predatory guardianship company holding "bank account, lease and front-door key" sits close to I Care a Lot and real conservatorship stories (from memory); Rennick has no trait beyond his billing model. Why it matters: Shared trope is acceptable, but a faceless guardian profiteer invites the comparison and wastes the one antagonist seat. Suggested fix: Give Rennick a believing motive: he was a founder of the original elder fraud protection scheme and still signs for his own Ward father. He thinks he is the safeguard.
- Problem 3: The signing device, the most distinctive thing here, is stated once in the setting and only pays off in book 4 ("unable to sign her own filing", "Sam signs his own lease"). Why it matters: The pitch's unique image is being held back as an ending instead of working as the series' recurring signature scene. Suggested fix: Put a signing moment in every book: Lena's supplier contract countersigned by Lakehead, Walt's archive deed, Jun refusing to sign the test sheet. Open the logline on the act: "Fail, and you cannot sign your name."
- Problem 4: "Stability index built from missed payments and address changes" is a social credit score, familiar from Black Mirror's Nosedive and The School for Good Mothers; Meridian's ore boats and grain elevators are decorative, not causal. Why it matters: The scoring mechanism is the second most familiar piece, and the setting does not yet shape the rule it hosts. Suggested fix: Tie the index to the port: seasonal lake work means winter layoffs and boarding-house moves, so dock families fail on address changes by design. The postcode marks then follow the shipping calendar.
- Reminded them of: Luke Kennard, The Transition (supervised failed adults; premise overlap only); Jessamine Chan, The School for Good Mothers (fitness by metrics); I Care a Lot (film; predatory guardianship company) (from memory); Black Mirror: Nosedive (stability score) (from memory); I, Daniel Blake (film; assessment and appeal bureaucracy) (from memory); Papers, Please (game; paperwork as the weapon) (from memory)
- Search note: No web search this round. Resemblances other than the two titles from the prior audit are from memory and unverified. None shares premise plus setting plus signature device; overlaps are trope level. The series title and the four book titles were not checked.

**superfan-reader (Elena).** An administrative dystopia I believe, with a real case engine; the villain and the leaker are off the shelf, but the moving pass mark is new to me. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 7, engine 8, character 6, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: "Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign." One rule, and I can see every lease, bank counter and front door it touches.; The postcode pass mark: "74 not 70" and "he scored 73". A number does the plot work; this is the piece I have not met before, and it is how real scoring systems discriminate.; Marian Voss, who "has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page", plus book 2's "loses the appeal, saves the archive". An honest adversary and a real loss.
- Problem 1: Paul Rennick is the stock profiteer: he "bills monthly per Ward" and that is all he is. The running thread ends on "Lakehead paid for it", which is a bribery reveal I have read many times. Why it matters: A bought mark lets the system off the hook. In my work the harm is usually lawful, documented and signed off, which is far more frightening. Suggested fix: Replace him first. Make Rennick a true believer with a Ward he genuinely protects, and make the postcode marks legal: an actuarial adjustment Lakehead lobbied for openly, minuted, defensible on paper.
- Problem 2: Teo Brandt is the conscience-stricken insider who "leaks schedules" because "his mother is a Ward", then "is fired" in book 3. Stock piece, stock motive, stock exit. Why it matters: A leaker hands Ines her evidence. I come for the paper-trail work, and a source inside the company shortcuts exactly that. Suggested fix: Keep Teo loyal to Lakehead and good at his job. Let Ines get documents through records requests, billing discrepancies and filing errors; let Teo's mother be the reason he believes wardship works.
- Problem 3: The "stability index built from missed payments and address changes" is named but never shown operating. Who computes it, from which data, and can a citizen see or contest their own inputs? Why it matters: This is where technology goes magic for me. An opaque score with no described data source reads as a plot device, not a system. Suggested fix: Add one sentence on the mechanism: the index is pulled from bank and tenancy registers, citizens see the total but not the inputs, and Guardians supply the address data they also profit from.
- Problem 4: Book 4, "Ines's renewal comes back failed", is the expected turn for a former Ward, and Ines herself is the familiar scrappy advocate with a family wound and a shabby office. Why it matters: I guessed the last book from the logline. "Exact, dry, funniest when furious" tells me her voice but not a choice she gets wrong. Suggested fix: Give her a compromising act earlier: in book 2 or 3 she signs something for a Ward that she should not, and that signature, not Lakehead's retaliation alone, is what fails her renewal.
- Reminded them of: I, Daniel Blake; Severance (Ling Ma's office dread, not the show's sci-fi); The Handmaid's Tale (the bank-account scene); Infomocracy; Better Call Saul (elder-law and Sandpiper arc); Papers, Please

**Rewrite: accepted (slight).** It ran over the length limit and was trimmed by a line editor before the gate.

- Changed: Running thread: the postcode marks are now a lawful actuarial schedule Lakehead lobbied for openly and nobody published, not a bribe (both critics' most generic element).
- Changed: Rennick is now a believer who helped write the first elder-protection rules and signs for his own father; Teo is loyal and good at his job, with a Ward mother who is doing well, and no longer leaks or gets fired. Ines's evidence comes from records requests, ledgers and misfiled forms.
- Changed: Signing moved to the center: the logline opens on losing your signature, and each book has a signing act (Lena's flour, Walt's deed, Jun's test sheet, Ines's own filing).
- Changed: Ines gets a compromising act: she signs Walt's deed in his name in book 2, Teo finds it in book 3, and it is what fails her renewal in book 4 (superfan: book 4 was guessable, lead had no wrong choice).
- Changed: Stability index tied to the port and given a mechanism: bank records plus address changes reported by Guardians, with winter layoffs and boarding-house moves costing dock families points. Dot Okafor's rent in ironing and gossip restored; Lakehead's lease review and audit detail in book 2 cut for length.
- Declined: Saying that citizens see their total but not the inputs: one more mechanism sentence than the page can carry at this length.
- Declined: Making the postcode marks follow the shipping calendar explicitly: the winter-layoff sentence already makes the port causal, and the thread line needed to stay short.
- Declined: Restoring the 'Ines is good company' line and 'one good jacket': no room under the word limit after the originality changes.
- For the author to decide: Ines now signs a deed in a Ward's name in book 2 and still wins her own appeal in book 4 after admitting it. Decide whether that win should carry a lasting penalty (for example, losing her advocate standing for a time) so it does not read as too easy.
- For the author to decide: Teo is now the loyal officer who finds the deed. Decide whether he stays an opponent through book 4 or shifts after seeing the postcode schedule; the pitch leaves it open.

**Gate (editor, blind A/B)** preferred the rewrite (slight). A's books cause each other: the deed Ines signs in book 2 is found in 3 and fails her renewal in 4. Its lawful, unpublished postcode schedule is fresher than B's paid-for corruption. Rennick signing for his father, the dock crews' winter moves and Dot add character and warmth. What the losing version did better: B's logline is clearer ("takes one failed citizen per book"). Its client ages, the Blue Drum lease under review, and the closing line about the advocate who lost her own key are worth restoring. A's "nobody breaks the law" contradicts Ines's deed.
