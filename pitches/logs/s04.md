# s04: The World-Sitters

Revision log. Genre: Science fiction (space exploration). Target readers: Men and women 45–70.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The World-Sitters. A protected world is closed to everyone except two resident wardens, posted for one local year and then relieved. The Reserve Service cannot pay much, so it recruits retired field scientists; people call them world-sitters. Leads: June Iwasaki, 66, field ecologist, and Walt Iwasaki, 68, mining geologist, married forty-one years: the only two human beings on each planet they keep. The work is counting, mending, noticing and writing it down. Each book is one posting on one world, with one marvel that made it a reserve (a reef that grows under the ice once in thirty years; a forest that walks eight meters a year; a night singer no instrument has recorded) and one thing that should not be there: a prospector's drone, a tourist yacht with a dead transponder, a line of bootprints, a silence where the dawn chorus used to be. With no help closer than six weeks, they work out what is happening and stop it, using field science, patience and four decades of finishing each other's arguments; the grandchildren's messages arrive in batches. No sapient aliens: animals, plants and stranger things, behaving as living things do. Each book delivers one real wonder and one real danger and ends when the relief ship comes. Thread: at twenty-four June saw something on her first survey that nobody believed, and it cost her a career; she picks each posting for what it can tell her about where to look, and on the fourth world she finds it.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> Earlier versions of this seed were audited. Found: Becky Chambers, To Be Taught, If Fortunate (a humane ecological survey of four worlds); John Scalzi, Fuzzy Nation, after H. Beam Piper's Little Fuzzy (mining rights hinge on certified native life); Alan Dean Foster, Voyage to the City of the Dead (married scientists' expedition); Janet Kagan, Mirabile (an older biologist solving ecological puzzles, from memory). 'The Life List' is taken as a title and has already been dropped. The seed has already been rebuilt around a retired couple posted as the only two wardens on a protected world. One search on that version found no matching novel; the nearest results were Elizabeth Moon, Remnant Population (an elderly woman alone on a colony world) and Jack Vance's Cadwal novels (human Conservators of a planet kept as a natural preserve). Never let a case turn on sentience.

### Round 2: Originality

**originality-auditor.** Familiar preserve-planet frame and stock trespassers, lifted by a custodial two-warden job, retired married leads and a thirty-year "mirage" grudge; shared tropes, no cloned premise. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 7, originality 7, engine 7, character 8, setting 6, fit 9, clarity 8.

- Keep: The custodial job itself: "the only two people on each closed planet they keep for one local year", with "a shelf of paper logbooks going back decades"; Walt, "who has never said whether he believes her", and Strand, who wrote "mirage" and now reviews her file: the thread is personal, not cosmic; Book 2's cause: "a greenhouse vine a warden let loose forty years ago" — the harm comes from the wardens' own predecessors, found in the logbooks
- Problem 1: Most generic element: the outside villain. "A prospector's drone drilling it" (book 1) and "a collector trapping a singer" (book 3) are the stock poacher and miner of every preserve-world story. Why it matters: It is the piece this shelf has met most often (Fuzzy Nation, Cadwal, any park-ranger procedural), and it opens the series on its least distinctive foot. Suggested fix: Keep one intruder at most. Make book 1's threat custodial: a previous warden's sensor string or ice-borehole, logged as routine, is letting light in early and mistiming the bloom. The drone becomes a clue, not the antagonist.
- Problem 2: Most distinctive element, the paper logbooks and the chain of earlier warden pairs, drives only book 2. Elsewhere the station is "a prefab hut, a greenhouse, a skiff", which is generic. Why it matters: Solving a planet by reading forty years of other couples' handwriting is the device no listed comparator has; at present it reads as set dressing. Suggested fix: Make the logbooks the method in every book: each case is cracked by a discrepancy between an old entry and June's own count. Say so in the series engine, and let June's twenty-four-year-old entry sit on Selah's shelf.
- Problem 3: The preserve-planet frame, "sixty-three living worlds closed to settlement, mining and tourism" under a "Reserve Service", sits close to Vance's Cadwal Conservancy (from memory) and reads as a generic national-park-in-space. Why it matters: It is a shared trope, not a shared premise, but the pitch gives the Service no rule of its own beyond two wardens and a year. Suggested fix: Give the Service one odd, specific rule that generates plot: wardens may remove nothing and add nothing, so every fix must be done with what earlier wardens left behind, and every intervention is itself a logged violation.
- Problem 4: The "threads climb out of sight" payoff is undefined, and a lone scientist vindicated before the doubter is a familiar shape; books 1 and 2 reduce it to collectibles ("finds one thread", "bags a second thread"). Why it matters: A mystery object with no ecological logic risks reading as generic wonder, and a reader may suspect a sentience reveal the brief forbids. Suggested fix: State in the pitch what kind of answer it is: a dispersal event of a salt-lake organism on a decades-long cycle, tying it to book 1's thirty-year tide. Each book's thread then adds a measured fact, not a souvenir.
- Reminded them of: Jack Vance, Cadwal Chronicles (from memory): planet held as a preserve by resident Conservators; shared setting trope, different scale and tone; Elizabeth Moon, Remnant Population: elderly lead alone on a world; shared age, different premise (sapient aliens there); Becky Chambers, To Be Taught, If Fortunate: humane survey of one world after another; shared tone and structure; Janet Kagan, Mirabile (from memory): older biologist solving ecological puzzles episodically; closest match to the engine; John Scalzi, Fuzzy Nation / Piper, Little Fuzzy: prospector versus protected life; resembles book 1's drone plot only; Nevada Barr, Anna Pigeon mysteries (from memory): a ranger, one park per book; the same series shape in another genre
- Search note: No web search this round. All resemblance judgments beyond the supplied audit are from memory and are marked as such; titles and the "world-sitter" term still need a web check in the later round.
