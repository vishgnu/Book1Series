# s03: The Riding Crew

Revision log. Genre: Near-future science fiction techno-thriller. Target readers: Men 35–65.

## Where it started

**Seed idea** (written by the series developer before any drafting):

> Working title: The Riding Crew. 2041. Thousands of cargo ships now cross the oceans with nobody aboard, watched from control rooms ashore. When one stops answering (dark, drifting, on fire, or steaming somewhere it was never sent) the law of the sea still wants a human master aboard before she comes near a coast. That job goes to riding crews: a handful of the last deep-sea sailors, winched onto ships built without a bridge, bunks or handrails, to bring them in by hand. Lead: Iker Etxeberria, 46, riding master, from a Basque fishing family, patient with steel, short with fools. With him: Mirren Tait, 31, engineer, who can make a locked control system take orders from a laptop and a crowbar; Adaeze Nwosu, 55, a former harbor pilot who can dock anything that floats; Teo, 22, a deckhand who has never sailed on a crewed ship; and whichever owner's observer is sent along to watch the money. Each book is one ship and one question: why did a ship with no crew do that? An ore carrier steaming slow circles in the South Atlantic. A container ship dark off Madagascar with her cold boxes alarming. A tanker locked by ransomware and still making fifteen knots toward a strait. They have to answer it while keeping her afloat: seamanship, physics, weather and deadlines do the rest. Thread: the Halcyon Dawn, a 300-meter ore carrier that left Brazil crewless two years ago, never arrived, and keeps being sighted under dead ships' names; in book four they board her. Technology that is real or nearly real, explained plainly; a crew that is good at its work.

**Originality audit of the seed** (web searches, 15 November 2026):

> An earlier version of this seed (a small salvage firm bidding against giants) was too close to Owen Laukkanen, Gale Force (a small family salvage tug against a big rival, an abandoned car carrier, a naval architect aboard), Corridan and Waid, Goliath, and Wilbur Smith, Hungry as the Sea. Also found: Sulagna Chaudhury, Dark Traverse (an automated ship hijacked remotely). The seed has already been rebuilt around a riding crew that boards crewless ships, with no salvage-firm rivalry and no car carrier. One search on the riding-crew version found no matching novel; the nearest result was 'The Last Engineer', a piece about one man's last voyage aboard an autonomous cargo ship.

**First draft:** “The Riding Crew”, 510 words.

### Round 5: World and logic

**logic-checker.** Strong, fresh premise with a real story-generating rule, but the Halcyon Dawn thread, the liability money and the hand-pump mechanics would not survive a mariner's first question. Would read on: yes. Scores: hook 8, originality 8, engine 8, character 7, setting 7, fit 9, clarity 6.

- Keep: The stated rule: a silent ship "may not come within twelve miles of any coast until a licensed human master is aboard and answerable for her"; "the only weapon is a signature" with a cast displaced by the same automation (last captain, the controller who took his helm, a pilot ended by remote docking); One ship, one question, a hard deadline of "weather, fuel or a coastline", solved with "satellite links, ballast and towlines"
- Problem 1: The running thread contradicts itself: the Halcyon Dawn "never arrived" two years ago, yet has "every landfall signed". A laden 300-meter ship cannot stay lost, fuelled and making port calls in 2041. Why it matters: Satellite radar and port records exist. The series-long mystery rests on something a sharp reader rejects in book one, so the book-four answer cannot feel fair. Suggested fix: Make her hidden in plain sight: renamed and re-registered, trading under a cloned identity with landfalls signed on Iker's license. The mystery becomes which of nine thousand hulls she is, and who signs.
- Problem 2: "Each signature pledges his house in Bermeo against hull, cargo and coastline." A house is a rounding error against an LNG carrier, and insurers, salvage awards and liability limits are never mentioned. Why it matters: The money does not follow through. Nobody would accept that security or sign on those terms, and a charterer who "claims Iker's house" reads as arbitrary. Suggested fix: State the rule: the convention requires a personal bond from the signing master, with the house as his posted surety, plus criminal liability for pollution. Add one line on who pays the crew: insurers, per ship landed.
- Problem 3: Ships have "one manual control: a hand pump at the steering gear" and no bridge, yet the crew brings her in "by hand". No engine control or conning position; "winched aboard" mid-ocean exceeds helicopter range. Why it matters: Hand-pumping a 300-meter ship's rudder is minutes per helm order with no view ahead. This is the core competence the series sells, and it must work. Suggested fix: Give every hull a mandated riding station: local engine control, the hand pump and a deck conning point with a portable nav kit the crew carries. Board from a named salvage tug that also holds the towline.
- Problem 4: No rule says who may command a ship: orders arrive "under the Halcyon Dawn's dead number" and two control rooms fight one rudder. Also, LNG carriers routinely burn cargo boil-off as fuel. Why it matters: A solvable puzzle needs a known authentication rule. Without one, forged orders are magic, and book one's oddity is normal practice to anyone who knows gas ships. Suggested fix: State it once: a ship obeys whoever holds her registered control key, and keys of scrapped or lost ships should be revoked. Make book one's oddity forced boil-off, emptying her tanks on purpose.
