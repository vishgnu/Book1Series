# The Closed Worlds

**Genre:** Science fiction (space exploration) · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete posting per book

**Logline.** June Iwasaki, 66, ecologist, and Walt, 68, mining geologist, are world-sitters: the only two people on each closed planet, for one local year. Each year the logbooks and June's count disagree.

**The setting.** The Reserve Service keeps sixty-three living worlds closed, each with a hut, sealed stores and a shelf of paper logbooks. Nothing in, nothing out. The relief ship is six weeks away; messages go monthly. Any departure from standing orders is logged as a breach; a fourth ends their postings unless the review board strikes one.

**The lead.** At twenty-four June watched threads climb from a lifeless salt lake. Her recorder caught nothing, her chief wrote "mirage," and she taught thirty years. She wants one more look, with a witness, yet trusts no count but her own. She talks like a field note: "Reef light down 40 percent since Walt's repair. The repair is excellent."

**The cast.** Walt, forty-one years married, won't sign for what he hasn't measured, so has never said whether he believes her; he answers in questions: "Forty percent of what?" Odalys Brandt, 52, postings officer, steers June to unaudited logbooks, without leave, before the Service pulps the paper. Emil Strand, 79, wrote "mirage" because the recorder was blank and chairs the review board.

**The series engine.** Every book is one posting and one marvel. The answer lies in decades of other couples' handwriting, checked against rock, water and June's tally. Each book one of them is wrong, and each leaves one lead toward June's lake.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *The Thirty-Year Reef* — Orrin's under-ice reef blooms nine years early; Walt seals the tide crack as ordered, rock growth bands prove early blooms normal, and he dives to reopen it: breach one.
2. *The Walking Forest* — Tamsin's forest walks after groundwater, rooting ahead, and has stopped; June waters one grove as a control, it never seeds, and the board rewrites the order: no breach.
3. *The Night Singer* — Calder's logbooks describe a night chorus no recorder holds; June finds the recorder filters song as noise, rewires it, and refuses a collector's permit: breaches two and three.
4. *The Quiet Basin* — On Selah, June's salt lake, Walt's levels show a survey well draining it; they cap it before Strand: breach four.

*Running thread:* whether anyone else can be shown what June saw. A warden's entry, "threads at dawn," points to Selah, and Walt asks what her threads looked like. Book 4: June lets Walt's numbers stand, and three people watch dormant brine life hatch and leave on silk, once in forty-two years. Strand still calls "mirage" right for a blank recorder, and strikes the breach: the well was the Service's own.

**Why readers come back.** A new world, a marriage that argues well, a puzzle solved with evidence to a finished ending, and a breach count that never moves when expected.

**What makes it different.** A couple in their sixties doing custodial work: counting, mending, writing it down. Nothing gets conquered, settled or shot.
