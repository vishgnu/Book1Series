# The World-Sitters

**Genre:** Science fiction (space exploration) · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete posting per book

**Logline.** June Iwasaki, 66, ecologist, and her husband Walt, 68, mining geologist, are world-sitters: the only two people on each closed planet, for one local year. Each year the logbooks and June's count disagree.

**The setting.** The Reserve Service keeps sixty-three living worlds closed to settlement, mining and tourism. Each has a hut, a skiff and a shelf of paper logbooks. The rule: nothing brought in, nothing taken out. The relief ship is six weeks away, and every fix is logged as a breach under their names. Four breaches mean the review board.

**The lead.** At twenty-four June watched threads climb from a lifeless salt lake. Her recorder caught nothing, her chief wrote "mirage," and she taught for thirty years. She wants one more look, with a witness, yet trusts no count but her own, Walt's included. She counts twice and talks like a field note: dry, exact, funny.

**The cast.** Walt, forty-one years married, found ore for companies that dug it out. He won't sign for what he hasn't measured, so has never said whether he believes her. Odalys Brandt, 52, postings officer, risks her post sending June to logbooks nobody audits. Granddaughter Maren, 11, mails tallies to be checked. Emil Strand, 79, wrote "mirage" because the recorder was blank, would again, and chairs her review board.

**The series engine.** Every book is one posting: one marvel, and one place where the logbooks and June's count disagree. Walt mends, June waits and counts, and each book one of them is wrong.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *The Thirty-Year Reef* — Under Orrin's ice a reef grows once in thirty years, now blooming nine years early. Walt shutters the tide crack, breach one; it blooms on. He reads growth bands in the rock: every third cycle runs early.
2. *The Walking Forest* — Tamsin's forest walks eight meters a year after groundwater, and has stopped. The Service orders it watered; the logbooks show it stopped once before, to seed. They wait, breach two, and it seeds. Walt asks what her threads looked like.
3. *The Night Singer* — Calder's logbooks describe a night chorus no recorder holds; a collector lands with a permit for one singer. Walt's tally shows one missing voice silences the chorus; June proves the Service recorder filters the song as noise and refuses the permit, breach three.
4. *The Quiet Basin* — On Selah a warden wrote "threads at dawn" forty-two years before June. The lake must brim by dawn; Walt's levels show a forgotten Service well bleeding it. June lets his numbers stand. They cap it, breach four, and Strand, come to close her file, stands beside Walt as the threads rise.

*Running thread:* what June saw at twenty-four, and whether anyone else can be shown it. Book 4: two witnesses, "mirage" struck, and a name: the lake's dormant life leaving on silk, once in forty-two years. Eleven lakes unchecked.

**Why readers come back.** A new world every book. Two people in charge of a planet, and a marriage that argues well. A puzzle solved with evidence and a finished ending.

**What makes it different.** Retired grandparents doing custodial work: counting, mending, writing it down. Each planet is solved by reading decades of other couples' handwriting. Nothing gets conquered, settled or shot.
