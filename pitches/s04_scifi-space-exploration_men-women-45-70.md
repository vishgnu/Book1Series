# The World-Sitters

**Genre:** Science fiction (space exploration) · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete posting per book

**Logline.** June Iwasaki, 66, ecologist, and husband Walt, 68, mining geologist, are world-sitters: the only two people on each closed planet they keep for one local year. Each year brings a drone or a bootprint, and one more chance to prove what her chief logged as "mirage."

**The setting.** The Reserve Service keeps sixty-three living worlds closed to settlement, mining and tourism. Each has one station: a prefab hut, a greenhouse, a skiff and a shelf of paper logbooks going back decades. The rule: two wardens, one local year, nobody else on the ground. Help is six weeks away, so any drone, hull, bootprint or broken ankle is theirs to deal with.

**The lead.** June Iwasaki, 66, field ecologist. At twenty-four she watched threads climb out of sight from a lifeless salt lake. Her recorder caught nothing, her chief wrote "mirage," and she taught for thirty years instead. She wants one more look, with a witness, and picks postings for where they point. She counts twice and talks like a field note: dry, exact, funny.

**The cast.** Walt Iwasaki, her husband of forty-one years, who has never said whether he believes her. Odalys Brandt, 52, postings officer, who gives June the worlds nobody wants. Maren, 11, the granddaughter whose messages arrive in batches. Emil Strand, 79, who wrote "mirage" and chairs the board that signs permits and reviews her file.

**The series engine.** Every book is one posting: one marvel that earned the reserve, one thing going wrong with it (a trespasser, an old warden's mistake, the Service's own paperwork), and two people with a rock hammer and a year. They measure, argue, test and stop it without a shot fired; then the relief ship lands. The Service has more reserves than wardens, so another world is always open.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *The Thirty-Year Reef* — Under Orrin's ice a reef builds only when a thirty-year tide cracks light in; they stop a prospector's drone drilling it mid-bloom, and June finds one thread.
2. *The Walking Forest* — Tamsin's forest walks eight meters a year after retreating groundwater, and has stopped; the logbooks lead to a greenhouse vine a warden let loose forty years ago, and they clear it. Walt bags a second thread.
3. *The Night Singer* — On Calder bootprints lead to a collector trapping a singer no instrument has recorded, on a permit Strand's board signed; they free it on Walt's wrecked knee, and June's file reopens.
4. *The Quiet Basin* — On Selah the dawn chorus stopped when its salt lake began draining; they cap a forgotten Service well, and Strand, landing early to close the file, stands beside Walt as the threads rise.

*Running thread:* what June saw rise from a lifeless salt lake at twenty-four; book 4 answers it in front of two witnesses, with eleven lakes unchecked.

**Why readers come back.** A new world to walk every book, seen through two people who know what they are looking at. A marriage that works. A puzzle solved with evidence and a finished ending. Leads the reader's own age, in charge of a planet.

**What makes it different.** The heroes are retired grandparents, and the job is custodial: counting, mending, noticing, writing it down. Nothing gets conquered, settled or shot.
