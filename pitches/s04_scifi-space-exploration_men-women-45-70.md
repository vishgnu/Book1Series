# The World-Sitters

**Genre:** Science fiction (space exploration) · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete posting per book

**Logline.** June Iwasaki, 66, ecologist, and her husband Walt, 68, mining geologist, are world-sitters: the only two people on each closed planet they keep for one local year. Every year, something in the old wardens' logbooks does not match what June counts.

**The setting.** The Reserve Service keeps sixty-three living worlds closed to settlement, mining and tourism. Each has one station: a hut, a skiff and a shelf of paper logbooks. The rule: two wardens, one local year, nothing brought in, nothing taken out. Help is six weeks away, and every fix is logged as a breach under their own names.

**The lead.** At twenty-four June watched threads climb out of sight from a lifeless salt lake at dawn. Her recorder caught nothing, her chief wrote "mirage," and she taught for thirty years instead. She still picks postings for where they point. She counts twice and talks like a field note: dry, exact, funny.

**The cast.** Walt, her husband of forty-one years, who has never said whether he believes her. Odalys Brandt, 52, postings officer, who gives June the worlds nobody wants. Maren, 11, the granddaughter whose messages arrive in batches. Emil Strand, 79, who wrote "mirage" because the recorder was blank, and would again.

**The series engine.** Every book is one posting: one marvel, and one place where the old logbooks and June's count disagree. Sometimes an earlier warden caused it, sometimes the planet did. Two people with a rock hammer and a year measure, argue and decide whether to touch it; then the relief ship lands. The Service has more reserves than wardens, so another world is always open.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *The Thirty-Year Reef* — Under Orrin's ice a reef grows once in thirty years. It is blooming early; an old warden's borehole is the leak, and they plug it. June weighs one thread.
2. *The Walking Forest* — Tamsin's forest walks eight meters a year after groundwater, and has stopped. The Service orders it watered; the logbooks show it stopped once before, to seed. They wait. Walt starts looking too.
3. *The Night Singer* — On Calder bootprints lead to a collector trapping a singer no instrument has recorded, on a permit issued in error; they free it on Walt's wrecked knee, and Strand strikes "mirage."
4. *The Quiet Basin* — On Selah, where June's first entry sits on the shelf, the dawn chorus stopped as the salt lake drained; they cap a forgotten Service well, and Strand stands beside Walt as the threads rise.

*Running thread:* what June saw at twenty-four; book 4 names it before two witnesses: the salt lake's dormant life leaving on silk, once in forty-two years. Eleven lakes unchecked.

**Why readers come back.** A new world every book, through expert eyes. A marriage that works. A puzzle solved with evidence and a finished ending. Leads the reader's own age, in charge of a planet.

**What makes it different.** The heroes are retired grandparents; the job is custodial: counting, mending, writing it down. Each planet is solved by reading decades of other couples' handwriting. Nothing gets conquered, settled or shot.
