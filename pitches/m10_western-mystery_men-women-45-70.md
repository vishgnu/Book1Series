# The Camas Fork Mysteries

**Genre:** Western mystery · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** Marty Voss, 54, holds the keys to three hundred headgates along her 24 miles of Idaho canal. In every book, water turns up where it should not be, and someone dies over it.

**The setting.** Harrow County, Idaho, on the Snake River Plain: potato cellars, beet fields and dairies laid over black lava rock, nine inches of rain a year. Nothing grows without the Camas Fork, a river split among fourteen ditches, the oldest dug in 1884. The rule is older than the state: first in time, first in right. In a short year a call on the river dries up junior fields first.

**The lead.** Marty Voss, 54, was a deputy in the 2001 drought when she served the shutoff that cost a neighbor his farm; his family still farms on her section. She rides ditch now for the honest numbers. She has no badge, only the right to lock a headgate, and every gate belongs to a shareholder she has known thirty years. She reads a wet bank like a crime scene and states the measurement before the opinion.

**The cast.** Sheriff Caleb Ortega, 36, who works the deaths and wants her kept to the water; Dale Hinckley, 61, district watermaster, keeper of the priority list; Ruth Aldecoa, 67, third-generation potato grower on the oldest ditch; Gene Voss, 58, Marty's brother, farming under the newest; Dr. Priya Nair, 38, a hydrologist whose well data the old families distrust.

**The series engine.** Each book opens with water that is not where the delivery sheet says: a headgate chained open, a flume shaved half an inch, a pond that fills at night. The death is Ortega's case; the water is Marty's, and each break comes from what only a ditch rider may lawfully do: measure, lock, sign the log, testify. Cases keep coming because every summer runs short and she sees every back gate.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *First in Time* — April: a farmer, 71, dies in his potato cellar while his headgate runs open four miles off, Marty's padlock cut.
2. *Call on the River* — July: a call shuts the newest ditches, Marty locks out her brother Gene, and a shaved flume turns water theft into a killing.
3. *Beneficial Use* — Harvest: a dairy pond filling at night, water claimed for acres never planted, and bones in the silt from Marty's 2001 shutoff.
4. *Use It or Lose It* — Winter: a farm's unused water goes to court, a lawyer dies in the records vault, and Marty's lock log dates the missing ledger.

*Running thread:* The 1911 decree ranked 214 rights from a surveyor's ledger no court has seen since. Pages surface in books 1–3; book 4 finds enough to reopen one priority date, Ruth Aldecoa's ditch or Gene Voss's, before next April's water.

**Why readers come back.** A complete, fair mystery every time: readers see each gauge reading when Marty does and can solve it beside her. One valley through a full year, and smart working people who know their ground. No cliffhangers.

**What makes it different.** The detective is a ditch rider whose only power is a padlock on a headgate. Her evidence is cubic feet per second in her own lock log, and every suspect's place in line was fixed in 1911.
