# The Camas Fork Mysteries

**Genre:** Western mystery · **Readers:** Men and women 45–70 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** Marty Voss, 54, a former deputy, is the woman who opens and locks every farmer's water gate along her 24 miles of Idaho canal. In every book, water turns up where it should not be, and someone dies over it.

**The setting.** Harrow County, Idaho: potato cellars, beet fields and dairies laid over black lava rock, nine inches of rain a year. Nothing grows without the Camas Fork, a river split among fourteen ditches, the oldest dug in 1884. The rule is older than the state: first in time, first in right. In a short year the oldest ditch is filled in full before a newer one gets a drop.

**The lead.** Marty Voss, 54, a sheriff's deputy for 22 years, quit after a shooting the review board cleared and she has not; the man's family still farms on her section. She rides ditch now for the honest numbers. She has no badge, only the right to lock a headgate, and every gate she locks belongs to someone she has known thirty years. She reads a wet bank like a crime scene and states the measurement before the opinion.

**The cast.** Sheriff Caleb Ortega, 36, her former trainee, who needs her eye and resents it; Dale Hinckley, 61, district watermaster, keeper of the priority list; Ruth Aldecoa, 67, third-generation potato grower on the oldest ditch; Gene Voss, 58, Marty's brother, farming under the newest; Dr. Priya Nair, 38, a hydrologist whose well data the old families distrust.

**The series engine.** Each book opens with water that is not where the delivery sheet says: a headgate chained open, a flume shaved half an inch, a pond that fills at night. Marty follows it to a death and to who needed the water. Cases keep coming because every summer runs short and she sees every back gate.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *First in Time* — April: a headgate chained open and a 71-year-old farmer drowned below it put Marty back beside the sheriff.
2. *Call on the River* — July: the watermaster shuts off the newest ditches, his deputy dies, and a shaved flume costs Marty a friend.
3. *Beneficial Use* — Harvest: a dairy pond filling at night, water claimed for acres never planted, and bones in the silt from the 2001 drought.
4. *Use It or Lose It* — Winter: a farm's unused water goes to court, a lawyer lies dead in the dry canal, and the missing ledger is entered as evidence.

*Running thread:* The 1911 decree ranked 214 rights from a surveyor's ledger no court has seen since; book 4 finds it and settles whether Ruth Aldecoa's ditch or Gene Voss's came first, with next April's water still to deliver.

**Why readers come back.** A complete, fair mystery every time: readers see each gauge reading when Marty does and can solve it beside her. One valley through a full year, March wind to first frost, and smart working people who know their ground. No cliffhangers.

**What makes it different.** The detective is a ditch rider whose only power is a padlock on a headgate. Her evidence is measured in cubic feet per second, and every ditch's place in line was fixed in 1911.
