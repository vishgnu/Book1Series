# The Right to Sign

**Genre:** Dystopian science fiction · **Readers:** Women and men 25–55 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In the Lakes Commonwealth, adulthood is a license renewed by exam. Fail, and a Guardian company holds your bank account, lease and front-door key. Ines Calder, 37, a former Ward, takes one failed citizen per book and has thirty days to win it back.

**The setting.** Meridian is a freshwater port: ore boats, grain elevators, a Guardian storefront on every third block. The Assessment began thirty years ago as fraud protection for the elderly; now every citizen sits it every five years: money, civics, and a stability index built from missed payments and address changes. Fail, and you are a Ward. Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign. Lawyers are barred from tribunals; lay advocates argue appeals.

**The lead.** Ines Calder, 37, was a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four. She takes appeals from the back office of the Blue Drum laundromat. She wants each client to leave able to sign; it costs her sleep and rent, and she has lost her brother's appeal twice. Wardship taught her which form gets filed out of order. Exact, dry, funniest when furious.

**The cast.** Paul Rennick, 54, runs Lakehead Guardian, which bills monthly per Ward. Teo Brandt, 29, Lakehead case officer, leaks schedules; his mother is a Ward. Tribunal chair Marian Voss, 58, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Sam Calder, 41, her brother, still a Ward: he scored 73.

**The series engine.** Every book opens with a failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Ines learns why this person failed and who gains, then argues the appeal in a room built to say no. The verdict lands inside the book, and Lakehead answers every win. New clients keep arriving: one citizen in nine fails.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Short Count* — Lena Marsh, 44, a baker with dyscalculia on a rezoned block; Ines wins by reading the local pass mark, 74 not 70, into the record; Lakehead audits her standing.
2. *Record Copy* — Walt Deering, 71, a union archivist, failed memory while holding files Lakehead wants; Ines loses the appeal, saves the archive; the Blue Drum's lease goes under review.
3. *Refusal to Sit* — Jun Pell, 22, a coder, will not take the test; Ines wins a narrow ruling that puts raw score sheets on record, Sam's 73 among them; Teo is fired.
4. *Her Own Hand* — Ines's renewal comes back failed; unable to sign her own filing, she argues her appeal, puts the postcode marks on record, and Sam signs his own lease.

*Running thread:* Why the pass mark moves by postcode, and who profits; book 4 proves Lakehead paid for it, and the Assessment stands with one published mark.

**Why readers come back.** Each book is a full case: a person you want to see keep their keys, a paper-trail investigation, a hearing with a verdict. No cliffhangers. Every win or loss changes how Meridian treats the next client, and what Lakehead does to Ines.

**What makes it different.** The dystopia is administrative. No uprising, no chosen rescuer, no bloodshed: the weapons are a lease, a score sheet and a filing deadline. The tribunal has no lawyers, and the advocate once lost her own key to it.
