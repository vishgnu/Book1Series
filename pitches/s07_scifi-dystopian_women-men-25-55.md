# The Right to Sign

**Genre:** Dystopian science fiction · **Readers:** Women and men 25–55 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In the Lakes Commonwealth, adulthood is a license renewed by exam. Ines Calder, 37, a former Ward turned lay advocate, takes one failed citizen per book, with thirty days to win back their signature.

**The setting.** Meridian is a freshwater port: ore boats, grain elevators, a Guardian storefront on every third block. Every five years each citizen sits the Assessment: money, civics, a stability index. Fail, and you are a Ward: a Guardian company holds your bank account, lease and front-door key. Wards can work and marry. They cannot sign. Lawyers are barred from tribunals; lay advocates argue appeals.

**The lead.** Ines Calder, 37, was a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four. She takes appeals from the back office of the Blue Drum laundromat. She wants each client to leave able to sign; it costs her sleep and rent. Wardship taught her which form gets filed out of order. Exact, dry, funniest when furious. One good jacket.

**The cast.** Dot Okafor, 62, owns the laundromat and takes rent in ironing and gossip. Teo Brandt, 29, Guardian case officer, leaks schedules. Tribunal chair Marian Voss, 58, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Sam Calder, 41, her brother, still a Ward.

**The series engine.** Every book opens with a failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Ines learns why this person failed and who gains, through leases and score sheets, then argues the appeal in a room built to say no. The verdict lands inside the book. New clients keep arriving: one citizen in nine fails.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Short Count* — Lena Marsh, 44, a baker with dyscalculia on a rezoned block; Ines wins and notes the local pass mark was 74, not 70.
2. *Record Copy* — Walt Deering, 71, a union archivist, failed memory while holding files a Guardian firm wants; Ines loses the appeal, saves the archive.
3. *Refusal to Sit* — Jun Pell, 22, a coder, will not take the test; Ines wins a narrow ruling that puts raw score sheets on record.
4. *Her Own Hand* — Ines's renewal comes back failed; a Ward again, unable to sign her own filing, she argues her appeal and puts the postcode marks on record.

*Running thread:* Why the pass mark moves by postcode, and who profits; book 4 proves it, and the Assessment stands with one published mark.

**Why readers come back.** Each book is a full case: a person you want to see keep their keys, a paper-trail investigation, a hearing with a verdict. No cliffhangers. Ines is good company, and every win or loss changes how Meridian treats the next client.

**What makes it different.** The dystopia is administrative. No uprising, no chosen rescuer, no bloodshed: the weapons are a lease, a score sheet and a filing deadline. The tribunal has no lawyers, and the advocate once lost her own key to it.
