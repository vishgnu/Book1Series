# The Right to Sign

**Genre:** Dystopian science fiction · **Readers:** Women and men 25–55 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In the Lakes Commonwealth, adulthood is a license renewed by exam. Fail, and you cannot sign your name: a Guardian company signs for your bank account, lease and front door. Former Ward Ines Calder takes one failed citizen per book.

**The setting.** Meridian is an ore port. The Assessment began as elder fraud protection; lenders gave passers cheaper credit, now everyone sits it every five years: a test, sworn declarations and a stability index built from bank records and Guardian-reported address changes. Dock crews change boarding houses each winter; every move costs points.

**The lead.** Ines Calder, 37, takes appeals behind the Blue Drum laundromat. At nineteen she admitted a missed payment and spent five years a Ward while her Guardian sold her mother's house. She wants her brother's signature back more than he does, and never concedes. Exact, dry, funniest when furious.

**The cast.** Sam Calder, 41, crane mechanic, a Ward, told her not to file a third time and still fixes her dryers. Teo Brandt, 29, Lakehead case officer, believes rules protect his Ward mother. Tribunal chair Marian Voss, 58, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Paul Rennick, 54, runs Lakehead Guardian and signs for his own father.

**The series engine.** Every book opens with a failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Scores are sealed, a ruling binds one case, and a third lost appeal is permanent. Ines learns why this person failed and who bills them, then argues the appeal. One citizen in nine fails.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Short Count* — Lena Marsh, 44, baker with dyscalculia, fails while Lakehead lists her shop for sale; Ines wins by putting Lena's sheet on record: 71, failed at 74, not the gazetted 70. Lakehead pleads clerical error.
2. *Record Copy* — Walt Deering, 71, union archivist, fails memory and sleeps beside the fee ledgers Rennick wants; Ines loses the appeal but saves the archive by witnessing his deed of gift to the union, back-dated one day.
3. *Raw Score* — Cal Hruska, 46, deckhand, passed the test and failed on winter moves between Lakehead boarding houses; Voss orders his postcode's sheets disclosed, all held to 74, Sam's 73 among them.
4. *Her Own Hand* — Teo reports the back-dated deed, by the book, and Ines fails her renewal. Then, finding his mother's postcode in Lakehead's fee tables, he hands them to Ines. She concedes the deed, her first admission since nineteen, and takes a year's probation; Voss orders the postcode schedule published; Sam signs his own lease.

*Running thread:* Who moves the pass mark by postcode; book 4 finds a schedule passed in open session, never gazetted, priced on Guardian fee income. The Assessment stands with one published mark, 72, a council compromise; 1,400 Wards sign again.

**Why readers come back.** Each book gives someone you want to keep their keys, a paper trail and a verdict, and moves Sam and Ines one conversation closer.

**What makes it different.** The dystopia is administrative and lawful; the one rule broken is the heroine's. The advocate who returns other people's keys loses her own.
