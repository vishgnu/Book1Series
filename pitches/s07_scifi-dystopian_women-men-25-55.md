# The Right to Sign

**Genre:** Dystopian science fiction · **Readers:** Women and men 25–55 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In the Lakes Commonwealth, adulthood is a license renewed by exam. Fail, and you cannot sign your name: a Guardian company signs for your bank account, lease and front door. Ines Calder, a former Ward, gets thirty days per book to win one back.

**The setting.** Meridian is a freshwater port of ore boats and Guardian storefronts. The Assessment began as elder fraud protection; now every citizen sits it every five years, scored partly on a stability index built from bank records and Guardian-reported address changes. Each winter laid-off dock crews move to boarding houses, and every move costs points. Those who fail, Wards, can work and marry but not sign. Lawyers are barred; lay advocates argue appeals.

**The lead.** Ines Calder, 37, was a Ward from nineteen to twenty-four. She takes appeals from the back of the Blue Drum laundromat, where Dot Okafor takes rent in ironing and gossip. The work costs her sleep and savings; she has lost her brother's appeal twice. Wardship taught her which form gets filed out of order. Exact, dry, funniest when furious.

**The cast.** Paul Rennick, 54, runs Lakehead Guardian; he helped write the first elder-protection rules and signs for his own father. Teo Brandt, 29, Lakehead's best case officer; his Ward mother has never missed rent. Tribunal chair Marian Voss, 58, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Sam Calder, 41, her brother, still a Ward, scored 73.

**The series engine.** Every book opens with a failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Ines learns why this person failed and who gains, from records requests, billing ledgers and misfiled forms, then argues the appeal in a room built to say no. One citizen in nine fails.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Short Count* — Lena Marsh, a baker with dyscalculia, cannot sign for her flour; Ines wins by reading the local pass mark, 74 not 70, into the record; Lakehead audits her standing.
2. *Record Copy* — Walt Deering, a union archivist, failed memory while holding files Lakehead wants; Ines loses the appeal and saves the archive by signing the deed in his name.
3. *Refusal to Sit* — Jun Pell, a coder, will not sign the test sheet; Ines's win puts raw score sheets on record, Sam's 73 among them; Teo finds the Deering deed.
4. *Her Own Hand* — The deed fails Ines's renewal; unable to sign her own filing, she admits it, puts the postcode schedule on record and wins; Sam signs his own lease.

*Running thread:* Why the pass mark moves by postcode; book 4 shows it is lawful, an actuarial schedule Lakehead lobbied for openly and nobody published. The Assessment stands with one published mark.

**Why readers come back.** Each book gives a person you want to see keep their keys, a paper trail and a verdict. No cliffhangers. Every outcome changes how Meridian treats the next client and what Lakehead does to Ines.

**What makes it different.** The dystopia is administrative, and nobody breaks the law. No uprising, no chosen rescuer, no bloodshed: the weapons are a lease, a score sheet and a filing deadline. Adulthood comes down to signing your name.
