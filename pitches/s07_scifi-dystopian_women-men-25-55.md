# The Signature Laws

**Genre:** Dystopian science fiction · **Readers:** Women and men 25–55 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** In the Lakes Commonwealth, adulthood is a license renewed by exam. Fail, and you cannot sign your name: a Guardian company signs for your bank account, lease and front door. Former Ward Ines Calder takes one failed citizen per book.

**The setting.** Meridian, 2084, is an ore port. The Assessment began as elder fraud protection; now everyone sits it every five years. Fail, and your door lock opens only on a Guardian's countersignature; the Guardian bills your wages. Dock crews change boarding houses each winter; every move costs points. Only lay advocates may argue appeals.

**The lead.** Ines Calder, 37, takes appeals behind the Blue Drum laundromat. At nineteen she admitted a missed payment and spent five years a Ward; her Guardian sold her mother's house. She wants her brother's signature back, and never concedes. Every case puts her own renewal under Lakehead's eye. Exact, dry, funniest when furious.

**The cast.** Sam Calder, 41, her brother, crane mechanic and Ward, wants a lease in his own name. Teo Brandt, 29, Lakehead case officer, believes rules protect his Ward mother. Tribunal chair Marian Voss, 58, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Paul Rennick, 54, runs Lakehead Guardian and signs for his own father.

**The series engine.** Every book opens with a failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Scores are sealed; a third lost appeal is permanent. Ines learns why they failed and who bills them, then argues while Rennick leans on her: locks changed early, the laundromat's account frozen, Sam's crane ticket pulled.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Her Own Keys* — Lena Marsh, 44, baker with dyscalculia, keeps her shop the day before Lakehead sells it; her score sheet shows a pass mark above the published one.
2. *His Own Name* — Walt Deering, 71, union archivist, loses his appeal, but Ines gets his archive to the union with a deed back-dated one day; its fee ledgers wait unread.
3. *His Own Door* — Cal Hruska, 46, deckhand, failed for winter moves; Ines wins back his bunk and his postcode's score sheets, Sam's among them.
4. *Her Own Hand* — Teo reports the back-dated deed and Ines fails her renewal; with fee tables he brings her, she concedes the deed, takes probation and forces the hidden schedule into print.

*Running thread:* Who moves the pass mark by postcode. Book 4 finds a schedule passed in open session, never gazetted, priced on Guardian fee income. The Assessment stands with one published mark; 1,400 Wards sign again, Sam on his own lease.

**Why readers come back.** Each book ends with a verdict and someone holding their own keys, and leaves one fact for the next: Lena's sheet, Walt's unread ledgers, Sam's score. Each brings Sam nearer his lease.

**What makes it different.** Near-future social dystopia run on hearings, not uprisings: administrative and lawful; the one rule broken is the heroine's. The advocate who returns other people's keys loses her own.
