# How these pitches were made

This file records the method, so the results can be judged and the run repeated. The per-pitch detail is in `logs/`.

## 1. The brief

- Twenty one-page series pitches: ten mystery and ten science fiction, all different stories and settings.
- Original work only. Nothing copied from existing books, films, shows or games.
- Each pitch reviewed and rewritten ten times by test-reader critics, with the improvements recorded.
- Each file named by genre and target readers.

Every pitch is written for the plan in `marketresearch/Business plan - four-book series.md`: four books of 60,000 to 70,000 words, one complete case or mission per book, a running thread answered in book four, no cliffhangers, written by an AI model from the author's series bible and edited by the author, for subscription readers in the US market.

## 2. The slate

Claude, the production partner, wrote twenty seed ideas of about 230 words each: the concept, the setting, the lead, the recurring cast, the engine that makes every book happen, and the running thread. The seeds are quoted at the top of each log.

The slate was built for spread, not for one favorite:

- **Subgenre.** The growth evidence in `marketresearch/notes/AI books on Amazon KDP/niche_growth.md` set the mix. Mystery: cozy paranormal, cozy fantasy, culinary cozy, historical, traditional, urban amateur sleuth, techno-mystery and western. Science fiction: space adventure, racing, ocean techno-thriller, exploration, cozy, dystopian, mystery, climate and first contact. Shrinking shelves were left out: space marines, colonization, science-fiction romance and post-apocalypse.
- **Target readers.** Taken from `marketresearch/Audience targets - cozy paranormal mystery.md`: women 45 to 75 for most cozy mysteries, women 25 to 45 for cozy fantasy, mixed and male-leaning groups for science fiction and the procedural mysteries. They are hypotheses, not measurements.
- **Leads and settings.** Leads range from 31 to 154 years old, men and women, alone and in teams. No two pitches share a setting.
- **Engines and threads.** Each seed has a different reason for a new case to arrive in every book, and the running threads were varied after the audit flagged that several repeated one pattern.

## 3. Originality audit of the seeds

Before any drafting, six audit agents searched the web for each seed's premise and working title: three or four differently worded premise searches and one or two title searches per seed, about 240 searches in all, reading result pages only. Each seed got a verdict: clear (nothing close), adjacent (existing works share some pieces) or collision (an existing work has the same premise, setting and signature device).

No seed was a full collision, but five were close enough to an existing series to be replaced, and most of the rest were moved or renamed. The replacements were audited too, and two of them were changed a second time. `logs/seed-audit.md` lists every finding with links and what was done about it.

## 4. The one-page format

Every pitch uses the same sections, so the twenty can be compared:

| Section | Budget |
|---|---|
| Series title, then a fixed line with genre, target readers and series format | |
| Logline | at most 45 words |
| The setting | 60 to 80 words |
| The lead | 50 to 70 words |
| The cast | 40 to 60 words |
| The series engine | 50 to 70 words |
| The four-book arc, one line per book, plus the running thread | |
| Why readers come back | 40 to 60 words |
| What makes it different | 30 to 50 words |

The target was about 520 words in rounds 1 to 6 and 500 from round 7. The pipeline counts the words itself. A draft or rewrite over the limit (580 words, then 540 from round 7) goes to a line-editing agent to be cut, and is thrown away if it is still too long. The pipeline also restores the fixed header line after every rewrite, so genre and target readers cannot drift.

Rules given to every writer: original work only and no naming of comparable authors or titles; concrete detail over abstraction; no stock phrases; one complete case or mission per book and no cliffhangers; the genre's promises kept for its readers; American spelling.

## 5. The ten rounds

Each round has one focus and two critics. Odd rounds are gated by a target reader, even rounds by an acquiring editor.

| Round | Focus | Critic one | Critic two | Gate seat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First contact: is the premise clear and wanted in ten seconds? | Cold reader from the target group | Ten-second browser scrolling on a phone | Reader |
| 2 | Originality: move away from anything it resembles and from stock pieces | Originality auditor, working from the seed audit and memory | Superfan who has read hundreds of books on the shelf | Editor |
| 3 | Series engine: four complete books that add up to an arc | Series editor | Binge reader deciding whether to borrow books 2, 3 and 4 | Reader |
| 4 | Character: a particular lead and cast, one relationship to return for | Character editor | Reader reading for the people | Editor |
| 5 | World and logic: rules stated, limits set, problems solvable by fair reasoning | Fair-play plot doctor for mysteries, plausibility checker for science fiction | Reader reading for belief | Reader |
| 6 | Midpoint cold read: fix what still loses a new reader, restore lost charm | Fresh cold reader | Second fresh cold reader | Editor |
| 7 | Market position: titles, shelf, cover image, reason to borrow the next book | Market positioner, judging titles from memory | Shopper judging titles and cover | Reader |
| 8 | Production fit and risk: what an AI draft and one editing author can deliver | Production editor | Reader wary of machine-written books | Editor |
| 9 | Line edit: tighten, cut repetition, check consistency | Line editor | Reader marking where she skimmed | Reader |
| 10 | Greenlight: last fixes only, at most three changes | Acquiring editor | Final cold reader | Editor |

One round, for one pitch, works like this:

1. **Critique.** Two critics read the current best version independently. Each is a fresh agent with no memory of earlier rounds or drafts. Each returns seven scores from 1 to 10 (hook, originality, series engine, character, setting, fit to genre and readers, clarity), a one-sentence verdict, whether they would read on, up to three strengths to protect, and up to four problems, each with why it matters and a concrete fix.
2. **Rewrite.** A third fresh agent revises the pitch. It must act on the problems it judges valid, protect the named strengths, change as little as possible, and stay inside the format. It may decline advice that would make the pitch more generic, and must say what it declined and why. It also lists questions only the author can settle.
3. **Gate.** A fourth fresh agent compares the rewrite with the version before it, blind, with the order alternating so position cannot decide. Only a rewrite that wins replaces the earlier version. A rejected rewrite is dropped.
4. **Carry forward.** The next round's rewriter is told what the losing version did better, and, after a rejection, which problems are still open. It also gets up to eight strengths praised in earlier rounds.

So each pitch gets ten critiques by twenty critic seats and ten rewrite attempts, of which only the winners survive.

### The correction after round 6

The run paused after round 6 for a check of the numbers. Originality, character and setting scores had held or risen, but the critics' mean clarity score had fallen from 6.8 in round 1 to 5.8 in round 6, and the pitches had grown from 506 to 522 words to between 543 and 578. Each round's fixes had added detail, and the gate had let most of it through.

Three things changed for rounds 7 to 10:

- The length limit dropped from 580 to 540 words, with a target of 500.
- Every rewriter was told that clarity now scored lowest, that every sentence must be understood on one reading, and that each of the four book lines is one sentence of at most 30 words.
- Gate judges were told to prefer, when two versions are close, the one a new reader understands faster.

The final versions are between 516 and 539 words.

### After round 10

The twenty pitches were developed independently, so they had picked the same names: Ruth appeared in seven pitches, Walt and Brandt in five each. Fifty names in seventeen pitches were changed by hand, keeping each name only where it belongs to a lead. One book title that matched an existing novel found in the seed audit was changed as well. These changes were made before the final checks and are listed in each log.

## 6. The test readers

Twenty-three reader personas were written from the audience research, each with an age, a place, reading habits and dislikes. Four are assigned to each pitch according to its target readers and rotate through the rounds. Examples: a 67-year-old retired school librarian who reads three or four cozies a week; a 58-year-old who runs a cozy reading group and has read about two thousand of them; a 58-year-old retired Navy engineering officer who drops a book when the technology is hand-waved; a 68-year-old retired Idaho hay farmer who sat on his irrigation district's board. Each log names the reader for each round.

## 7. Safeguards

Rewriting a text many times with a language model has known failure modes. Each has a counter-measure here:

| Failure mode | Counter-measure |
|---|---|
| Drift toward bland, average prose | The gate; the right to decline advice; strengths carried forward; a rule to change as little as possible |
| Growing denser and longer to answer every note | A word limit enforced by the pipeline; a rule that anything added must be paid for with a cut; gate judges told that denser is not better |
| Pleasing editors at the cost of readers | The gate seat alternates between a target reader and an editor |
| A critic defending its own earlier advice | Critics, rewriter and judge are separate agents with no memory of earlier rounds |
| Position bias in comparisons | The order of the two versions alternates |
| Copying | The seed audit; an originality round; web searches of the titles; a rule in every prompt |
| Format drift | The pipeline restores the fixed header line and trims or discards over-length rewrites |

## 8. Final checks

1. **Blind panel.** Three seats (a target reader, an acquiring editor and a market positioner) scored the first draft and the final version side by side without knowing which was which, then chose one.
2. **Title check.** A web search of the series title and the four book titles of each final pitch. Where the search found an existing book with the same title in the same genre, the title was replaced by hand and the new one searched once.
3. **Slate ranking.** Three judges each ranked all twenty final pitches through one lens: reader pull and market fit, production fit, and distinctiveness and staying power.

## 9. Results

The run used 885 agent calls for the first drafts and the ten rounds, 83 for the final checks, and about 40 more for two pilot runs and the seed audit. No call failed.

- **Rewrites.** 200 rewrites were attempted across 20 pitches and 171 were accepted by the gate (86%).

| Round | Focus | Rewrites accepted | Discarded for length | Critics' mean score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First contact | 20 of 20 | 0 | 7.6 |
| 2 | Originality | 18 of 20 | 0 | 7.4 |
| 3 | Series engine | 17 of 20 | 0 | 7.5 |
| 4 | Character | 19 of 20 | 1 | 7.5 |
| 5 | World and logic | 11 of 20 | 0 | 7.3 |
| 6 | Midpoint cold read | 19 of 20 | 0 | 7.5 |
| 7 | Market | 18 of 20 | 0 | 7.4 |
| 8 | Production and risk | 15 of 20 | 0 | 7.6 |
| 9 | Line edit | 17 of 20 | 0 | 7.5 |
| 10 | Greenlight | 17 of 20 | 0 | 7.8 |

- **Final check.** In the blind comparison, the final version beat the first draft for 20 of 20 pitches (a majority of three seats), and won 59 of 60 individual votes. The panel's mean score moved from 7.0 for first drafts to 7.9 for final versions.
- **Title checks.** clear: 12, major: 5, minor: 3. Pitches with a major collision: m06, m10, s01, s02, s04.
- **Retitled after the check.** m06, m10, s01, s02, s03, s04, s09: every title with a major collision, and two with a minor one, was replaced by hand. Each new title was searched once and came up clear. The logs list the changes.

## 10. Limits

- **The critics are AI personas, not readers.** They are a structured second opinion. No score here predicts sales, and a real test is cheap: show the three or four favorite pitches to actual readers of the genre.
- **The same model family wrote, criticized and judged.** Shared blind spots are likely. The gate removes rewrites the model itself thinks are worse; it cannot catch what the model cannot see.
- **A gate decision is one vote.** A rejected rewrite was not necessarily worse, and an accepted one not necessarily better. Scores from different rounds come from different critics and should be compared with care.
- **Web search was rationed.** The environment allows 200 searches per launch. Live searches were used for the seed audit and for the final title check. The originality round worked from the seed audit and memory, and the market round judged titles from memory. A search is not a catalog or trademark check.
- **Facts are unchecked.** The professions, places, law, science and history in the pitches were written by a model. Verify them before developing any pitch.
- **A pitch is not a book.** Nothing here shows that the series can be written well. The business plan's first checkpoints test that.
