# The Fair Judge Mysteries

**Genre:** Culinary cozy mystery · **Readers:** Women 55–75 · **Series:** four books, 60,000–70,000 words each, one complete case per book

**Logline.** Dot Engelhardt, 67, retired test cook, judges numbered, nameless pies and preserves at Upper Midwest county fairs. At each, a death is called an accident; the proof otherwise is on her table until Sunday, when it gets eaten.

**The setting.** One summer, four fairs: Tamarack County, Sorrel, Pike Lake, the State Fair. Each is a week-long town: dairy barn, grandstand, midway. Judges bunk in the 4-H dormitory; bakers and carnies follow the circuit. Nothing leaves the exhibit hall until Sunday.

**The lead.** Thirty years in the Halstad Mills test kitchen taught Dot to tell lard from shortening, and one kitchen from another. She judges people like crusts: once. She wants this summer with her granddaughter, Seattle-bound in September; every hour on a case comes out of it. She talks like a recipe card: "Shortening. I'd have forgiven the lie before the crust."

**The cast.** Kayla Engelhardt, 19, drives the Buick and hasn't baked since Dot called her crust tough. Harlan Voight, 70, livestock judge, brings coffee, his basset Oleo and the wrong suspect. Bev Aasen, 61, fair secretary, and Lorraine Tvedt, 72, ribbon letterer, share the dormitory oven. Rita Voss runs the Ferris wheel.

**The series engine.** Every book is one fair: judging, a death, and one numbered entry that contradicts someone's story. Every taste clue is also visible to the reader: in book 1 the "homemade" jam sets clear as factory jelly, and its lid carries price-sticker glue. Dormitory supper, two or three recipes. The state holds ninety fairs.

**The four-book arc.**
1. *Jam No. 41 Is Murder* — A ride inspector dies under Rita's Ferris wheel; Dot wrongly blames Rita until store-bought jam breaks an alibi, and Kayla still won't bake.
2. *Pie No. 117 Is Murder* — A fair-board man dies in the milk house; a state investigator blames the carnival, the lard in his pie says otherwise, and Harlan's chair moves closer.
3. *Sourdough No. 9 Is Murder* — Harlan's fishing partner never returns to his announcer's booth; Lorraine is suspected, Kayla cracks half the case, and one oven's scorched corner marks two winners.
4. *Butter No. 302 Is Murder* — The State Fair treasurer dies beside the butter sculpture; Bev confesses the baking, Dot names the man who paid and killed, then asks Kayla for a pie.

*Running thread:* Someone bakes blue-ribbon entries for officials, who sign ride-safety papers unread. Bev baked to keep Tamarack's fair open, keeps her seat at supper, and Dot is asked back.

**Why readers come back.** A week at the fair without the heat: dill pickles held to the light, the percolator at five. Every case closes Sunday; what carries over is the weeks before Seattle, whether Kayla bakes again, and the next entry number in the dead inspector's notebook.

**What makes it different.** The detective's instrument is her palate and the evidence is edible. No shop, no café, no hometown: the village travels, fairground Monday, empty field the next.
