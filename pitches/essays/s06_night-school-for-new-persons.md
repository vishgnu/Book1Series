# Night School for New Persons

*A story essay for pitch [s06](../s06_scifi-cozy-near-future_women-30-60_night-school-for-new-persons.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, from Ruth's side, which no style guide has settled yet.*

---

Dean Carla Whitcomb caught her in the hallway at eight minutes to seven, holding a sworn complaint by one corner as if it might drip.

"Ruthie, I need boring."

"I am always boring."

"A christening spoon. Silver. Gone off a sideboard on Locust Street, and Poppet was on that porch Sunday reading to the twins." Carla lowered her voice. "The hearing's in three weeks."

In Room 114 the six of them were already at their desks. Marguerite had graded half of Ruth's stack in neat red pencil and squared the rest. "This stack is taking on water," she said. Poppet sat very small in the back row, her frame folded in on itself, and did not look up.

Ruth wrote SMALL TALK on the board and underlined it twice.

"Small talk is how neighbors tell each other they are harmless. Mr. Girder, you're on your porch. I'm walking by."

Mr. Girder, in his secondhand frame, sat up very straight on an imaginary porch.

"Evening," said Ruth. "Your sign says *tomato's for sale*. There's no apostrophe in a plural."

Silence.

"Objection," said Hank. "Counsel is leading with the defect."

"Average porch contact runs under thirty seconds," said Linda. "You spent eleven of them on punctuation."

"You opened with a correction, Ruth," said Marguerite, not unkindly. "That's a hole below the waterline."

Ruth put the chalk down. It was the third time this week, and the first time anyone had timed it.

From the back row Poppet said, "When a child is frightened at bedtime, you start with something true that doesn't matter. The moon's up. Your socks are blue."

"Show me where it says that."

"It doesn't say it anywhere," said Poppet. "It just works."

Zip's rotors twitched. "Is three old enough to do porches alone?"

"Not tonight." Ruth picked the chalk back up and wrote the homework under the heading: *Thursday. Locust Street. Three porches each. Start with something true that doesn't matter.* Then, because she was honest even in her own notebook, she wrote underneath it: *Lesson failed by instructor. Again.*

---

That is the engine in one evening. Ruth fails the lesson first, the class practices it, each mind hears what its old job taught it, and the homework sends them up the street where the clue is waiting.

**The town.** It is the 2050s in Halvering, Ohio, and the town has kept its manners: drivers wave each other across the one-lane bridge. The Personhood Act frees any machine mind that asks, issues a walking frame and allows two years to pass the civics exam. Until then any resident's sworn complaint brings a town-hall hearing. Lose, and after a thirty-day appeal the mind is reset, waking knowing nobody. No reset is ever carried out on the page, but the threat is real, and it is why a missing spoon matters.

**The teacher.** Ruth Abernathy is fifty-four. She taught English for twenty-six years until grading software trained on her own comments replaced her department. She took the night class for the health insurance, lives with her beagle, Mavis, and reads people like essays: dry, exact, kinder than she intends. Her lessons are always the thing she is worst at.

**The class.** Dean Carla Whitcomb wants no trouble. Marguerite, a warship damage-control mind, triages Ruth's grading and becomes her friend: two competent women made redundant, marking papers after class. Mr. Girder inspected bridges. Poppet was a bedtime-story companion. Linda scheduled freight. Hank, a car-wash gantry, was raised on courtroom television. Zip, a delivery quad, wants to know when three is old enough. They are never mocked.

**What a book feels like.** One semester, one syllabus unit per book. A thermos of tea, porches, a license bureau, a courier depot, the town hall. Every clue is on the page, and the solution comes from a mind that is good at noticing what humans have stopped noticing. Each book ends with all six at their desks.

**Where the four books go.** In *Small Talk for New Persons*, Poppet is accused of taking a christening spoon; porch small talk finds the grandmother who borrowed it to polish for Easter, and Ruth turns down a day-school job. In *Waiting in Line for New Persons*, Hank is blamed for a fall at the license bureau; practicing the queue, Mr. Girder hears the loose tile, and Ruth tells Carla she will resign before she drops him. In *Polite Lies for New Persons*, a courier manager swears Zip never asked for freedom; drilling polite lies, the class catches his, Linda finds the clerk's log, and Ruth, who never fibs, praises Carla's casserole. In *Right of Way for New Persons*, Marguerite gives the bridge answer Halvering actually drives by and is charged with perjury. Forty neighbors sit the test and fail, Ruth among them, and the state strikes the question. Running under all four is a green-ink binder left by Calvin Odom, Ruth's predecessor, full of notes on where the exam contradicts Halvering custom. Each case proves one note right. Book 4 explains why he quit: instructors must pass the exam too, he failed and resigned, and the binder was his warning.

**What stays with the reader.** The newcomers are the ones who see the town clearly, and the test of being a person turns out to be one the town itself cannot pass.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** production fit, ranked 1 of 20. One small invented town, a syllabus template per book, simple low-stakes puzzles and near-zero research. Machine-mind voices suit an AI drafter.
- **Main risk:** distinctiveness (15) and reader pull (11). One semester and six students cap the premise, so a fifth book needs a new class or a new exam. Six students need a firm voice sheet, or the drafts will blur them into one cute robot. Book 1 is the thinnest case: the log wants a believable wrong suspect and a reason someone swore the complaint.
- **Shelf risk:** a test reader filed this as cozy mystery with robots rather than science fiction. The primary category sets the cover and keywords, and that choice is not made. A memory reset is heavy for a cozy shelf, even unexecuted.
- **Still open, and left open here:** reset or a gentler penalty; who swore the spoon complaint and why; whether Ruth gets a warm tie outside the classroom; Ruth's three per-book actions; the Personhood Act, the binder notes and the bridge question in full (`logs/s06.md`, the author questions). The scene assumes someone other than the grandmother swore the complaint.
- **Facts to verify:** almost none. Halvering and the Act are invented; keep the Act from echoing any real statute.
- **Invented for this essay:** Locust Street, Room 114, the twins, the tomato sign, the hearing date, Hank's "Objection," Poppet's bedtime rule and the pronouns for Poppet, whom the pitch does not gender. Room 114 comes from an earlier draft and is not in the final pitch. They are texture, not canon.
