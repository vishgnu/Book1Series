# Aurochs Country

*A story essay for pitch [s09](../s09_scifi-climate-adventure_women-men-25-55_aurochs-country.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which nobody has settled yet.*

---

Hanka Sommer saw the limp before she saw the cable. Four hundred meters off, past black water and a line of birches that had once been the eastbound lane, a wisent cow walked behind the herd with her head held wrong. It rose each time her near forefoot came down.

"Near fore," Hanka said. "Low on the leg. Hold the barge."

Deniz cut the motor. He had the drone feed up on his tablet and was squinting at a screen the size of a hymnbook, trying to see what she had seen with her eyes.

She went over the side into water with ice at its edges. She darted the cow from thirty meters and waited, counting, while the herd moved off and the cow folded her legs and lay down in the snow like a tired dog.

The cable was yellow, plastic-sheathed steel, the kind surveyors stake along a transect. It had cut a ring above the hoof, not yet to the bone. Hanka cut it with snips, cleaned the wound and packed it. The Charter let rangers touch an animal only to undo human harm. This was human harm, so she took her time.

"There's another," Deniz called from the birches. "A young bull. Same cable."

By dusk both animals were on their feet, and the cable was coiled in a sack in the barge with its survey tag still on it.

The gauge for the calving ground was in the tower of a church whose nave was all reeds. Hanka climbed the stairs past the empty bell frame and read the staff with her torch. It was lower than last month. It was lower than the line.

Deniz watched her write it down. His grandparents held the deed to the calving ground. He did not say so, and she did not ask.

"If you read it a hand higher," he said, "nobody would know."

"The water would." She signed it in her own name.

Across the flood, the roof of Zerbow's pump house showed as a dark square in the last light.

"Did you know someone there?" he asked.

"Four hundred twelve sandbags," Hanka said. "He chalked it on the pump house."

---

That is the series in one evening. Hanka heals what people broke, then writes down the truth that loses her the argument. Nobody makes her. She cannot imagine doing otherwise.

**The country.** Between the Elbe and the Oder, in 2084, the land has been given back to water. Back-bred aurochs, wisent, wild horses and bears move through drowned villages and along birch-grown autobahn. Rangers live on barges, fly drones and read gauges in church towers. The Charter holds the fen: nobody lives here, nothing leaves, and rangers touch animals only to undo human harm. But the law follows the water. Ground that stays dry through a flood season is released, and its old families may move back. The fen is working, and that is the problem. Every dry summer brings the herds onto released ground, where a breeder will pay a year's wage for one wild-born calf.

**The lead.** Hanka is thirty-four, a ranger and a veterinarian, and she can read a limping wisent at four hundred meters. She was nine when her father drowned sandbagging Zerbow's dike. Each autumn she barges her mother out to his grave. She wants the fen wild, and she wants her mother's forgiveness, and she cannot have both. She signs every dry reading herself.

**The company.** Renate Sommer, sixty-six, calls the Charter "the theft of Zerbow" and is partly right. Tomasz Wrona, fifty-seven, trained Hanka and keeps his real counts in a notebook. Jutta Reimann, forty-one, a hydrologist, wants sluices to keep the fen wet through dry summers, which purists call cheating. Deniz Aydin, twenty-three, is a first-year ranger who wants the badge, and his grandparents hold the deed to the calving ground.

**What a book feels like.** One season, one species, one case. The books are wet, cold and physical: barge engines, frost on a dart rifle, blood drawn from a calf and milk from a cow. Every finding goes to the Elbe-Oder Assembly, where half the seats belong to returner families and half to the downstream cities that need the fen to hold their floods. The animals behave like animals. Hurt ones are treated and recover, and no named animal dies. Each case closes, and each leaves a debt for the next book to collect.

**Where the four books go.** In *Wisent Winter* Hanka treats the two snared wisent, traces the cable to Jutta's crew, and still signs their honest readings. The calving ground is released, and the returners gain votes. In *Aurochs Spring* a breeder claims nine calves. Hanka and Deniz prove them wild-born by blood and milk, and Hanka lets Tomasz leave a tenth calf, already sold, off the count. *Konik Summer* opens on a campfire that lights dry peat. Hanka breaches a dike without orders to save the horses, counts the nests the water floods, and names her mother as the camper. In *Bear Autumn* a bear trails the surveyors, and Hanka moves the camps, not the bear. Snared hair and bar-worn teeth show it was raised in a pen, and Tomasz released it to scare the surveyors off. The thread is whether the Charter survives the year-end vote. It does, for ten years, at a price: Jutta's sluices are built, Zerbow's church hill goes back to Renate, Tomasz loses his badge, and Hanka is demoted for naming her own silence over the tenth calf.

**What stays with the reader.** The series offers hope without a clean victory. The fen survives because it is managed, the mother gets her hill, and the daughter pays for honesty with her rank. Readers will remember the gauge in the church tower, and a woman who climbed to read it when she knew what it would say.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** the conflict comes from recovery, not collapse. The strongest witness against Hanka's cause is her own honest report, and that engine is fresh.
- **Main risk:** the slate ranked it 20 of 20. A German rewilding setting, land law and a demoted heroine sit far from US subscription tastes. Physical stakes are low, and books 1 and 4 read more as findings than missions. For one part-time editor, the specialist load (veterinary practice, hydrology, ecology, real geography) is the heaviest on the slate.
- **Shelf risk:** climate fiction borrows weakly. The hopeful-ranger readership skews older than the brief's 25 to 55.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether the book 3 fire is an accident (the essay keeps the pitch's campfire); which bird's nests are flooded, since the log's lapwings nest mainly in spring; whether Hanka is formally reinstated in book 4; whether to keep the real rivers or move to an invented basin; whether a recurring breeder is wanted (`logs/s09.md`, the author questions). The essay assumes the silence Hanka names is the tenth calf.
- **To verify:** forelimb lameness signs and darting a wisent; matching calf to dam by blood and milk; hair snares and bar-wear on captive bear teeth; peat fire and rewetting; whether bears could plausibly range here. The Bruch, Zerbow and the Charter are invented, and they echo real East German restitution history.
- **Invented for this essay:** the yellow survey cable and its tag, the young bull, the reed-filled church and its empty bell frame, Deniz's offer to misread, and the drone tablet. They are texture, not canon.
