# The Night Notary

*A story essay for pitch [m08](../m08_urban-sleuth-mystery_women-men-40-65_the-night-notary.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

The kitchen in Pacoima smelled of burnt coffee and the lemon spray somebody had used on the table an hour ago, for her. Gloria Ibarra noticed things like that. People who scrubbed a table for a notary at two in the morning wanted the paper signed more than the man who was signing it.

The man was Mr. Bettencourt, eighty-one, in a cardigan buttoned one hole off. His license was real. She had checked his face against the photograph, a heavier man with the same ears. In the hall, out of the light, a friend of the family leaned on the wall in a windbreaker and did not come in.

"Mr. Bettencourt," she said. "Can you tell me what this paper does?"

He looked at the deed. Then he looked past her shoulder at his son-in-law, who stood by the refrigerator with his arms folded.

"It's for the house," the son-in-law said. "So Denise and I can help with the taxes."

"Sir, I asked him, not you."

The son-in-law smiled as if she had made a small joke. Gloria had spent nineteen years in a courthouse watching men smile like that at judges.

"Mr. Bettencourt?"

"It's for the house," the old man said. "Darren handles it."

She asked him the month. He said March. It was October. She asked who would own the house after he signed. He said he would, of course, he and Maxine. The funeral card for Maxine was on the refrigerator, under a magnet shaped like a lemon.

Gloria uncapped her pen. On the next clean line of her journal she wrote the date, the time, 2:07 a.m., the document, the signer's name. In the box where a thumbprint would go she wrote nothing. In the notes she wrote: *Refused. Signer could not state purpose of document. Third party answered for signer.* Then she turned the journal around so the son-in-law could read it, because he was going to anyway.

"You drove out here for nothing," he said.

"No, sir. I drove out here for that."

In the car she called Nadine, who was always awake. "Come home, honey," Nadine said. "Write it up tomorrow. I'll tell the client."

By morning another notary had stamped the deed, and Mr. Bettencourt was at the bottom of his own back steps.

---

That is the series at its smallest scale: a table, a few people, one of them lying, and a woman whose only power is to say no and write it down.

**The city.** Los Angeles after nine at night, seen from the paperwork side: loan papers on a Pacoima kitchen table, a power of attorney at a hospital bedside, an affidavit at a county jail. It is porch lights, a family's good dining chairs and a long drive home on an empty freeway. A California notary gives no legal advice. She identifies the signer, journals the date and time, takes a thumbprint for a deed, and may refuse anyone who seems confused or pushed. That narrow job is the whole of Gloria's authority.

**The lead.** Gloria is forty-seven and spent nineteen years as a superior court clerk before she bought a seal. Six years ago she notarized an impostor's deed on good ID, and the Batistes lost their house. She keeps that journal page and has never faced them. A second bad stamp would end her commission. Her days belong to her father, Ernesto, seventy-nine, a retired locksmith who is losing his memory and has never been rude to a stranger. She is dry, exact and polite, the kind of polite that stops a room.

**The company.** Grace Oyelaran, fifty-eight, a hospital night chaplain, calls when a patient is rushed to sign. Nadine Kovac, fifty-two, runs the signing service that books Gloria's jobs and talks her home at three in the morning. Warren Tsai, forty-four, the district attorney's fraud investigator, subpoenas her pages. Glenn Purcell, sixty-one, the escrow officer, sees every deed she turns away. She reports every refusal to all three.

**What a book feels like.** Every case starts at a signing. The police see an accident or a civil dispute. Gloria's journal, the recorded deeds and the court files give Tsai grounds to open a file. Every clue is ink on a page the reader has already seen: a time, a name, a signature, an empty thumbprint box.

**Where the four books go.** In *Witness My Hand* the widower's death is ruled a fall, but the son-in-law's alibi witness once signed Gloria's journal under another name, and the son-in-law killed for the house. In *Sworn Before Me* an inmate overdoses after Gloria refuses his affidavit clearing a deed forger. The stamped copy is timed at evening count, when no cell opens, and the forger's lawyer killed him. In *Penalty of Perjury* Grace calls her to a confused dying widow who can barely make an X. Gloria refuses. The recorded deed bears a fluent signature, forged by the niece who withheld the widow's medication. In *Satisfactory Evidence* Gloria is suspended over a forged seal and learns that Ernesto, too polite to refuse, signed a stranger's deed to his own house. Every bad ID she ever journaled shares one issue date and one maker. The question under all four is who sends a second notary each time she refuses. It is Nadine, whose failing service sold each refused signer to the notary who made the IDs, the Batiste impostor's included. Her invoice from book 1 billed the refused deed as closed. Both are charged, and Gloria sits at the Batistes' table, their judgment won.

**What stays with the reader.** A woman who keeps her worst page and rereads it, and a series in which the strongest word anyone says is "No." The warmth is real and small, a friend's voice on the phone at three in the morning. The cost is that the friend was selling the signers.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** refusal as the lead's only power. It is new on the amateur-sleuth shelf, every clue is ink the reader can check, and the thread pays off inside the cast with an invoice planted in book 1.
- **Main risk:** procedure. Every plot hinges on exact California notary, deed-recording, jail and hospital practice, and one legal error breaks a case built on paperwork (production fit ranked 17). Nadine must stay warm and visible for three books without looking guilty, which AI drafts handle badly.
- **Shelf risk:** night-time Los Angeles paperwork crime sits between cozy and procedural with no clear shelf, and the titles are stock legal phrases that crowd searches.
- **Still open, and left open here:** the second notary's name and whether he is one recurring figure; Purcell's role; whether book 3 shows Gloria catching Ernesto's deed before it records; whether Gloria writes extra observations in her journal, which a later clue may need (`logs/m08.md`, the author questions). In the scene, the pitch's line "Sir, I asked her, not you" is adapted to "him," because the book 1 signer is a man.
- **To verify:** required journal entries (time, thumbprint for deeds, ID details), grounds for refusal, how a district attorney's investigator obtains journal pages, whether a signing service or escrow officer would learn of a refusal, jail count practice, and discipline after a bad stamp.
- **Invented for this essay:** Mr. Bettencourt, Darren, Denise and Maxine, the man in the hall, the lemon spray and magnet, 2:07 a.m., October, the back steps and Nadine's words on the phone.
