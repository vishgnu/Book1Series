# The Century Unit

*A story essay for pitch [s08](../s08_scifi-mystery_men-women-40-70_the-century-unit.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, from Varga alone; whether Pell gets a point of view is still open.*

---

The house on Larch Street had been rewired four times and repainted more often than anyone could remember, which in Port Sable meant more than seventy years. The front door was new. The wall beside it was not. That was where the builders had found the doorbell, plastered over, its small glass eye still looking out at the street.

Ottilie Brenn kept a notarized diary, like most careful people. She had it open on her knee before Inspector Varga sat down.

"I've told this many times," she said. "My father came home at a quarter to nine. He rang, because he'd lost his key. I was five. I let him in."

"Do you remember it?"

"I remember telling it." She turned the pages toward them. A notary's stamp, blue and square, sat at the foot of each entry. "Here, 2091. Here, 2120. The same each time."

"That's the story," Varga said. "I asked for the receipt."

Then she waited. It was the part of the job she did best, and the part young officers could not stand. Out on the harbor a kite-freighter came about, its sail cracking like a sheet on a line.

Midge Pell had the doorbell open on a cloth between them like a clam. She had spent three days coaxing it, one small fact at a time.

"It kept a log of every press," Pell said. "Your father's is there, Mrs. Brenn. Eight forty-six."

Ottilie let out a breath. The 2041 file put the death at twenty past nine, and that was the one thing in it nobody had ever disputed.

"But its clock is an hour off," said Pell. "It was set in winter and never went forward in the spring. Nobody could reach it to fix it. Nobody remembered it was there."

Varga wrote *8:46* in her notebook, and *9:46* beside it, and drew one line under both.

Pell glanced at the page. "That's two accounts now," she said. "Mine has a battery."

Ottilie closed the diary. She did not argue. She sat, Varga thought, like someone listening for a bell in another room.

---

The scene holds the whole method. Every witness is alive, sincere and useless. Every object is mute until someone patient makes it say one thing. And the inspector's notebook is only one more account, which the last book will prove.

**The city.** Port Sable in 2157 is a harbor city of kite-sailed freighters and diary notaries. In 2049 a treatment called the Forfeit ended aging, and its price was memory: a person keeps only the latest seventy years. So nobody alive remembers anything before 2087, and the courts refuse testimony older than memory. Forgetting a killing does not undo it in law. Conviction means forty years confined, most of a living memory. That leaves the Century Unit with paper, cloth, bone and dead devices from our own century, and with witnesses who are a century older than their alibis.

**The lead.** Dessa Varga is 154 and runs the Unit. She remembers nothing of the sergeant who trained her at twenty-seven, Corin Hale, who quit during her first homicide. He is alive somewhere in the city and remembers none of it either. He survives for her only as pencil corrections in her old notebooks. She trusts her own handwriting too much. She asks for the receipt before the story, then waits.

**The company.** Pell coaxes one small fact from each dead device and calls Varga's notebooks "one more account", which is both a joke between them and a warning. Sergeant Ilan Okafor is sixty-one, the only one who remembers everything, and he is writing down his mother's kitchen before his first forgetting, nine years off. Silas Roe, the defense advocate, argues that a client who cannot remember the act did not commit it, and he believes it.

**What a book feels like.** Each book opens with an object surfacing from a wall, an auction or the harbor mud. Someone still holds a house or a pension because of an old story, and no killer may keep what the killing won. So Varga traces how the story drifted and who gained. No suspect dies of old age, so no case closes itself. The witnesses are met with patience, not contempt, and nobody is hurt on the page. The pleasure is two women at a bench arguing over a ferry ticket, one reading the date, the other the ink.

**Where the four books go.** *The Larch Street Doorbell* breaks a five-year-old's alibi with a camera clock an hour off, and the daughter, now 121, still owns the house it won. In *The Judge's Raincoat* a ferry ticket sewn into a lining puts a judge beside a clerk who drowned in 2026. Hale signed the report, and one page of it has since been replaced. *The Clinic Queue* reopens a nurse's killing in the first Forfeit queue, where the order of names shows who stood behind her, and shows that Hale stood in the line too. *The Sealed Tin* brings the series home: a phone in a sealed tin reopens Varga's own first homicide, a pawnbroker whose partner still holds the shop. Okafor leads, Varga is a witness, and Pell authenticates her notebooks against her. Each book adds one document to the Hale file. The fourth answers whether Varga drove out the man who trained her, and the answer comes from her own handwriting.

**What stays with the reader.** The series asks what remains of a person once memory is gone, and it answers: the things they wrote down and the things they kept. A reader closes the last book thinking about their own drawers, their old phones and the stories they have told so often they no longer know where the telling began.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** it ranked first of twenty for distinctiveness. Ageless witnesses who remember only the retelling have no shelf twin, and our own century supplies the objects without end.
- **Main risk:** arithmetic. Ages, the seventy-year window and dates a century old touch every scene, and one slip breaks the premise. Fix the timeline in the bible before drafting (Varga born 2003; cases in 2041, 2026, 2049 and 2030). Book 2's case predates her service, so only Hale links it. A further puzzle: the daughter was thirteen when aging stopped in 2049, and the pitch does not say how the Forfeit treats children. The essay avoids describing her.
- **Shelf risk:** it falls between the mystery and science fiction shelves. The subtitle question (add the year or not) is still open.
- **Still open, and left open here:** what exactly Varga did in 2030 and what it costs her; whether she and Hale meet on the page and where he has been; book 4's victim, a placeholder; whether the Forfeit was universal or chosen; the forty-year sentence (`logs/s08.md`, the author questions).
- **To verify:** whether a 2040s doorbell camera would log presses locally, and whether any battery or memory chip could plausibly be read after a century in a wall. Pell's coaxing covers this, but readers of this age will ask. The daylight-saving explanation for the hour assumes the city still kept clock changes in 2041.
- **Invented for this essay:** Ottilie Brenn, the notarized diary dates, the 8:46 log, the 9:20 time of death, the daylight-saving mechanism and the rewired house. They are texture, not canon.
