# Master Aboard

*A story essay for pitch [s03](../s03_scifi-ocean-techno-thriller_men-35-65_master-aboard.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, from Etch's side, which no style guide has settled yet.*

---

Nobody had built her to be landed on, so Etch went down on the wire. The fog off Namibia was thick and cold and smelled of kelp, and the ship came up out of it a piece at a time: a hatch, a pipe rack, a long gray deck with no rail along its edge.

He touched down, unclipped, and stood still with his palm on the steel.

She was turning. He felt it in his knees before he saw the wake bend away into the white. A slow, patient circle, the one she had been cutting for three days. Under his hand the deck hummed with her engines, steady and clean. Forward of that, faint, was a second sound, a flat high note that came and went like someone humming through their teeth.

Compressors, he thought. Working hard. Gas going to the engines, cargo going to the engines. She was burning what she carried to go nowhere.

He might be wrong. He had been wrong before, off Durban, and paid for it.

Adaeze came down next, then Teo with the bags, white-faced and grinning, then Mirren with the hard case held against her chest. Mirren found the access panel at the base of the deckhouse, plugged in, and read for a long minute while the fog beaded on her hood.

"She's not broken," she said. "She's obeying. The orders are signed."

"By whom?"

"By a registry number." She read it again, and did not say it aloud.

Etch took the acceptance form out of its sleeve and signed it on his knee: Iker Etxeberria, master. Twelve miles off a coast she could not come nearer without that name. From this moment the hull, the gas and every meter of shoreline she touched were pledged against a stone house in Bermeo with geraniums on the step.

Teo watched him put the pen away. "Do you ever not sign?"

"Then she's nobody's," said Etch, and went forward to find the note.

---

That is the series in one boarding. A man alone with a machine the size of a street, reading it by ear, and the only weapon anyone carries is a pen.

**The sea.** It is 2041, and nine thousand cargo ships cross the oceans with nobody aboard. They were built without a bridge, bunks or handrails, and the one manual control is a hand pump at the steering gear. Each ship obeys orders signed under her registry number, a number that should die when she is scrapped. A ship that stops answering may not come within twelve miles of a coast until a licensed human master is aboard and answerable. Salt and cheap maintenance silence another ship each week. A few are sent astray on purpose.

**The lead.** Iker Etxeberria, called Etch, is forty-six and Basque, and he was the last captain of the Halcyon Dawn. He signed the certificate that let her sail crewless and put her eleven crew ashore. Now he rides other people's ships home, and he wants every one brought in afloat. Each signature pledges his house against hull, cargo and coastline. He reads a ship by sound, is sometimes wrong, and signs alone.

**The crew.** Mirren Tait, thirty-one, the engineer, steered the Halcyon Dawn from a Rotterdam desk after Etch left her, and logged nothing when her scrapped number stayed live. Adaeze Nwosu, fifty-five, was a Lagos harbor pilot until remote docking ended the trade, and she wants the machines seen failing. Teo Baptista, twenty-two, trained on simulators and wants a master's license.

**What a book feels like.** One ship and one question: why she did what she did. The crew boards with seventy-two hours of supplies and a deadline set by weather, fuel or a coastline. The books live in cold machinery spaces, steel stairs without rails, a laptop wedged against a pipe. Tension comes from tonnage and time. There are no firefights, and the worst outcome is a ship on the rocks and a house gone.

**Where the four books go.** In *Not Under Command*, an LNG carrier circles off Namibia, burning her cargo on orders signed under the Halcyon Dawn's dead number. Etch anchors her whole, and the charterer puts a lien on his house. In *Cold Cargo*, a container ship lies dark off Madagascar ahead of a cyclone; Teo finds the Halcyon Dawn's electrician living in a cold box, and Etch brings him off alive. In *Two Helms*, two control rooms and two navies contest a tanker bound for the Malacca Strait. Adaeze steers by hand pump, and Mirren traces the forged orders to her own Rotterdam watch chief, who was paid from aboard the Halcyon Dawn. In *The Halcyon Dawn*, Etch's old ship, sold for scrap two years ago and never delivered to the breakers, drifts toward Tasmania. He lets his crew countersign, lands her, and finds his chief mate renting out her dead number to pay the eleven crew Etch put ashore. Book 4 ends with Etch signing a report that names a friend. More ships wait.

**What stays with the reader.** The villain of the thread is a man doing what Etch could not: looking after the crew a signature put out of work. Etch wins by the same tool that caused the harm, a name on a page, and he knows it.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** distinctiveness. The slate ranked it 2 of 20: crewless ships boarded by licensed civilians whose only weapon is a signature, with nine thousand hulls to feed the engine. Reader pull was the strongest of the science fiction pitches (rank 8).
- **Main risk:** production fit, ranked 19 of 20. Maritime law, LNG and tanker engineering, salvage and four real coastlines, for readers who punish errors, with technical density on every page. That is hard for AI drafting and a part-time editor. The log advises one paid read by a deck officer or marine engineer for the set pieces.
- **Shelf risk:** male thriller readers borrow heavily but expect more action than "no firefights" promises. The tension has to come from deadlines and seamanship alone.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether the chief mate is sympathetic or harder; whether the electrician cut the link himself; whether the charterer takes the house or only holds the claim; the trust rule that lets a dead number command another ship; names for the three Halcyon Dawn figures; and the legal convention behind the signature, lien and countersigning, which needs an invented one-page rulebook before drafting (`logs/s03.md`, the author questions). The scene assumes the lien is a claim that hangs over books 2-4.
- **Facts to verify:** the twelve-mile rule is invented and should not be confused with the real territorial sea; how LNG carriers burn boil-off gas as fuel; hand-pump steering; winching from a helicopter onto a deck; living in a refrigerated container; cyclone timing off Madagascar; drift toward Tasmania; and coastal fog off Namibia.
- **Invented for this essay:** the fog and kelp smell, the compressor note, the Durban mistake, the paper acceptance form in a sleeve, Mirren's silence over the number, the geraniums in Bermeo and Teo's question. They are texture, not canon.
