# The Sworn Locksmith of Pellory

*A story essay for pitch [m04](../m04_cozy-fantasy-mystery_women-25-45_the-sworn-locksmith-of-pellory.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

The clockmaker's door was on the six-hundredth step, between a bakery and a woman who sold string. Sergeant Maud Garrick climbed the last flight behind Hester with her witness book under her arm and her breath coming in short pieces.

"They carried him down at seven," Maud said. "Nobody's touched it since."

Hester looked at the door before she touched it. Oak, iron-banded, a brass hand plate worn bright by sixty years of the same three palms. The binding mark over the lintel said three: the old man's hand, his son's and his son's wife's. The baker had seen the door shut at midnight, when she lit her ovens. At five it stood open. The son and his wife had been at a wedding down by the river all night, nine hundred steps away, with two hundred guests to swear to it. And the old man had been dead since before eleven, because every clock in his shop had stopped, and he wound them at eleven every night of his life.

A hand lock opens to a bound hand. There had been no bound hand on this step to open it.

Hester set down her case and knocked on the plate, three soft raps and one hard. The salt had taken the feeling from her fingertips years ago, on the ships, so she listened instead. "Easy," she told the lock. "Nobody's cross with you." The bolt answered with a clean, oiled knock. She put her eye to the keyhole with a lamp. The wards were bright. No scratch, no wax, no wire.

"Not picked," said Bram Tolliver from two steps down. He had come up for the bread and stayed for the trouble.

"No," said Hester. "Opened. By a hand it knew." She looked at the binding mark again: neat, tight, signed in the corner by Cal Wainscott. "So the binding's wrong. It's answering to somebody it shouldn't."

Maud wrote that down.

"Five," he said.

"I haven't done anything yet."

"That's why it's five."

Up the stairs a door banged, and quick boots started down. Cal Wainscott, coming to take it personally.

---

Hester has made her first mistake of the series within a page, and she has made it honestly: the evidence really does point at the binding. The reader holds the same rule she does. A charm knows its condition was met, never who met it, so a fourth hand nobody remembers is already a possibility the rule allows.

**The town.** Pellory climbs nine hundred steps, and a judge climbs them twice a year. In between, nobody can be charged with a crime until the sworn locksmith says how the door was opened. Every door is charm-bound one of four ways. A hand lock opens to any bound hand, a word lock to its word, an hour lock at its hour, and a promise lock when its promise breaks. By the Lock Law, only the sworn locksmith may open a door no charm explains. So the woman who mends the town's locks is the gate every case must pass through.

**The locksmith.** Hester Wardlow is thirty-eight and has no magic at all. Ships' strongboxes salt-scarred her fingertips, so she knocks to hear a lock, and she talks to locks like shy horses. She left Pellory when her grandmother Odile would not explain the shop's cellar, and Odile died before she came home. To take the shop she swore Odile's oath on the cellar's promise lock: open a door unwitnessed and the cellar springs open and the town takes the shop. So she never opens a door alone.

**The company.** Bram Tolliver, a retired burglar, grades her lockwork out of ten. She has never had an eight. Sergeant Maud Garrick is her lawful witness and keeps a book that is missing one of Odile's doors. Cal Wainscott, the town binder, takes every unexplained door personally and rebinds Hester's own shop door tighter, unasked. In book 2 a sixteen-year-old named Peg joins as apprentice. Each book adds one chair to Hester's table.

**What a book feels like.** Every case opens on a neighbor's worst morning, with a door locked, or open, as no charm allows. The kind of lock names the suspects, because only certain people held the hand, the word, the hour or the promise. Every clue is on the page before she swears. Two books turn on deaths and two on thefts, and the deaths stay offstage. The click at the end is always one of the four rules, bent in a way the reader could have seen.

**Where the four books go.** In *The Clockmaker's Three Hands*, a clockmaker dies behind a door bound to three hands. Hester wrongly blames Cal's charm, then finds a forgotten fourth hand, bound in childhood. In *A Word in the Market*, a strongroom is robbed and Cal's binding points at Peg. Hester shows the word was sitting in a market song, and gains an apprentice. In *The Early Bell*, a cashbox vanishes through an hour-bound inn door. Hester clears Bram by proving the town bell rang early, and learns why Odile hired a burglar. *The Cellar Ninety Steps Away* spends the oath. The town clerk dies behind a promise-bound bolt, and Hester forfeits the shop to open a door unwitnessed. Her own cellar lock springs ninety steps away, which proves a promise lock answers at a distance, and the killer's alibi of distance fails. The cellar's ledger answers the running question. Odile did open doors unwitnessed. Most of them hid a kindness. The one missing from Maud's book hid the clerk's fraud. Hester re-swears. Cal rebinds the cellar, at her asking this time, and stays for supper.

**What stays with the reader.** The difference between "unasked" and "at her asking." The slow burn is carried in one craftsman's verb, and the series ends when a prickly man finally does what Hester wants instead of what he thinks she needs. Under it sits a gentler question, asked by a woman who has built her life on keeping one rule: whether breaking it can be kind.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** the law sends her in, so she never has to snoop, and four fixed lock rules give fair-play puzzles the reader can work. With no real-world research, production fit ranked fourth.
- **Main risk:** the engine spends itself. Four rules over four books leaves later cases to combinations (distinctiveness ranked 16th). Every impossible door must pass a logic check, and AI drafts tend to bend rules quietly, so the bible must define door, witness and opening first.
- **Shelf risk:** the shop-and-town frame is familiar in cozy fantasy, and test readers who came for romance found Cal thin. One critic thought the natural readers are puzzle fans 35-60, not 25-45.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether Cal is a romance lead or a warm closing note; how wrong Odile was about the clerk; what counts as a door, a witness and opening, and whether Bram or Peg can witness; whether re-swearing gives Hester the shop back, which the essay assumes (`logs/m04.md`, the author questions).
- **Needs checking:** little, since the world is invented, though lock vocabulary (wards, bolts, plates) should be right.
- **Invented for this essay:** the six-hundredth step, the baker and the string seller, the son and his wife as the other two hands, the wedding, the stopped clocks, the knock-and-listen method, and Bram's five. They are texture, not canon.
