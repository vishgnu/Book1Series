# The Camas Fork Mysteries

*A story essay for pitch [m10](../m10_western-mystery_men-women-45-70_the-camas-fork-mysteries.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

The padlock lay in the mud beside the headgate, its shackle cut clean, and Marty Voss did not pick it up. She photographed it first, with the gate's painted number in the corner of the frame. Then she looked at the bank.

A wet bank told you everything if you let it finish. This one said the water was still running through the Pruitt turnout, the gate she had locked shut the evening before. It said the flow had come on hard, hard enough to tear a fan of silt across the field-side ditch, and that the silt was still soft.

Up the lane, past the old Pruitt house, the sheriff's cars sat with their light bars dark outside the potato cellar. Caleb Ortega walked down to her with his hat in his hand. He had been her trainee once. He still walked toward her like one.

"Coroner thinks midnight, give or take," he said. "There's water on the cellar floor."

Marty took the measuring stick off her truck and went down the main ditch to the staff gauge below the turnout. The ditch was clear and cold and smelled of snowmelt and the basalt it ran over. She read the gauge, then read it again.

"What's it say?"

"Four tenths low." She wrote the figure in the log. "What I think comes after."

He waited. He had learned to.

She walked back to the recorder box, opened it with the second key on her ring and slid out the chart. The pen line ran flat all night along the ditch's ordinary level, then dropped, sudden and square, a little after six.

"Six-ten," she said. "That's when somebody opened my gate and took four tenths off the main ditch. Your man was dead at midnight."

"So somebody cut your lock six hours after he died."

"Somebody opened the gate at six-ten. That's what the chart says, and that's what I'll sign." She closed the gate, hung a new lock and snapped it shut. Then she signed the chart, dated it and wrote the time beside her initials. "Who, and why they'd want water in a dead man's cellar, that's yours."

Ortega took the copy. He would act on it because she had signed it, and on nothing she had only said.

---

That is the engine in one morning. There are two records that cannot both be true, the reader sees both, and the detective will only swear to the one she measured.

**The valley.** Larkspur County, Idaho: potato cellars and dairies over black lava rock, nine inches of rain a year. Fourteen ditches split the Camas Fork, the oldest dug in 1884. First in time is first in right, so in a dry year the oldest ditch fills before a newer one gets a drop. In a dry valley a place in that line is worth killing for, every year.

**The rider.** Marty Voss is fifty-four and has been the watermaster's ditch rider since 2002. She has no badge, only the padlocks on the headgates. The year before she took the job, as a sheriff's deputy, she served the 2001 shutoff that cost Vern Pruitt his farm, and she wants to know it was right. She reads a wet bank the way other people read a face: what ran, how deep, and when. Her opinions wait behind her measurements.

**The company.** Sheriff Caleb Ortega, forty-seven, was the trainee who drove her to the Pruitt shutoff, and he acts only on what she has signed. Dale Hinckley, sixty-one, the watermaster, keeps the ditch ledger in his late wife's recipe tins, all of it except the 1911 pages. Maite Aldecoa, sixty-seven, a Basque herder's granddaughter, farms on the ditch first in line. Gene Voss, fifty-eight, Marty's brother, farms on the newest.

**What a book feels like.** The death is Ortega's case. The water is Marty's. Each break comes from something only a ditch rider may do: measure, lock, sign the log, testify. The books follow one water year, from April turn-on through July, harvest and winter, with the figures in cubic feet per second and every measurement open for the reader to check. Each case closes in its own book, and each book turns up one missing 1911 page, moving a named ditch up the line.

**Where the four books go.** In *First in Time* it is April. The old Pruitt farm's buyer lies dead in his potato cellar and Marty's padlock is cut, yet the gauge chart says the water came six hours after he died. In *Call on the River* it is July. Ordered to lock out her brother Gene, Marty finds his neighbor dead beside a flume shaved to cheat the gauge. Her log clears Gene, and he stops speaking to her. In *Duty of Water*, at harvest, a dairy pond filling at night gives up Vern Pruitt, missing since 2001. Marty's delivery sheets show the pond stood dry until 2003, so someone moved him. In *Futile Call* it is winter. Hinckley lies under ice at a locked headgate, his tins emptied, and Marty's own log says only her key opened it. Gene, silent since July, drives her to the courthouse. Under all four runs the 1911 fraud: someone back-dated the Aldecoa claim ahead of the ditch that fed the Pruitt farm, and Marty enforced that order in 2001. In book 4 she says under oath that her shutoff was wrong. The pitch does not name Hinckley's killer or the 1911 forger, and neither does this essay.

**What stays with the reader.** A woman whose only weapon is a true measurement, turning it on herself in a courtroom. Her brother at the wheel of a pickup in winter, not saying much. The sound of water in a ditch at six in the morning, and the knowledge that somebody in the valley is always awake to hear it.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** every case turns on two records that cannot both be true, and the thread pays off with Marty's own sworn correction of her own past act. The brother rift adds warmth.
- **Main risk:** arithmetic and law. The reader is invited to check every figure, so flows, dates, priorities and gauge readings must stay consistent across four books (production fit ranked 18). AI drafts will produce plausible hydraulics that do not add up, and water ledgers are a dry draw with an emotionally reserved lead.
- **Shelf risk:** it sits beside established rural Western mysteries, with only the ditch-rider premise to tell it apart. Camas is a real Idaho name (a county, a prairie, a creek) next to an invented county.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether Marty rides for a canal company or as deputy watermaster (the essay follows the pitch's "the watermaster's ditch rider"); whether Maite is ally or opponent once the 1911 date falls; which side of the date Gene's ditch sits on; how a forged 1911 date survived the state's later adjudication; Marty's life off the ditch (`logs/m10.md`, the author questions).
- **To verify:** prior appropriation as Idaho actually administers it, a watermaster's and ditch rider's duties, lock and key practice, staff gauges and recorder charts, whether a decreed priority can be reopened for fraud, and the rainfall figure.
- **Invented for this essay:** the gate number, the photograph, the silt fan, the coroner's midnight estimate, the six-ten reading, the second key, the new lock and the water on the cellar floor.
