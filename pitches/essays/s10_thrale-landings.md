# The Thrale Landings

*A story essay for pitch [s10](../s10_scifi-first-contact_women-men-30-65_the-thrale-landings.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which nobody has settled yet.*

---

The capsule had come down in the night on the black gravel above the seal beach, and by the time Tobias Eze reached it, two of the seals had decided it was a rock.

Inside, on nothing, sat a cup.

He lifted it out in cotton gloves and carried it up to the station like an egg. In the workroom he set it on the felt, switched on the lamp, and spoke without looking up. Saoirse Lim wrote it down.

"Earthenware. Hand-built, not thrown. Red-brown body, unglazed. Two chips at the rim, both worn smooth, so old damage, used after. Staining inside in a band about two fingers deep. Lip wear heavier on the left." He turned it. "Somebody drank from this for a long time, with the same hand."

"And this," said Saoirse.

Below the rim, pressed into the clay before it was fired, was a thumbprint. He bent to it with the loupe. Whorls. A small scar across them, healed.

"Human," he said.

Nobody in the room said anything for a while. Outside, the wind worked at the anemometer.

"Condition?" Saoirse asked at last, because someone had to.

"Stable. No repairs." He set the cup on the scale and waited for the figure to settle. "Two hundred and six grams."

He wrote it in the station log, then again in his own notebook, an old customs habit. Things on shelves had a way of getting lighter.

"So what do we send back?" said Saoirse. "What can we make?"

"Nothing new. New things are copies of something." He capped his pen. "We send what has been used. Something that doesn't claim anything. I've been wrong about that once."

From the doorway Hana Kovač said, "Pool's open. Two to one it's a grave good."

"It isn't."

"You've never won, Tobias."

"I've never been wrong about an object," he said. "Only about the reply."

On the wall behind him, someone had chalked a number in a box. *40*. Tomorrow it would say thirty-nine.

---

That is the series in one afternoon. An object no one can explain, a man who reads it by wear and weight, and forty days to answer it with something made by hand on a cold island.

**The island.** Thrale is black gravel, seals and weather, with a seam of red clay under the station. Every four hundred days a capsule lands with one object inside. Earth sends a reply, and the senders answer with a new object or with an empty capsule. A farmer's seed was answered with a flower extinct since 1912. A flag got three empty capsules, and half the treaty nations left. One more empty, and the station closes. There is no signal, no ship and no translated speech. There is only the record of what went up and what came down.

**The lead.** Tobias is fifty-eight and spent thirty years appraising seized antiquities by wear and weight. He trusts a thing's history, not its making, so anything new reads to him as fake. The flag was his choice. He once told his daughter, a shoemaker, that she sells copies, and her letters sit unanswered in a drawer. He talks like a condition report. His marriage is "stable, with old repairs."

**The company.** Saoirse Lim, thirty-one, is a textile conservator who unpicks her own stitching every night and keeps asking the question Tobias won't: what can we make? Renata Falk, forty-four, a treaty indemnity officer, was demoted for insuring the flag, and she guards the next reply with a long memory. Hana Kovač, fifty-two, is the cook. She throws pots from the island's clay and runs the betting pool that Tobias has never won.

**What a book feels like.** Each book is one landing and forty days. Tobias and Saoirse test every candidate reply against one rule: the senders answer what has been used, never what claims. Every new object breaks part of that rule. The reader gets the object and every clue Tobias has, so a reader can beat him to it and bet with the crew. The tension is quiet and exact: a weight that is four grams off, a notch too many, a seam in the wrong place. Each ending yields one fact about the senders, and changes who may sign or block the next reply.

**Where the four books go.** In *The Thumbprint Cup* the clay cup carries a thumbprint nine thousand years old. Tobias catches a swapped replica by four grams. Hana's mended bowl goes up and is answered, so repair counts, and Renata gains a veto. *The Broken Tally* brings half a tally stick with one notch more than the station's logged landings. Tobias finds the unrecorded landing and carves the matching half, which proves the senders keep count, and Renata lifts her veto. *The Unworn Shoe* is a child's shoe, never worn, in leather from no animal. Tobias calls it a copy until Saoirse's stitch count proves the senders made it. She sews its mate and co-signs the reply. *The Weighted Box* is empty and too heavy by exactly the mass of Earth's last three replies. Renata rules it the closing empty. Tobias answers anyway, with Hana's new cup, which he cannot authenticate. The thread's answer: the human cup was an earlier human reply, and the senders answer what is made or grown now, not what is kept. Who they are stays open. Tobias writes to his daughter.

**What stays with the reader.** The series turns a man's flaw into its last discovery. The senders wanted the new thing all along, and so, it turns out, did his daughter. Readers will remember a fresh cup with Hana's thumbprint in it, going up unproven, and an old appraiser finally sending something he cannot vouch for.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** first contact through objects alone is unmistakable. One island, four people and one object per book keep continuity light, and production fit ranked 7 of 20.
- **Main risk:** reader pull ranked 17. The senders never explain themselves, which may frustrate readers who want payoff, and the tension has to hold across 60,000 quiet words. The puzzles live on hard numbers (four grams, the notch total, the mass of every reply), so the bible needs a locked landing table, or the drafts will drift.
- **Shelf risk:** the object titles do not say science fiction, and an isolated polar-station frame is familiar. The subtitle and cover question is open.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether the station stays open after Renata's ruling; whether book 4 needs a final received object; who missed or hid the unrecorded landing in book 2; whether the daughter ever appears on the page; what it implies that the senders have been collecting for nine thousand years (`logs/s10.md`, the author questions). The essay assumes the letter in book 4 is sent, not answered.
- **To verify:** fingerprints in ancient fired pottery and how they are dated; tally-stick practice; extinct flowers and the 1912 date; sub-Antarctic island ecology; plausible weights for a hand-built cup.
- **Invented for this essay:** the seals on the capsule, the cup's chips, staining and scar, the 206-gram weight, the thumbprint in Hana's new cup, Hana's odds, the chalked day count and the anemometer. They are texture, not canon.
