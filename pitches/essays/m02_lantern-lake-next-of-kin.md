# The Lantern Lake Next-of-Kin Mysteries

*A story essay for pitch [m02](../m02_cozy-paranormal-mystery_women-45-75_the-lantern-lake-next-of-kin-mysteries.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

The hall lamp in the Vandermark house had a pleated shade gone the color of weak tea, and Edie Farrow asked for it to be switched on at two in the afternoon. Nobody asked why. People rarely did, in a house with a death in it.

Stan Dombrowski poured her coffee from his thermos into its own lid and handed it across. He had stood at the foot of these stairs on Tuesday with his coroner's bag, and he did not look at them now.

"Accidental," he said. "That's what I wrote."

"Then that's what it is," said Edie, "until somebody hands me paper."

Otto Vandermark sat on the hall bench with a cane between his knees and his guardian standing behind him. The woman who said she was Otto's half-sister, and Alma's, stood by the hat rack in a good wool coat. She had driven in from out of state with a photocopied birth certificate and a way of saying "Daddy" that made Otto blink.

Edie set the box of Alma's papers on the hall table and lifted the lid. Shoe boxes, mostly, and a parish register on loan from the church at the lake end. Its spine smelled like a cellar. "Ninety years quiet," she told it. "Take your time."

Then she looked at the wallpaper.

The lamp threw three shadows up the stair wall. Otto's was bent over the cane. The woman's was tall in her coat. Edie's own stood straight as a ruler, the way it stood everywhere, in every room, except at her daughter's kitchen table. A brother and a half-sister should lean like two trees off one stump. Edie had once watched half-brothers at a graveside lean until their shadows touched heads, and neither of them had known.

Otto's shadow leaned toward nobody. Neither did the woman's.

Edie wrote in her notebook: *Relationship: unproven.* She did not write why. She never did. The sight gave her one fact, kin or not, and nothing else. Not who this woman was, not who had sent her, not what Alma had found out before the stairs. That would come from paper, or it would not come at all.

"I'll need your mother's maiden name," she said pleasantly to the woman in the coat. "And your father's church."

Then she finished Stan's coffee and drove to the courthouse, where the brass lamps were already lit.

---

That is the whole method. The gift tells Edie that something is wrong. It cannot tell her what is right, and it can never go in a report. Everything after the hall lamp is paperwork, and the paperwork is the detecting.

**The county.** Harrow County, New York, is a lake, hillside vineyards and a courthouse whose brass lamps are lit for every hearing. Six families still argue over who sold the canal lock land. When someone dies without a will, the court must find blood kin or the state inherits. So every estate is a quarrel about who belongs.

**The woman.** Edie is fifty-seven and the court-appointed genealogist, paid from the estate: no heir, no fee. She was a foundling, left on the courthouse steps in 1969. Her shadow has leaned toward exactly one person, her daughter. She has never searched for her own people, in case nobody was looking for her. She is dry and exact, she talks to file boxes, and when she finds family news she tells its owner privately first. They decide what the court hears.

**The company.** Tess, twenty-nine, is a genetic counselor who thinks her mother is merely observant, until book 3. Lois Pavlik, the court clerk, reads Edie's mail upside down, and on Thursdays the two of them split one slice of pie and argue over the bigger half. Ambrose Teague, the county historian, swept the courthouse floors at seventeen, which puts him in the building in 1969. Stan is a dairy farmer and the elected coroner, and he brings Edie coffee at death scenes.

**What a book feels like.** One unclaimed estate and one death that looks accidental, with several people who gain money, land or silence by it. The rules are tight. Shadows lean only between parent and child or between siblings, half or full. The sight needs light and both people alive in the room, and it says kin or not, never how. Consented DNA only confirms what the records prove. You see each shadow when Edie does. Every case closes, and nobody is harmed on the page.

**Where the four books go.** In *Kin by Porch Light*, Alma Vandermark, eighty-one, falls downstairs, and her supposed half-sister casts no lean toward Alma's brother Otto because she is an actress hired by Otto's thieving guardian. In *Heirs at High Noon*, swimmer Carl Hoffner drowns, and Stan's shadow leans toward Carl's sister, which makes Stan a half-brother, an heir and a suspect, until Edie proves a neighbor forged a deed and drowned Carl. In *Deeds Under Glass*, Hollis Ostrander dies before he can will his greenhouse to his gardener. His brother's shadow leans toward the young man, a half-brother owed half, and Edie proves which baptism page the brother forged. *Wills by Lamplight* takes away her light. Midwife Agnes Pruyn dies in a blackout and one birth ledger goes missing. Edie finds whose entry was worth killing for, and another entry leads her to a kitchen where her own shadow leans. Each book adds one fact about the newborn on the courthouse steps: a monogrammed blanket, a cousin match, Ambrose's name on the 1969 report. Book 4 answers who left her and gives Edie living kin. The pitch does not say whose kitchen, and neither does this essay.

**What stays with the reader.** A woman who can see everyone's family but her own, and who has made a career of being careful with other people's news. When her own news comes, the reader has watched her hand it to forty strangers gently, and wants someone to do the same for her.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** a gift that asks a question and real records work that answers it. The foundling thread pulls from book 1. It ranked fourth for reader pull.
- **Main risk:** accuracy. This readership is full of hobby genealogists, and New York intestacy and records procedure must be right (production fit ranked 10th). The cases are webs of half-siblings and forged pages, which AI drafts will tangle unless every book has a fixed family-tree chart. The foundling thread is also spent by book 4, so a fifth book needs a new engine.
- **Shelf risk:** genealogy mysteries already exist, and one test reader missed a pet and a named gathering place, which this shelf expects.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whose kitchen and who left the baby; whether Ambrose protected or falsified the 1969 record; why Edie never shared a lit room with her kin; whether Agnes's death is a killing or a fall in the dark; who killed Alma and Hollis, which the pitch does not state; whether to add a courthouse cat (`logs/m02.md`, the author questions).
- **Needs checking:** New York Surrogate's Court practice, court-appointed genealogists paid from estates, estates passing to the state, elected coroners in New York counties, and access rules for baptism and birth records.
- **Invented for this essay:** the tea-colored lamp, Stan's thermos, the claimant's coat and out-of-state drive, the borrowed parish register, the graveside half-brothers and the notebook line. They are texture, not canon.
