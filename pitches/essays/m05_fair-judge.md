# The Fair Judge Mysteries

*A story essay for pitch [m05](../m05_culinary-cozy-mystery_women-55-75_the-fair-judge-mysteries.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

The exhibit hall at the Tamarack County Fair smelled of floor wax, kettle corn and forty jars of strawberry jam warming in the morning sun. Dot Engelhardt worked down the table with a clean spoon for every jar and a glass of water she never drank, only rinsed with.

No. 38 was runny. No. 39 had been cooked too long and tasted like a penny. No. 40 was good, honestly good, the kind of jam a woman makes once a year in a hot kitchen and swears at. Dot wrote *Lovely. Skim the foam.* on its card.

Kayla stood at her elbow with the score sheets, in a fair T-shirt and the face of a nineteen-year-old who had been up since five. She had not picked up a spoon all week.

No. 41 was beautiful. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Dot held it to the window. Homemade strawberry sets a little cloudy, a little rough with fruit, the light coming through it red and uneven. This one was clear as a church window. She tasted it: sweet, firm, polite, nothing a kitchen had done to it. Then she turned the lid over and ran her thumb around the rim, and her thumb came away tacky.

"Price sticker," she said. "Somebody soaked it off and missed the glue."

"So it's store-bought," said Kayla. "So disqualify it."

"I will." Dot read the jar's little label, in careful ballpoint. *Strawberry. Put up Tues.* Tuesday night was when the ride inspector had died under the Ferris wheel. The whole fair had been told it was an accident by Wednesday noon. And somebody, it seemed, very much wanted to have spent Tuesday night at home over a hot stove.

She did not know whose jar it was. That was the point of judging by number.

"Write it down," she said. "Forty-one. Disqualified. Factory set, sticker glue on the lid."

"Gran. It's jam."

"It's a jar telling me it was never in a kitchen. I'd forgive the jam. I won't forgive the label." She set No. 41 at the back of the table under a tea towel. Nothing left the hall until Sunday. On Sunday it would all be eaten.

Across the hall, Chairman Gordon Rask was pinning blue ribbons on the quilts, one at a time, himself.

---

Dot can taste a lie, and the reader can see the same lie held up to the window and feel it on a thumb. The evidence also comes with a deadline. On Sunday it goes into somebody's mouth.

**The fairs.** One summer, four fairs: Tamarack County, Sorrel, Pike Lake, then the State Fair. Each is a town that lasts a week, with a dairy barn, a grandstand and a midway. The judges bunk in the 4-H dormitory, and the same bakers and the same ride crew follow the circuit from fair to fair. Chairman Gordon Rask pins every blue ribbon himself. Nothing leaves the exhibit hall until Sunday. Dot's village is a fairground on Monday and an empty field the Monday after.

**The judge.** Dot is sixty-seven and spent thirty years in the Norquist Mills test kitchen, which taught her to tell lard from shortening by the first bite of crust. She talks like a recipe card, short and in order. What she wants this summer is not a case. It is time with her granddaughter, who leaves for Seattle in September, and every hour spent on a death comes out of that.

**The company.** Kayla drives the Buick and has not baked since her grandmother called her crust tough. Harlan Voight, the livestock judge, brings coffee, his basset Oleo and the wrong suspect. Bev Aasen, the circuit secretary, saves everyone a plate. Lorraine Tvedt, who letters the ribbons, corrects the supper menu in ink. They all share one oven in the dormitory, which is where the evenings happen.

**What a book feels like.** Each book is one fair: the judging, a death called an accident, and one numbered entry that contradicts someone's story. Every taste clue has a twin the reader can see, as with jam that sets clear as factory jelly and a lid with sticker glue. It is a week at the fair without the heat: dill pickles held to the light, the percolator at five. Supper and two or three recipes close every book.

**Where the four books go.** In *Jam No. 41 Under the Ferris Wheel*, ride inspector Lyle dies under the Ferris wheel. Dot wrongly blames the operator, Rita Dahl, apologizes with pie, and keeps Lyle's notebook. In *The Lard in Pie No. 117*, a fair-board man dies in the milk house. Leaf lard in a pie clears the ride crew and names the dairy superintendent, who was watering the milk, and Harlan's chair at supper moves closer to Dot's. In *Sourdough No. 9 and the Empty Booth*, the fair announcer, Harlan's fishing partner, dies. Kayla spots the same oven's scorched corner on two winning loaves and exposes the baker he caught entering twice. In *Butter No. 302, Best in Show*, the State Fair treasurer dies beside the butter sculpture, Dot names Chairman Rask as the killer, and then she asks Kayla for a pie. The notebook answers the running question. Its blue-ribbon numbers are payment: officials win ribbons with entries Bev bakes, then sign Rask's ride inspections unread. Bev never knew what the ribbons bought. She tells the sheriff, and she keeps her seat at supper. The pitch does not say who killed Lyle, and this essay does not decide it.

**What stays with the reader.** The pie at the end. A grandmother has spent a summer refusing to forgive bad crust, and the last thing she does in book 4 is ask the granddaughter she hurt to bake for her. The fairs fold up behind them. September is still coming.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** the clue the reader can see as Dot tastes it, plus a built-in clock (Sunday for the evidence, September for Kayla). It ranked third for reader pull.
- **Main risk:** four accidental deaths on one circuit in one summer strains belief, and three of the four victims are fair officials. Every food clue and recipe must be kitchen-tested, and AI drafts will produce confident recipes and baking chemistry that nobody has tried.
- **Shelf risk:** culinary cozy is the most crowded shelf on the slate (distinctiveness ranked 18th). The numbered titles are distinctive but clumsy, and "Best in Show" alone is a common cozy title.
- **Still open, and left open here:** who killed Lyle in book 1; whether the book 2 and 3 culprits stay (the log calls them placeholders); how much guilt Bev carries; whether Lorraine falls under suspicion and Harlan's grief is shown in book 3; the dormitory or the old Winnebago; numbered titles or another pattern (`logs/m05.md`, the author questions).
- **Needs checking:** county fair judging practice (numbered entries, what happens to entries on the last day), whether homemade jam and factory jelly really differ by eye, leaf lard against shortening in a crust, ride inspection rules in the chosen state, judges' housing, and "Norquist Mills" against real flour brands.
- **Invented for this essay:** entries 38 to 40 and the score card, the window, the rinse glass, the "Put up Tues." label, Tuesday night as the time of death, the tea towel, and Rask pinning quilts during the jam judging. They are texture, not canon.
