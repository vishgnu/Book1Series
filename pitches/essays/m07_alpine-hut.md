# The Alpine Hut Mysteries

*A story essay for pitch [m07](../m07_traditional-alpine-mystery_women-men-45-70_the-alpine-hut-mysteries.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which the pitch does not settle.*

---

At dark, Ursi Feuz counted heads. She did it every night from the dining-room doorway, the hut book open on her forearm, touching each face with a finger she kept low by her hip so nobody felt counted. In 2019 she had let a voice on the radio do the counting for her. Five on the ledge, the voice said. There had been six.

Eleven names for the eighteenth of August. Ten faces at the long table, steam lifting off the rösti pan, rain going sideways past the window.

"Fourteen," she said.

Nora Gilgen looked up from the sink. "The notary. He went up early. He said it was the altitude."

"Did you see him go up?"

"I saw him say it."

The storm had shut the path at four and the helicopter at five. On the radio Wachtmeister Zumbrunn had said, from his warm office in the valley, that nobody could climb before tomorrow afternoon. Ursi went to the bunk room. The blanket on fourteen was still folded square.

Then she went to the boot room, because that was where guests told the truth. Guests lied about fitness and boots. They did not lie to the rack. His were in the third row, newspaper balled in the toes, dry as bread.

She found him with her headlamp, on the scree below the terrace. She did not climb down. She stood at the rail until she was sure, rain running into her collar, and then she went in and opened the log.

Nora read over her shoulder. "Flags wet. Rail at hip height. No witness. You're writing a fall."

"I'm writing everything that makes it a fall. First."

"That's backwards. That's the part that ends your year."

"I'd like it to be murder," Ursi said. "That's why the fall goes first, and in full."

Nora was quiet, which was rare. Then: "I racked those boots myself when he came in. Half past five."

Ursi wrote that down too, word for word, and who said it. Under it she wrote: at table, ten. In the book, eleven. She did not write the next word. She would be serving breakfast at seven.

---

That is the engine in one evening. The weather closes the door, the hut book says who is behind it, and the warden writes down the evidence against her own interest before she lets herself look at the rest.

**The hut.** The Schwarzhorn Hut sits at 2,740 meters in the Bernese Oberland, four hours' walk above the valley, with thirty-four bunks, one stove and one radio. The hut book never leaves the building, and every guest signs it, so the suspect list is a page of handwriting on the table where the soup is served. When weather shuts both the path and the helicopter, the police are a day away, and Ursi can hold no one. She can only log.

**The warden.** Ursi is fifty-two. She worked the rescue winch for twenty years, until her knee gave out. The Aarwald Mountain Club has given her one trial year: a death ruled accidental ends it, a murder does not. It makes her the most compromised detective in any room, and she knows it. Her wound is a number, the count of five she trusted on a ledge in 2019, when the sixth climber died overnight. Now she counts heads herself, at dark, and trusts boots more than people.

**The company.** Res von Allmen, the supply pilot, brings her the crossword. He flew in 2019 and has never said who radioed that count. Nora Gilgen, twenty-three, quit medical school and argues about everything but evidence. Wachtmeister Beat Zumbrunn needs her log, because without it the prosecutor rules accident. Below the hut, on the alp, Vreni Morf makes cheese at eighty-four. Her brother Emil went up in 1961 and never came down.

**What a book feels like.** One body, at most eleven guests, and a sealed hut. The clues are domestic: which boots are wet, who slept in which bunk, what the radio log says against what a guest says. The reader gets every one of them, and no gore. Around them runs the routine of a working hut, and the odd intimacy of the form: the detective cooks breakfast for the person she is about to name.

**Where the four books go.** Under every case lies one page. The hut book's 1961 entry records a private deal that gave the Morfs their alp, and each victim stands between someone and that page. *Death on the August Page* opens in an August storm, with a notary who came to read it lying below the terrace and his boots dry on the rack. *The Winter Room* is the October closing party: the club treasurer, who kept a copy of the deal, dies in the winter room, its flue capped a day early. *Level Four* is March, with avalanche danger at level four. The guide Lukas Morf, Vreni's great-nephew and heir, lies buried with his transceiver off, though Ursi's door check logged it sending. *The Last Signature* comes in June fog before the club votes on Ursi. An archivist sent to copy the 1961 page dies on the cellar stairs, and the volume vanishes. Beneath all four runs a signature. "E. Morf" has been written in the book every August since Emil vanished, in the same hand, shakier each decade. Book 4 names the signer, shows whose 2019 count failed, and settles whether Vreni keeps her alp and Ursi her hut. The pitch does not say who the signer is, and this essay does not guess.

**What stays with the reader.** The shaky "E. Morf" on each August page, and an old woman on the alp below, one fact nearer to her brother each book. And a question asked by a warm stove on a cold night: what does a woman write down when the truth and her job point different ways?

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** the inverted stake. Murder saves Ursi's job, so every log entry is a moral act, and the clues (boot rack, bed plan, radio log) are fair-play by nature.
- **Main risk:** familiarity and belief. Distinctiveness ranked last on the slate: the closed-circle hut is a known form, and four murders at one hut over one page strain belief. For AI drafting, each book needs a consistent eleven-guest bed plan and log, plus Swiss hut, police and avalanche detail a part-time editor cannot check alone.
- **Shelf risk:** a Swiss setting and Swiss names narrow US subscription appeal. Searches for the series name will be crowded by an existing long-running cozy line with "Alpine" in its titles, and *The Winter Room* shares a title with a well-known young-adult novel.
- **Still open, and left open here:** who signs "E. Morf" and whose 2019 count failed; who gains if the 1961 promise lapses at the June vote; whether to keep Schwarzhorn, a real summit name (`logs/m07.md`, the author questions).
- **To verify:** Swiss hut practice (hut book, boot room, winter room, transceiver checks), Bernese police rank and response, the avalanche scale, and above all whether an unregistered written promise in a hut book could bind grazing rights under Swiss law. The trial-year rule is the pitch's own invention.
- **Invented for this essay:** the date, bunk fourteen, the newspaper in the boots, Nora racking them at half past five, the scree below the terrace and Zumbrunn's estimate of when police can climb.
