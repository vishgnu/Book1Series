# The Signature Laws

*A story essay for pitch [s07](../s07_scifi-dystopian_women-men-25-55_the-signature-laws.md). It gives a feel for the series, not a decision about it: the scene and any detail not in the pitch are illustrative. The scene assumes close third person, past tense, which nobody has settled yet.*

---

The dryers at the Blue Drum ran hot in March, and Ines Calder did her best work beside number six, which Sam had rebuilt twice and which still knocked like someone wanting in.

Danuta Wojcik sat across the folding table with her hands flat on a paper bag. There was flour in the creases of her knuckles. She had brought rye rolls because she did not know what you brought to an advocate.

"Read me the letter," Ines said.

"I can read it. It's the numbers." Danuta pushed it across. "It says not passed. It says Brightwater Guardian countersigns my door from Monday. And the till. And the flour account. Their desk opens at nine. I light the ovens at four."

Ines read it twice. It was a good letter, in its way: short, polite and very sure of itself. It named a case officer, N. Paredes. It listed eleven fees. It did not give Danuta's score, and it did not give the mark she had needed.

"So you'll bake at nine," Ines said, "and pay Brightwater for the privilege, and the whole street will eat lunch rolls for breakfast. Very modern."

Danuta laughed, then looked as if she might cry, then did neither.

"Tell me the test. Not the result. The room. How long, what order, what you wrote down."

"The budget first. I ran out of time. Then where I've lived. Then the payments." Danuta turned the bag a quarter turn. "I swore I missed one. The gas, last winter. I did miss it."

"You told them the truth."

"Was that wrong?"

Ines thought of a table like this one, a pen, a form, and a house that a stranger later sold for her. "No," she said. "It was expensive."

She turned the letter over and drew two boxes on the back with a laundry pencil. In one she wrote *your score*. In the other, *their mark*.

"They've sealed both," she said. "So we need both. We have thirty days."

Under number six, Sam said, "Don't promise her." He said it every time. She had never once taken it as advice.

---

That is the series in one sitting: a person who told the truth on a form, a polite letter that takes her front door, and an advocate who will not accept a number she has not been shown.

**The place.** Port Stannard in 2084 is an ore port of cranes, boarding houses and red dust on every sill, in a Lakes Commonwealth that has turned adulthood into a license. The Assessment began as protection against elder fraud. Now every adult sits it every five years: a timed household budget, a count of addresses, a sworn list of missed payments, all checked against bank records. One in nine fails. Then your lock opens only on a Guardian's countersignature, and the Guardian bills your wages for it. Nobody marches. Nobody burns anything. The menace is a desk that opens at nine.

**The lead.** Ines is thirty-seven and works from the back of a laundromat. At nineteen she admitted one missed payment and spent five years a Ward, and her Guardian sold her mother's house. She has conceded nothing since. She is exact and dry, and funniest when she is furious, which is often. She has lost two appeals for her brother. A third loss would make his Wardship permanent, so she will not file again without proof.

**The company.** Sam, forty-one, is a crane mechanic and a Ward. He told her not to file again and still fixes her dryers, which is the whole of their love in one sentence. Niko Paredes, twenty-nine, a Brightwater case officer, signed his mother into Wardship and believes it saved her. Aila Kangas, fifty-eight, chairs the tribunal, has ruled against Ines eleven times and reads every page. Paul Rennick runs Brightwater and signs for his own father. Nobody here is a cartoon. Every one of them thinks the system is mostly working.

**What a book feels like.** Each book is one failed Assessment and a hearing thirty days out. Scores are sealed, so Ines has to learn why her client failed and who profits from it, one document at a time. Meanwhile Rennick leans on her in ways that never look like threats: the laundromat's account frozen for review, Sam's crane ticket pulled. The climax is a room, a chair, a stack of paper and a verdict. Each book ends with one thing won back and one document carried forward.

**Where the four books go.** *Seventy-One* saves Danuta's shop and shows that her 71 was failed against a mark of 74, not the published 70. *Countersigned* is the loss. Amos Deering, a seventy-three-year-old union archivist, loses his appeal, and Ines commits her one crime: she back-dates a deed to before his Wardship so that his archive and its fee ledgers reach the union. *Winter Address* wins back the bunk of Jan Hruska, a deckhand failed for moving boarding houses each winter, and with it his postcode's score sheets, Sam's among them. In *Her Own Hand*, Niko reports the back-dated deed. Then he finds his mother's postcode in Brightwater's fee tables and brings them to Ines. At her own renewal she concedes the forgery and puts the schedule on record. The answer to the thread is a pass-mark schedule set by postcode, passed in open session, never gazetted, and priced on Guardian fee income. The Assessment survives with one published mark. Fourteen hundred Wards sign again, and Sam signs his own lease. Ines is a Ward again, and Sam signs for her.

**What stays with the reader.** The series refuses the big ending. The system bends and does not break, the only law broken is broken by the heroine, and she pays the full price for it. What a reader keeps is the last exchange of signatures: the brother who could not sign for himself in book 1, signing for his sister in book 4.

## Before choosing this one

- **Strongest element:** one rule followed into rent, locks, tills and leases, fought out in hearings against people who believe in it. The invented law needs no outside research, and production fit ranked 9 of 20.
- **Main risk:** the slate ranked it 19 of 20. Dystopia readers expect rebellion and romance, and this ends in reform with the heroine a Ward. The payoff also rests on a schedule "passed in open session, never gazetted", and nobody has decided how a public vote stayed hidden. The numbers (71, 74, 70, eleven rulings, 1,400 Wards) need a locked ledger, or the AI drafts will drift.
- **Shelf risk:** book 4 reforms the Assessment and takes Ines's signature, which removes the engine. A fifth book would need a new premise.
- **Still open, and left open here:** whether Ines's second Wardship is a five-year term or open-ended; what Amos keeps by the end of book 2; how the schedule stayed hidden; the value of the single published mark (the final pitch gives none); whether Wards can vote; the target readership (`logs/s07.md`, the author questions).
- **To verify or handle with care:** dyscalculia and an elderly man failing a competence exam both need research and probably a sensitivity reader. The premise also echoes real guardianship abuses.
- **Invented for this essay:** dryer number six, the rye rolls, the countersign desk's hours, the eleven fees, the missed gas payment, the "Don't promise her" line, and the red dust on the sills. They are texture, not canon.
